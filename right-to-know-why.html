The Right to Know Why

People deserve understandable explanations of consequential decisions. The records behind those decisions should preserve the basis needed for meaningful later review.

This public-interest initiative introduces the Justification Review Standard (JRS) and Decision Reconstruction Risk (DRR) through free educational materials that individuals, advocates and community organizations can read and share.

Why a recorded explanation matters

A record can show what was decided without showing which sources informed the decision, when relevant events occurred or how the responsible person reached the conclusion. JRS refers to the resulting difficulty of independent later review as Decision Reconstruction Risk (DRR).

JRS is a human-applied documentation-review instrument. It helps identify gaps in retained records for appropriate verification and review. It cannot create missing evidence, establish that a decision was incorrect or determine whether unlawful conduct occurred.

Important boundary. The Right to Know Why is a public-interest principle, not a newly created legal right or a universal right to obtain confidential decision records. Disclosure and review obligations depend on applicable law and circumstances.

Professional background

JRS was independently developed by Phillip Wikes, former Lead Civil Rights Officer at the Maryland Commission on Civil Rights. His experience in investigative review, record assessment and civil rights administration informs the documentation concerns addressed by the instrument.

That professional history is not a government endorsement or agency adoption of JRS. The instrument remains in operational validation.
