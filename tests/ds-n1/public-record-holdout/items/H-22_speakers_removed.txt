At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a Lead Transportation Security Officer (LTSO), SV-1802, F Band, at the Agency’s Los Angeles International Airport (LAX) in Los Angeles, California. On November 23, 2016, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging that the Agency discriminated against her on the bases of race (African-American), sex (female), and color (Dark Brown) when, on February 25, 2016, Complainant was given a One Calendar Day Suspension. On January 5, 2016, Complainant was standing, with three other LTSOs near the briefing area, discussing lane assignments. While Complainant was stating her opinion about the assignments, one of the other officers, who is a Hispanic male coworker, loudly told Complainant to “be quiet.” Complainant then told the male coworker not to talk to her like that – that he could talk to his boyfriend, male friends that way, but not her. Report of Investigation, page 57. The coworker replied “Are you really going to attack my personal life?” One of the other LTSOs pulled Complainant into the breakroom to diffuse the situation. Afterward an individual averred that she and the coworker “had a discussion and they both apologized.” Later, the Transportation Security Manager (Supervisor 1) requested that Complainant meet with him, the coworker, and the Supervisory Transportation Security Officer (Supervisor 2). The Transportation Security Manager asked for a statement from Complainant and the coworker. Both provided a statement. an individual averred that she thought that was the end of the matter. On January 13, 2016, the Supervisory Transportation Security Officer (Supervisor 2) brought Complainant into his office and conducted a pre-decisional meeting with the Complainant. He offered the an individual an opportunity to respond, but he averred the Complainant did not respond. Thereafter, Supervisor 1 issued Complainant a one-day suspension. an individual said he suspended her because she confirmed she made the offensive statement and he believed she had a disciplinary history that included a Letter of Reprimand for a similar disrespectful comment toward an officer. He said the Table of Penalties recommended a one-day suspension for circumstances such as Complainant’s conduct and prior offense. an individual acknowledges that she had received prior counseling, but she maintains that she had not received a prior Letter of Reprimand. She said the coworker was issued a non- disciplinary letter of counseling, although Complainant believes that her comparator received a Letter of Counseling on four occasions preceding this Letter of Counseling. The Agency said that the coworker did not have any discipline for similar conduct in his files at the time of the January 5, 2016 incident. an individual averred that the race/sex and color discrimination against her is evident because she was given a suspension, but a similarly situated lighter-complexioned male co-worker, who made equally offensive comments and had prior discipline, received only counseling. At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided the Complainant with a copy of the report of investigation. The Complainant requested a hearing before an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). Over the Complainant's objections, the AJ assigned to the case granted the Agency’s September 25, 2017 motion for summary judgment. The AJ determined the record was complete and appropriate for the entry of summary judgment because the determinative facts were undisputed. The AJ reasoned that Complainant and the coworker were not similarly situated. The AJ stated that the supervisor suspended Complainant because Complainant told the coworker not to talk to her “like he talked to his boyfriend/boyfriends.” Based on the witness statements, Supervisor 1 understood that the comment referenced the co-worker’s sexual orientation and that the comment was “high in severity and offensive.” The AJ found that Complainant put forth “insufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact over the description of the incident.” Even if the Complainant meant to refer to the co-worker’s male friendships, the record shows that it “clearly registered with multiple people as a comment about [the co-worker’s] sexual orientation.” The Agency subsequently issued a final order adopting the AJ’s finding that the Complainant failed to prove that an individual subjected her to discrimination as alleged. This appeal followed.
