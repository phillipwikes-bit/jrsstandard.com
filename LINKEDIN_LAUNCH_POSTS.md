## Post 1: Friday 14 August. Accuracy is not the same as reconstructability

> A decision can be correct and its record still fail.
>
> Those are two different properties and we keep testing only the first one.
>
> Accuracy asks: was the conclusion right?
> Reconstructability asks: can someone who was not there work out why, from the file alone, months later?
>
> A record can pass the first and fail the second. It usually does not look broken when it does. It reads well. It is complete on its face. Every heading is filled in.
>
> Then it is challenged, and the reasoning that made the decision sound turns out to have happened in a room, in a conversation, in someone's head. Not in the file.
>
> The file is what answers. It is the only thing that answers.
>
> I have spent a year measuring this gap. It has a name now: Decision Reconstruction Risk.
>
> The uncomfortable part is that it is invisible until the moment it costs you, and by then the record is closed.

**Why it opens the week:** it names something people recognize and gives them no reason to feel sold to.

## Post 2: Monday 17 August. Fluent groundlessness

> The first of seven ways an AI-assisted record fails.
>
> **Fluent groundlessness.** The record reads well and reaches a confident conclusion, and no identifiable evidence supports it.
>
> Try this on something you wrote recently.
>
> Find the sentence that states the finding. Now find the evidence it rests on. Not a sentence that gestures at evidence. The actual thing a reader could go and check.
>
> If your finger stops moving, that is the mode.
>
> What makes it dangerous is that it is invisible to the usual quality checks. It is grammatical. It is well organized. It sounds decisive. Reviewers approve it because there is nothing to object to on the page. The problem is what is not on the page.
>
> AI-assisted drafting has made this more common, and the reason is mechanical rather than mysterious: a model produces confident prose whether or not the evidence is there, and confident prose is exactly what a reviewer reads as finished.

**Adapt:** if someone replies with an example from their field, ask if you can use it de-identified. That is how post 2 becomes post 7.

## Post 3: Tuesday 18 August. Basis substitution

> The second failure mode, and the one I see most often.
>
> **Basis substitution.** A summary stands in for the evidence, so the reader cannot verify the claim.
>
> "The complainant's account was consistent with the documentary record."
>
> That sentence is doing a lot of work. Consistent how? Which documents? A reader who wants to check has nowhere to go. They have to take the writer's word for it, which is precisely what a record exists to make unnecessary.
>
> This one is worse than fluent groundlessness in one respect: the evidence often does exist. Somebody read those documents and formed that view, and they were probably right. It just never made it into the file in a form anyone else can reach.
>
> The test is simple. **Wherever your record says what a document or a witness said, can a reader get to the document?** Not "is it in the case file somewhere." Can they get to it from what the record actually says?
>
> Summaries are not evidence. They are a claim about evidence.

## Post 4: Wednesday 19 August. What 36 reviewers in 16 countries found

> Some numbers, with the caveats attached, because numbers without caveats are how this field got its credibility problem.
>
> I built a 24-record set. Some records were AI-drafted with real documentation weaknesses; others were sound. The answer key was fixed and independently verified before anyone scored anything.
>
> Then I asked practitioners to read them. **36 reviewers completed the full set, across 16 countries and 5 continents.** Lawyers, HR directors, compliance leaders, auditors.
>
> On the detection question, **16 independent experts across 11 countries produced 384 graded reads and identified weak records 83.9% of the time (95% CI 72.7 to 95.1).** Those 16 are part of a larger group: **58 independent experts across three studies have graded records for this work, 36 of them completing a full 24-record set across 16 countries.** The detection figure belongs to the 16; the programme belongs to all 58, and a post about the programme should say 58.
>
> Agreement between reviewers was substantial: Gwet's AC1 of 0.739 among experts and 0.624 among trained reviewers.
>
> Now the caveats, which matter more than the figures.
>
> These are constructed records, not real case files. The confidence interval is wide because the expert panel is small. A randomized comparison against unaided review is deployed and **has not reached its enrollment target, so I cannot yet tell you the standard beats reading carefully without it.**
>
> What I can say: independent professionals in different jurisdictions, reading the same records against the same conditions, largely agree about which ones would not survive review. That is reliability, and it is a prerequisite for everything after it. It is not proof of effectiveness, and I would rather say so than let a number travel further than it should.

**Why this one earns the most:** the caveats are the credibility. Do not trim them to make the numbers look better.
