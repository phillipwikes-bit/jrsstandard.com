# Sharing results with reviewers: policy, timing, and ready-to-send drafts

Prepared 2026-08-02. Answers the question: when reviewers are asked to confirm their name and title, can they be told they will receive results, and what can they actually be sent once the study closes.

---

## 1. Short answer

**Yes, and you are already committed to it.** The Arm B candidate message promised that completers "are welcome to a written recommendation from me and a summary of the results." That is a promise on the record. It should be honored, and it can be mentioned now in the verification message with a timing caveat.

Two constraints govern how:

1. **Aggregate only.** Your own ethics terms state that reviewer responses are "used only in aggregate." That settles the individual-score question below.
2. **After closure, not before.** Nothing goes out while the comparison arm is still recruiting.

---

## 2. What can be shared, in three tiers

| Tier | Content | Send it? |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Aggregate study results** | Overall detection rate, cross-vendor reproducibility, reliability, what the study did and did not establish | **Yes.** Promised, and safe. |
| **2. The reviewer's own individual score** | "You personally scored X of 24" | **No, not by default.** See Section 3. |
| **3. Any other reviewer's score or answers** | Comparative rankings, named performance | **Never.** Violates the aggregate-only term. |

---

## 3. Why individual scores should not go out by default

This is the part worth deciding deliberately rather than by reflex.

**The consent terms already answer it.** Reviewers agreed to responses being "used only in aggregate." Sending someone their personal accuracy is outside what they agreed to, even though it is their own data.

**The practical reason is sharper.** Individual scores in this study range from 100 percent down to below chance, and that is true in the named expert panel as well as the comparison arms. Several accomplished, senior professionals who gave you their time scored poorly against the key. Sending an unsolicited "you scored 37 percent" to a named contributor, some of whom are co-authors or references on other work, would be a serious and avoidable injury to the relationship, and it would discourage exactly the people you want in the replication round.

**The bind that follows.** A reviewer who is told "the panel averaged 83 percent" may reasonably ask where they personally landed. Recommended handling: release scores **only on individual request**, delivered privately, framed against the key rather than against other reviewers, and never as a ranking. If someone asks, they have chosen to know, which is different from being told.

A one-line pre-emption in the results email removes most of the awkwardness: *"Individual results are held confidentially and are not included here. If you would like your own, ask me and I will send it to you privately."*

---

## 4. Timing: a single release date after full closure

**Do not send results to any group while the comparison arm is still recruiting.**

The comparison arm is blind. Its whole validity rests on participants not knowing the method before they judge the records. Reviewers talk to each other, post on LinkedIn, and forward emails. A results summary sent to completed participants in July reaches a prospective participant in August, and that participant's data is then compromised.

This applies to the named panel too, not only the blind arm. The two groups overlap professionally.

**Rule: one release date, after (a) comparison-arm recruitment is closed, and (b) the data are locked.** Not staggered, not "just the panel first."

What you can say in the meantime, and should: results are coming, here is roughly when.

## 6. Draft: results summary for the named expert panel (send at closure)

*JRS-branded. These reviewers know the standard by name.*

---

Subject: The results of the study you took part in

Dear [Name],

I promised you a summary of what the study found. Data collection is now closed and the analysis is locked, so here it is.

**What you took part in.** An international panel of experienced professionals independently reviewed the same set of 24 constructed records, judging whether each one contained enough for a later, independent reviewer to reconstruct the basis for its conclusion. Your reads were scored against an answer key that was fixed and independently verified before any scoring took place.

**What the study found.**

Reviewers on the panel identified the records correctly [XX] percent of the time, which clears the threshold set in advance, before any data were examined. That is the study's central result and it is a real one: experienced professionals, working independently across [XX] countries, can identify records whose reasoning cannot be reconstructed.

Separately, three AI systems from three different vendors applied the same review to the same records and agreed [XX] percent of the time. That measures consistency of application rather than correctness, and I am careful to keep those two ideas apart.

**What the study did not establish.** It did not show that the standard improves on unaided professional judgment. A separate comparison was built to test exactly that, and at the sample size reached, the result points in the standard's favour but is not statistically conclusive. It is reported as an open question rather than a win. I would rather tell you that plainly than dress it up.

**Your part in it.** You are credited by name as a contributor, in the wording you approved. Results are reported in aggregate only; individual results are held confidentially and are not included here. If you would like to know your own, ask me and I will send it to you privately.

[If applicable: The paper is under review at [venue] and I will send you the citation when it appears.]

Thank you. The panel's independence is the reason any of this is worth reporting, and that came from you and the others giving real attention to a task with nothing in it for you.

With appreciation,

Phillip Wikes
Creator, Justification Review Standard (JRS)

## 7. Draft: results summary and debrief for the comparison arm (send at closure)

*This group was blind. Debriefing them after closure is an obligation, not a courtesy. This message is the first time they learn what the study actually was, so it must explain the design honestly and without making them feel used.*

---

Subject: What the Records Review Study was actually testing

Dear [Name],

You completed all 24 records for the Records Review Study, and I promised you a summary of the results. Data collection is now closed, so I can tell you the full picture, including a part of the design I deliberately did not share at the time.

**What I did not tell you, and why.** Participants were randomly assigned, by a hash of the code you were given, into one of two groups. One group reviewed the records using a structured five-condition standard called the Justification Review Standard. The other answered a single general question about whether each record was adequately supported. Nobody was told which group they were in, and nobody was told the study was a comparison.

That concealment was the point. The study asks whether the structured standard helps a reviewer detect records whose reasoning cannot be reconstructed. If participants had known, the comparison would have been worthless. Withholding it was necessary to the design, and now that collection is closed there is no reason to withhold it any further. If you would like to know which of the two versions you received, tell me and I will confirm it.

**What the study found.**

The group using the structured standard scored higher than the group without it, by [XX] points. The direction favours the standard and has done so consistently. However, at the number of participants reached, that difference is **not statistically conclusive**: the sample is small enough that a gap this size could still arise by chance. Under the analysis plan written before any data were collected, this is reported as an open question, not as a result in the standard's favour.

A separate panel of reviewers, working with the standard, identified the records correctly [XX] percent of the time, clearing a threshold set in advance. Three AI systems from three different vendors applied the same review and agreed [XX] percent of the time, which measures consistency rather than correctness.

**What happens with your data.** Responses are used in aggregate only. You are credited by name as a contributor if you consented to that, in the wording you approved, and you may withdraw your name at any point before publication. Individual results are held confidentially and are not included here. If you would like your own, ask and I will send it privately.

**One thing I want to say directly.** These records were built to be difficult, and several were designed so that a confident, professional-sounding document contains no traceable basis at all. Performance varied widely across every group, including among very senior people. That variation is the finding, not a reflection on any individual. It is precisely why the question is worth studying.

Thank you for giving real attention to a task where you were told less than you might have liked. The study does not work without people willing to do that.

With appreciation,

Phillip Wikes

## 8. Checklist before either message goes out

- [ ] Comparison-arm recruitment formally closed, no outstanding invitations live.
- [ ] Data locked; final figures computed and inserted in place of every [XX].
- [ ] Numbers verified against the database on the day of sending, not carried from an earlier draft.
- [ ] Named-credit wording confirmed with each recipient (the verification message).
- [ ] Anyone who elected anonymity receives the aggregate summary with no named-credit paragraph.
- [ ] No individual scores in any message body.
- [ ] Withdrawn participants (V-AI-17) excluded entirely from the send list.
- [ ] The comparison result is stated as inconclusive in both versions, with no softening.

---

## 9. The one rule underneath all of this

The summary these reviewers receive should say the same thing the paper says. If the paper reports the comparison as a null and the reviewer email calls it promising, that gap is exactly the kind of documentation problem this whole programme exists to identify. The reviewers are the people most entitled to the unvarnished version.
