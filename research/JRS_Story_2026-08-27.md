# The JRS Story

## How a Civil Rights Review Problem Became a Business

JRS did not begin with artificial intelligence.

It began with my work reviewing civil-rights cases.

As a Lead Civil Rights Officer with the Maryland Commission on Civil Rights, I spent years examining decisions after they had already been made. When a decision was challenged, the person reviewing the case often was not present when the events occurred. They had not conducted the investigation and could not rely on the decision-maker's memory.

They had the record.

Over time, I encountered a recurring problem. A decision could have a legitimate and defensible basis, but the record did not always preserve enough information to explain how that decision had been reached.

There might be emails, interview notes, reports, messages, observations, and a final conclusion. The problem was that the connection between those pieces was sometimes incomplete.

The record could show the outcome without adequately showing the path that led to it.

That experience led me to identify and name Decision Reconstruction Risk, or DRR.

In simple terms, DRR is the risk that a consequential decision cannot later be independently reconstructed from the record itself.

That became the starting point for JRS.

## Why the civil-rights connection matters

My civil-rights experience also showed me why incomplete records can have consequences beyond ordinary recordkeeping.

A weak record does not necessarily mean that someone acted improperly. It can, however, make improper conduct harder to identify.

When the evidence and reasoning behind a decision are not adequately preserved, it becomes harder to determine whether subjective judgments, inconsistent treatment, assumptions, incomplete information, or bias affected the outcome.

That applies to human decision-making.

It also applies when AI becomes part of the process.

AI does not have to be deliberately discriminatory to reproduce weaknesses already present in the information, practices, or decisions from which it draws.

This is where DRR intersects with civil rights, and the connection is about reviewability rather than blame. **If the record cannot show how a consequential decision was reached, then no reviewer can properly examine it at all**, whether they are looking for error, inconsistency, bias, or simply an explanation the organisation can stand behind.

JRS does not determine whether bias occurred. It examines whether the record preserves enough for someone else to make that determination.

## AI made the problem harder to see

Generative AI can take scattered information and turn it into a polished, professional-looking document.

That is useful.

It can also make an old documentation problem harder to recognise.

A record can look complete without preserving the evidence behind it. The language may be clear and the conclusion may sound reasonable, while the underlying decision trail remains difficult to reconstruct.

**This is the failure mode that matters most, and it is the one AI has made common.** A record with an obvious gap looks thin to anyone who opens it, and someone catches it. A record that reads well and cannot be reconstructed passes file-by-file review and fails later, under examination, when it is too late to fix.

That is the specific problem JRS was built to catch: not the record that looks wrong, but the record that looks right and cannot answer the question later.

## JRS is the practical method

I did not want another general statement telling organisations to be transparent, accountable, or responsible.

I wanted a practical review that someone could apply to an actual record.

That became the Justification Review Standard, or JRS.

JRS examines five conditions:

1. **Basis identification.** Whether the basis for each conclusion can be identified within the record.
2. **Decision-process traceability.** Whether a later reviewer can trace the decision process from evidence to conclusion.
3. **Reconstructability.** Whether a reviewer with no prior knowledge can reconstruct the basis from the record alone.
4. **Evidentiary sufficiency.** Whether the evidence in the record is sufficient to support the conclusion.
5. **Chronology.** Whether the sequence of events can be followed, with dates and intervals.

These five conditions create the basic structure for examining a decision record.

JRS does not decide whether a decision was legally correct.

It does not determine whether someone discriminated.

It does not replace professional judgment.

It helps make the decision record more reviewable.

## The easiest way to understand JRS

I often compare JRS to a spell checker.

When you write a document, the spell checker does not write it for you. It does not decide what you believe or determine whether your argument is correct.

It identifies something that may need attention before you finalise the document.

JRS works on the same basic principle.

The difference is what it checks.

A spell checker checks the words.

JRS checks the decision record: the evidence, the chronology, the reasoning, and the sufficiency.

The person responsible for the decision remains responsible for the decision.

JRS provides another opportunity to identify a weakness before the record becomes final.

## The methodology had to be tested

I did not want JRS to remain a framework based solely on my professional experience.

The methodology therefore moved into structured testing.

An important part of that work was my collaboration with Ubayet Hossain, FRM.

Ubayet is an Associate Director for Model Validation at KPMG India. His professional background includes model validation, reliability, and quantitative methodology.

His involvement mattered because of a specific methodological challenge.

It is easy to say that a reviewer can spot a weak record.

It is much harder to establish that independent reviewers, working separately, will identify the same weaknesses consistently, and to distinguish a documented finding from a personal interpretation.

That is where Ubayet's model-validation background became important.

I developed the Decision Reconstruction Risk construct and the JRS review method.

Ubayet contributed to the validation methodology used to test the work. That work addressed how the proposed classifications could be established, how independent reviewers could be evaluated, and how agreement and reliability could be measured.

The distinction matters:

**I developed what was being tested. Ubayet helped establish how it could be rigorously tested.**

His contribution helped move the work beyond a professional judgment about what a reviewer might notice and toward a methodology that could be subjected to structured evaluation.

## Different perspectives were deliberate

I did not want the research evaluated only by people who approached records the way I did.

Investigators, auditors, lawyers, compliance professionals, technology professionals, researchers, and other practitioners can see different weaknesses in the same record.

A reviewer may read the same passage differently depending on professional experience.

One reviewer may see ordinary administrative language.

Another may recognise a gap that warrants further examination.

A third may identify context that changes the reading entirely.

For that reason, the research incorporated independent experts from different professional disciplines, countries, cultures, and professional backgrounds.

The purpose was not to create agreement.

It was to reduce the chance that one professional perspective would determine what everyone else could see.

That became an important principle behind the research:

**Different minds. Fewer blind spots. Stronger evidence.**

## The research and pilot programmes

The research has included structured testing and two real-world pilot programmes.

The first examined public records in New York.

The second examined HR records in the United Arab Emirates.

The New York pilot provided an opportunity to apply JRS to actual public-records determinations rather than relying only on constructed examples.

The UAE pilot moved the methodology into an employment environment and a different jurisdiction.

These pilots were important for another reason. They provided different types of records in which to examine whether the methodology could identify genuine weaknesses in reconstruction, rather than weaknesses that only appear in examples built to contain them.

The objective was not to conclude that the methodology solves documentation.

It was to examine whether independent reviewers could identify relevant evidence in a record and reach sufficiently consistent classifications.

The pilots remain part of a developing validation programme.

They provide evidence about how the methodology performs in practice. They are not being presented as proof that JRS solves every documentation problem.

## Hekim expanded the perspective

The work later expanded through collaboration with Hekim Colpan, an AI governance and compliance professional based in Germany.

Hekim's background includes AI governance, data protection, ISO/IEC 42001, information security and compliance, the EU AI Act, GDPR accountability, and DORA.

That brought together two different professional perspectives:

U.S. civil-rights investigation and record review, and European AI governance and compliance.

The jurisdictions and professional environments differ, but the underlying record problem is recognisable in both.

An organisation still needs to be able to explain and reconstruct a consequential decision from the record it kept.

That helped reinforce the broader position of JRS.

DRR is not simply an AI problem.

It is a documentation problem with implications for governance, accountability, compliance, investigations, HR, legal review, audit, risk, and, in some circumstances, civil rights.

## The business has three connected lanes

The business is not simply a research project and it is not primarily a traditional consulting business.

It is being developed around three connected groups: practitioners, organisations, and platform vendors.

Each has a different role.

### Practitioners are the seeding layer

Practitioners are people who already review consequential records.

This includes investigators, HR and employee-relations professionals, civil-rights and human-rights professionals, compliance and ethics professionals, internal auditors, legal professionals, risk professionals, AI-governance professionals, records professionals, researchers, and educators.

The objective is to let these professionals learn JRS and use it within workflows they already have.

They do not have to purchase software to learn the methodology.

The objective is independent use.

### Organisations are the adoption layer

The second lane involves organisations.

An organisation may create consequential records in HR, compliance, investigations, legal, audit, risk, or AI governance.

The objective is to determine whether DRR exists in that environment and whether JRS can fit into an existing review or control process.

The organisation can apply the methodology to its own records and determine whether the problem is real, where it occurs, and whether JRS provides a useful control.

If a repeatable technology need becomes apparent, that creates a path toward the Review Engine.

### Platform vendors are the commercial lane

This is where the business becomes a technology business.

The primary commercial buyer is the platform vendor.

The target market includes GRC systems, investigation platforms, HR case-management systems, legal technology, IRM and risk platforms, and other systems where consequential records are created and finalised.

The commercial product is the JRS Review Engine.

JRS is the methodology.

The Review Engine is the technology intended to operationalise that methodology within the platforms professionals already use.

The intended commercial progression is:

**Evaluation → Integration → Licence.**

## The Review Engine is being prepared for licensing

The Review Engine is the technology layer of the business.

Its purpose is to take the JRS methodology out of a purely manual review environment and make it possible to incorporate the review into software.

The intended workflow is straightforward.

A professional creates a consequential record.

Before the record becomes final, the Review Engine evaluates it against the five JRS conditions and returns a per-condition finding with a routing determination.

The system identifies potential weaknesses in the decision record and surfaces areas that warrant human attention.

**The call is stateless.** The record text is evaluated in memory and discarded. It is not written to any table, not echoed back, and not logged. For a platform vendor, that removes the data-residency and retention obligations that would otherwise come with embedding a third-party review into the moment their customers' most sensitive records are written.

The Review Engine is intended to surface findings for human review, not to replace human judgment about what those findings mean.

The technology has been developed to the point where controlled platform evaluation and integration discussions are appropriate.

It should not, however, be described as a fully validated AI system.

The underlying technology remains subject to operational validation and further testing. Reproducibility is disclosed rather than hidden, and reproducibility is not accuracy and not validation. No effectiveness claim is made, and every API response repeats that disclosure in its own payload.

That does not prevent commercial development.

A qualified platform vendor can evaluate the Review Engine's functionality, integration requirements, workflow fit, security requirements, and potential customer value while the validation programme continues.

The intended commercial path is therefore:

1. Evaluate the Review Engine.
2. Determine whether it fits the platform.
3. Test its behaviour in the relevant workflow.
4. Conduct integration work where appropriate.
5. Move toward licensing when the evaluation and integration requirements are satisfied.

That is the current commercial stage of the technology.

## Why the spell-check analogy also explains the product

The spell-check analogy describes the intended user experience.

You do not create a document somewhere else simply to check its spelling.

The check operates inside the writing environment.

JRS is intended to work in the same general way.

A professional creates a consequential record.

The Review Engine checks the record before finalisation.

It identifies potential weaknesses in the evidence, chronology, reasoning, or sufficiency.

The professional decides what those findings mean and what action should be taken.

The technology provides the check.

The human remains responsible for the decision.

## The two public initiatives

The business also has two sister initiatives.

They address the same underlying issue from different perspectives.

### The Right to Know Why Initiative

This begins with the individual.

When a consequential decision affects someone's livelihood, rights, opportunities, safety, or ability to seek justice, the person should be able to understand why the decision was made.

The initiative is not presented as creating a new legal right.

It gives a name to an expectation associated with due process, procedural fairness, and accountable decision-making.

If a decision affects me, I should be able to understand why.

### The Decisions You Can Defend Initiative

This begins with the organisation.

If a consequential decision is challenged later, the organisation should be able to explain and defend how it reached that decision from the record.

The two initiatives approach the same issue from different sides.

The individual wants to know why.

The organisation needs to be able to explain why.

JRS provides the practical method for examining whether the record supports that explanation.
