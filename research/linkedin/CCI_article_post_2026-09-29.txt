VERSION 1. The one I would post. Opens on the concrete failure.
================================================================================

An employment file can contain emails, attendance records, completed work and a
year of prior feedback, and still fail the only test that matters later.

The final memo reads well. It just does not connect its conclusions to any of
that material.

Hekim Colpan and I wrote about this for Corporate Compliance Insights. AI is now
drafting performance reviews, warnings and termination memos, and it is good at
producing documents that sound substantiated. Under McDonnell Douglas an
employer has to articulate a legitimate reason and show it was actually applied.
Fluency does not do that. Evidence does.

The part that surprised me most is what happens across files rather than inside
one. AI reuses subjective phrasing. "Cultural fit." "Executive presence."
"Struggles with change." Any one of those reads as ordinary management language.
The same three across a dozen files, clustering among employees who share a
protected characteristic, reads differently to a reviewer with the whole set in
front of them.

Nobody wrote that pattern deliberately. The tool did, because that is what tools
do.

We set out six checks to run before a record is finalised, including one I would
apply even without AI in the picture: read the file as though the person who
wrote it has left, and ask whether you could still explain the decision from
what is on the page.

VERSION 2. Shorter. Leads on the pattern finding.
================================================================================

"Cultural fit" in one employment file is ordinary management language.

"Cultural fit" in nine files, clustering among employees who share a protected
characteristic, is something a reviewer will ask about.

Nobody wrote that pattern on purpose. AI drafting tools reuse subjective
phrasing, and the pattern only becomes visible when someone reads the files side
by side, which is exactly what happens in a dispute and rarely happens before
one.

Hekim Colpan and I wrote this up for Corporate Compliance Insights, with six
checks to run before a record is final.

The underlying question is older than AI: if the person who made this decision
is gone, can the record still explain it?

VERSION 3. For a compliance and legal audience. Most direct.
================================================================================

New in Corporate Compliance Insights, with Hekim Colpan.

McDonnell Douglas asks an employer to articulate a legitimate reason and show it
was actually applied. AI-assisted drafting has changed how employment records
get written. It has not changed that test.

The exposure is not that AI writes badly. It is that AI writes well, and a
polished narrative can sit on top of an evidentiary record it never actually
cites. Worse, it reuses phrasing, so subjective language repeats across files in
patterns no individual author intended.

Six controls to apply before a record is finalised, including identifying the
human author and the tool, linking conclusions to verifiable evidence, and
reviewing subjective language across employees rather than one file at a time.
