This article would not exist without Hekim Colpan.

He volunteered for a piece about something most employers will not notice until they are sitting in a deposition.

Here is the problem.

An employment file can hold emails, attendance records, completed work and a year of prior feedback, and the final memo can still fail. It reads well. It just never connects its conclusions to any of that material.

Under McDonnell Douglas you have to articulate a legitimate reason and show it was the one you actually applied. Fluency does not do that.

Then it gets worse.

AI reuses subjective phrasing. "Cultural fit." "Executive presence." Any one of those reads as ordinary management language.

The same phrases across a dozen files, clustering among employees who share a protected characteristic, read very differently to someone holding the whole set.

Nobody wrote that pattern deliberately. The tool did.

Open one of your own termination memos from the last year. Can you point to the evidence behind each conclusion, or only to the sentence asserting it?

We set out seven controls to apply before a record is final, and we mean them as a minimum. The one I would apply even without AI in the picture: read the file as though the person who wrote it has left, and ask whether you could still explain the decision from what is on the page.

The European section is Hekim's, and it is the part I could not have written. An ISO/IEC 42001 auditor's read of what the AI Act actually reaches here, including the part most US coverage skips, that a great many employment workflows sit outside the high-risk regime entirely and are left to ordinary employment law.

His section is built on citations rather than assertions, which is the discipline the article argues records should have.

Thank you, Hekim. I hope this is the first of several.
