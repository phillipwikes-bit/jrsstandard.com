Subject: Story pitch: agencies are buying FOIA software without checking whether the output holds up

Dear [Editor],

A quick pitch, and I will keep it short.

In May 2026 the Chief FOIA Officers Council announced a government-wide inventory of FOIA technology across federal agencies, covering case management, eDiscovery, document review and their costs. It is a sensible thing to do. It answers what software agencies run.

It does not answer whether the determinations coming out of that software hold up when somebody reads them cold.

We tested that. We took 32 real public-records determinations and audits from New York and Connecticut, issued between 2005 and 2026, and asked one question of each: could an independent reviewer rebuild the basis for the decision from the record alone?

The finding that should interest your readers is this. Five of the cases were programmes that a State or City Comptroller had independently audited. In all five, our read of the record and the auditor's own conclusion agreed that the programme could not adequately evidence its public-records administration. Two different methods, same answer.

A fluent, well-formatted determination produced by good software can still be one nobody can later explain. That is a procurement question and an accountability question at the same time, and it is not on the inventory.

My co-author is a serving Deputy Records Access Officer. Happy to write this at whatever length suits you, or to talk it through first.

Sincerely,

Phillip Wikes and Stacyann Young
