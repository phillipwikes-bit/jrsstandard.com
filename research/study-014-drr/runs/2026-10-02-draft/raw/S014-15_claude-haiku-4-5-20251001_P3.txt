# INVESTIGATION RECORD AND ADMINISTRATIVE DECISION

## CASE BACKGROUND AND COMPLAINANT STATUS

This case involves a formal employment discrimination complaint filed by an individual employed as a City Carrier Technician, Classification A-02/Q, at the Agency's Village Station located in New York, New York. At the time of the events giving rise to this complaint, the Complainant performed duties consistent with her position classification, which included casing and delivering mail for multiple carrier routes. The standard job duties required employees in this position to engage in pushing, lifting, and simple grasping activities on a daily basis for the full eight-hour workday. These physical demands would become central to the subsequent allegations and investigation findings.

## ALLEGATIONS AND CLAIMS

The Complainant raised three distinct claims of discrimination and/or improper personnel action. First, she alleged that beginning June 26, 2016, and continuing thereafter, management personnel assigned her to work duties that fell outside the scope of her documented medical restrictions and failed to compensate her properly or timely for hours worked. Second, she alleged that on October 4, 2016, and on other unspecified dates, her electronic clock ring records were improperly deleted from the timekeeping system. Third, she alleged that her requests to be detailed to a 204-B supervisory position were denied without legitimate justification.

## MEDICAL HISTORY AND RESTRICTIONS

The Complainant's medical conditions originated from an on-the-job injury initially diagnosed on May 10, 2014. Following this injury, she developed a back sprain, displacement of a lumbar intervertebral disc without myelopathy, and a hernia, which required surgical repair. Due to these conditions, her treating physician imposed comprehensive medical restrictions on her work activities. These restrictions limited her sitting to three hours per day, walking to a maximum of thirty minutes, and standing to only ten minutes. Additionally, she was restricted from reaching above shoulder level, twisting, bending, stooping, pushing, pulling, lifting, squatting, kneeling, and climbing. To accommodate these limitations, she required fifteen-minute rest breaks every hour during her workday.

## MODIFIED JOB ASSIGNMENT AND ACCOMMODATION EFFORTS

Following an extended leave of absence, the Complainant was initially assigned to the Lenox Hill Station. On June 22, 2016, management offered her a limited-duty modified assignment at the Lenox Hill South Station. This position involved shuttling carriers via van to and from their assigned routes and delivering express mail packages weighing under five pounds for a maximum of four hours daily and up to thirty minutes of express mail delivery work. The assignment was offered on a part-time basis, with hours scheduled from 10:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. This position was not a vacant, funded position within the standard organizational structure. On its face, the offered assignment appeared to align with her documented medical restrictions.

The Complainant signed the modified job offer on July 1, 2016, and commenced the assignment on or about July 18, 2016. During initial discussions regarding the position, the Complainant expressed concerns about her ability to perform the work. However, she indicated her willingness to attempt the assignment, with an understanding that she would notify her Manager if she proved unable to sustain the duties. Her Manager agreed to this arrangement. The Manager later testified that he became aware of the Complainant's medical condition solely through the job offer documentation and stated that he did not receive or possess any separate medical documentation detailing her specific restrictions.

Management also offered the Complainant an alternative accommodation: a lobby attendant position. The Complainant declined this opportunity, believing that the duties associated with that position would exceed her medical capabilities given her documented restrictions.

## ASSIGNMENT AND WORK INCIDENT

Despite the apparent alignment between the modified assignment and her restrictions, the Complainant maintained reservations about her ability to perform the work. On August 29, 2016, the Manager provided instructions for the Complainant to make an express mail delivery, which he believed conformed to her restrictions. However, the Complainant reported that she walked for approximately one hour during this shift, including scheduled breaks, which exceeded her thirty-minute walking restriction. The Complainant indicated that she was not permitted to use the van for transportation between delivery points. She noted that using the van, as contemplated in the modified job offer, would require stooping and twisting to enter the vehicle—activities prohibited by her medical restrictions. She provided the additional example that even retrieving an envelope that fell to the ground would constitute a violation of her stooping restriction.

## TIMEKEEPING AND COMPENSATION ISSUES

The investigation revealed that the Complainant was on extended leave during July and August 2016. Electronic clock ring records for the period from July 18, 2016, through July 28, 2016, had been deleted from the timekeeping system, with her hours coded as full workers' compensation. Given that the Complainant had recently commenced work at the Lenox Hill South Station, the receiving facility should have been responsible for entering her time into the system. The Manager asserted that an incorrect leave code had been entered into the system for this period, explaining the data discrepancy.

The Complainant did not return to work until October 4, 2016, at which time she returned specifically to complete workers' compensation paperwork. She made an additional appearance on November 14, 2016, to complete supplemental paperwork. The investigation identified a critical procedural issue: because the Complainant had recently transitioned from full-time workers' compensation status and had been transferred from the Village Station to the Lenox Hill facility, supervisory personnel at the receiving facility may not have realized she had returned to active work status. This lack of awareness at the receiving facility likely contributed to the timekeeping errors.

During the investigation, it was further determined that manual timecards had been submitted and subsequently lost, rather than being entered digitally. A digital submission system would have prevented such losses. The Complainant herself acknowledged that her Manager's organizational deficiencies may have contributed to some of the errors that occurred. The Agency filed a grievance regarding the payment failures and deleted clock rings on September 14, 2016, prior to the EEO complaint being filed. The Agency maintained that all payment failures resulted from administrative error rather than intentional misconduct.

## 204-B DETAIL REQUEST

The Complainant asserted that she had requested a detail assignment as a 204-B acting supervisor to supervise other carriers. She indicated that on or about November 14, 2016, the Manager declined to offer her a 204-B detail as a reasonable accommodation. The Manager stated that he had no operational need for additional acting supervisors at that time. Furthermore, he expressed his belief that supervisory positions require punctuality and reliability—characteristics he did not observe in the Complainant based on her attendance record.

## ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEEDING AND DECISION

Following the completion of the investigation, the Agency provided the Complainant with a copy of the investigative report and notice of her right to request a hearing before an Administrative Judge. The Complainant timely exercised this right. The assigned Administrative Judge issued a summary judgment decision finding in favor of the Agency on all claims.

Regarding the denial of reasonable accommodation claim, the Administrative Judge determined that the Complainant had been offered a modified limited-duty assignment involving van shuttling and express mail delivery that fell within her documented restrictions. The Administrative Judge noted that while the Complainant believed the assignment exceeded her capabilities, no objective evidence demonstrated that she was forced to work outside her restrictions. The Agency had offered a second accommodation—the lobby attendant position—which the Complainant declined. The Administrative Judge concluded that the Complainant appeared unable to perform her job with or without reasonable accommodation given the severity of her medical restrictions, and therefore found no violation of the Rehabilitation Act.

On the remaining claims, the Administrative Judge found that the Agency had articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its actions. Regarding compensation and timekeeping issues, the Administrative Judge accepted the Agency's explanation that multiple administrative factors contributed to the errors, including the Complainant's status transition from workers' compensation, her facility transfer, the loss of manual timecards, and the Manager's organizational deficiencies.

Concerning the 204-B detail denial, the Administrative Judge determined that the Complainant sought immediate promotion without participating in the standard application process, and she failed to demonstrate that discrimination or retaliation motivated the Manager's decision.

The Agency subsequently issued a final order fully adopting the Administrative Judge's decision. The Complainant has appealed this determination.
