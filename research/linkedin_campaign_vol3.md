## 1. Behind-the-Scenes Case Study

I spent thirteen years reading records after the decision was already made. Here is what that work taught me, and what I built because of it.

From 2012 to 2025 I was a Lead Civil Rights Officer at the Maryland Commission on Civil Rights. My job was to open a file, months or years after the fact, and determine whether the decision inside it could be defended.

**The pattern I did not expect:** the decisions were usually sound. The records could not prove it.

Good investigators, real evidence, defensible conclusions, and a file that could not carry any of it. The reasoning had lived in someone's head, in a hallway conversation, in a phone call nobody logged. By the time I read it, that context was gone and the record was alone.

**The obstacle:** every attempt to fix this ran into the same wall. "Write better documentation" is not an instruction anyone can act on. It has no test, no threshold, no way to tell a finished record from an unfinished one.

**What I built instead:** a single question with five conditions under it. Can an independent reviewer reconstruct how this conclusion was reached from the record alone? That question is testable. It can be taught, applied, and disagreed about productively.

Three takeaways from thirteen years and the validation work since:

1. The quality of a decision and the defensibility of its record are different things, and organizations confuse them constantly.
2. A record that needs its author present to make sense is not finished, no matter how well it reads.
3. AI has made this worse in a specific way: fluency now hides thinness that used to be visible on the page.

Where the work stands now: three AI models from three different vendors applied the standard to the same records and agreed 84 percent of the time, a consistency signal that is distinct from accuracy. Independent reviewers across 10 countries on 5 continents have completed structured reviews, reaching substantial agreement under a pre-registered analysis, reported as interim while the sample completes.

## 2. "I Ran This Every Night for 30 Days" Case Study

I had three AI models from three different vendors read the same records every night for a month. I was testing something most people assume rather than check.

**The assumption everyone starts with:** that a documentation standard is inherently subjective. That two reviewers, or two systems, will simply see a record differently, and there is nothing to be done about it.

If that assumption is right, no documentation standard can ever be more than one person's taste, and none of this is worth building.

**What the run produced:**
- 84 percent mean agreement on the review outcome across three vendor models, on 15 constructed records.
- A range of 78 to 87 percent as the record set grew from 3 records to 15, meaning the number held while the sample expanded.
- Independent human reviewers, separately, reached substantial agreement under a pre-registered analysis. Interim, on a sample still accumulating.

**The hidden catch, and I want to be precise about it:** agreement is not correctness. Three systems converging tells you the standard is being applied the same way, not that the application is right. Those are two different studies, and conflating them is exactly the sort of overclaim this project exists to argue against.

What consistency buys you is the precondition. A standard nobody applies the same way twice cannot be evaluated for accuracy at all, because there is nothing stable to evaluate.

So: reproducible, yes. Accurate, not yet established, and I will not say otherwise until the data says it.

## 4. Anatomy of a Public Failure

Every large documentation failure looks like a sudden collapse. It never is. It is a slow accumulation of records that nobody could reconstruct, and one day somebody asks.

The pattern repeats across sectors, so here it is stripped of any single organization's name.

**The trigger.** A challenge arrives. An appeal, an audit, a discovery request, a journalist with a records request. Someone asks the organization to show why a decision was made.

**The surface story.** The narrative that follows is almost always about a person or a policy: someone made a bad call, a rule was inadequate, a team was under-resourced.

**The actual root cause.** The organization cannot produce the reasoning. Not because it was never sound, but because it was never written down in a form that survives the people who held it. The decision existed. The evidence of the decision did not.

**The three warning signs, visible long before the trigger:**
1. Approvals recorded with no named reviewer and no date.
2. Justifications that cite "standard process" instead of the specific facts considered.
3. Records that only make sense to someone who was in the room.

**The prevention.** Pull three consequential records at random from the last quarter. Hand them to someone uninvolved. Ask them to explain, from the record alone, why the decision was made. The number that fail is your exposure, measured today rather than discovered later.

## 5. Opposing Expert Panel

There are two coherent positions on AI-assisted documentation right now. Both are held by serious people. Both are partly right.

**Position A, the acceleration case.** AI drafting is the largest productivity gain administrative work has seen in decades. Investigators, HR teams, and records officers spend a punishing share of their time on prose rather than judgment. Handing the prose to a model returns that time to the actual work. The trade-off is worth it, and the alternative is losing the capacity entirely.

**Position B, the evidentiary case.** Fluent output creates a specific and novel risk: the record now looks finished whether or not the reasoning is present. The traditional signal of a thin file, that it read as thin, has been removed. Speed has been purchased with the loss of a warning system nobody knew they relied on.

**Where I actually land.** These are not opposed, because they are answers to different questions. A is about drafting. B is about evidence. The mistake is treating a drafting decision as though it settles an evidentiary one.

Keep the acceleration. Add a check at the point the record is finalized: could an independent reviewer reconstruct this decision from the record alone? That single question preserves the productivity gain while restoring the signal AI removed.

The organizations that get hurt over the next few years will not be the ones that used AI. They will be the ones that used it and never added the check.

Which position does your organization actually operate on, whatever the policy says?

One green box around a claim citing a dated ticket number (label: "this is what a basis looks like").

**Body:**

We marked up a record the way an auditor would. Three red circles, and the file stops being a defense.

This document passed internal review. It reads as professional. It would survive a manager's glance and probably yours.

**Flaw 1: the ungrounded characterization.** "Did not meet expectations" cites nothing. It is a conclusion wearing the costume of a finding. Under scrutiny, it is the author's opinion with no path back to evidence.

**Flaw 2: the missing chronology.** The conclusion has no date, and neither do the events it rests on. A pattern claim without dated instances is not a pattern claim. It is an impression.

**Flaw 3: the phantom review.** "Reviewed and approved" with no reviewer name, no date, and no record of what changed. In discovery, that reads as the absence of deliberation rather than evidence of it.

**What the one green box shows.** A single claim tied to a dated, retrievable artifact. That is the entire fix, applied to every material assertion in the file.

Your records are being read this way whether or not anyone has told you.

**Body:**

**Node A, the input.** Raw material. Notes, dates, correspondence, evidence, whatever actually happened. This part is usually fine. Organizations are good at generating facts.

**Node B, the filter.** Five conditions, applied before the record is finalized. Is the conclusion reconstructable, is the basis identifiable, does the chronology hold, is the reasoning traceable, is the evidence sufficient for the stakes. **The dotted line is the entire problem.** AI-assisted drafting can take raw notes and produce a finished-looking output while routing around the filter completely. The record arrives at Node C having never passed through Node B, and it looks identical to one that did.

That bypass is invisible in the finished document. It is only visible when someone tries to reconstruct the decision and cannot.

## 13. System Failure Retrospective

When institutional records fail, the public story is almost always about a person. The structural story is almost always about documentation, and it is far less satisfying to tell.

**The surface narrative.** A decision is challenged. Coverage focuses on whoever signed it. The framing is competence or intent: someone was careless, or someone was hiding something.

**The institutional root cause.** In case after case, the organization is not concealing the reasoning. It genuinely cannot produce it. The people who held the context left, the informal explanations were never written, and the file that remains states conclusions without their basis. There is nothing to disclose because there is nothing recorded.

**Why this is worse than it sounds.** An organization that hid its reasoning at least had reasoning. An organization that cannot reconstruct its own decisions is unable to distinguish, internally, between its good calls and its bad ones. It has lost the ability to audit itself, which is the capability every other control depends on.

**How modern teams reproduce this exact error.** By treating AI-assisted drafting as a documentation solution rather than a drafting one. The output arrives complete and confident. Nobody checks whether the reasoning came with it. Twelve months of that and the archive is full of records that describe decisions without evidencing them.

**The one non-negotiable rule.** No consequential record is final until someone who was not involved could reconstruct the decision from it. Not a style guide. A gate.

## 14. Policy vs Friction Diagnostic

Your 20-page documentation policy is not failing because people ignore it. It is failing at three specific friction points, and all three are fixable.

**Intended rule.** Every consequential record identifies its evidence, names its reviewer, and is finalized only when complete.

**Friction point 1: the policy has no test.** "Adequately documented" is not a standard anyone can apply at 4:45 on a Friday. Without a pass or fail question, every reviewer applies a private threshold, and the thresholds diverge immediately.

**Friction point 2: the check happens after finalization, if at all.** Quality review that occurs downstream of the record being locked cannot change the record. It can only discover problems, which feels like oversight and functions like archaeology.

**Friction point 3: the drafting layer is invisible to the policy.** The policy governs the document. The risk now lives upstream of the document, in what was entered into a tool and what came back. Nothing in a typical policy reaches that layer.

**The hidden risk.** The gap between policy and practice does not read as a policy problem when it surfaces. It reads as an absence of deliberation, which is a substantially worse allegation to answer.

**The structural patch, one change.** Move the check to the moment before finalization and give it a binary test: could an independent reviewer reconstruct this decision from the record alone? Yes finalizes. No returns it. Everything else in the policy stays.

Policy that survives contact with a Friday afternoon is the only kind that matters.

## 17. The Standard-Setter Listicle

Five capabilities quietly moving from "nice to have" to "expected" for records, HR, audit, and compliance professionals in 2026.

Not tools. Capabilities, which is what survives when the tools change.

**1. Reading a record for reconstructability, not readability.**
The skill of asking whether a stranger could rebuild the decision, separately from whether the prose is good. These come apart constantly and most training conflates them.

**2. Auditing the drafting layer.**
Knowing what tool touched a record, what went in, what came back, and whether any of that was preserved. Discovery requests now reach this layer routinely.

**3. Spotting proxy language in aggregate.**
Recognizing that a subjective descriptor is a stylistic quirk in one file and a pattern claim across forty. This is only visible if someone looks across records rather than at them.

**4. Writing a basis line.**
The specific craft of attaching a claim to a dated, retrievable artifact in one sentence, without inflating the record. Underrated, teachable, and rare.

**5. Applying a shared standard rather than a private threshold.**
The difference between "this seems fine to me" and a criterion your colleague would apply the same way. Everything auditable depends on this one.

Which of these is already in your team's job description, and which one is the gap?
