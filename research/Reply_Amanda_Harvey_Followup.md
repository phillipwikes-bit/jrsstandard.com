## The key finding: her AI question rests on a comparison that does not hold

She is reading 84 percent (machines) against 0.74 (humans) and inferring that the machines applied the standard more consistently. **Those are different metrics on different scales.**

| Rater group | Raw agreement | Chance-corrected (AC1) |
|---|---|---|
| Three cross-vendor AI models | **84%** | not computed |
| Human experts | **88%** | 0.74 |
| Human trained reviewers | **83%** | 0.63 |

On the comparable metric, **human experts agreed slightly more than the models did**, 88 percent against 84 percent. The apparent gap was an artifact of comparing a raw percentage to a chance-corrected coefficient, which is a stricter measure by construction.

Answering this precisely is worth more than any positive claim in the thread. It corrects a reasonable misreading, demonstrates the methodological care the initiative argues for, and the correction happens to favour the human reviewers rather than the tool.

**Caveat to state, so the corrected comparison is not itself overclaimed:** the two figures come from different record sets (10 versus 15), different rater counts, and were never designed as a head-to-head test. The honest position is that the evidence does not support her hypothesis, and does not firmly support its reverse either.

## On domain variation: decline, and explain why

Per-reviewer accuracy by professional domain exists in the data and **should not be reported.** Two reasons, both stated in the reply:

1. **Statistical:** at 15 reviewers, domain subgroups contain one to three people each. Any pattern would be noise, and a post-hoc subgroup analysis on cells that small is the kind of result that does not survive replication.
2. **Confidentiality, which is the harder constraint:** the panel is small enough and the domains distinctive enough that a breakdown would effectively identify individuals. Several reviewers occupy the only seat in their domain, so "the audit specialist scored X" names a person. Reviewers consented to aggregate reporting only.

Declining on both grounds is stronger than declining on either alone, and the confidentiality reason is unarguable.

## THE REPLY (1,179 characters, 1,193 with her name tag, fits the 1,250 cap)

Amanda, the AI question is the sharpest one I have had, and the answer is a useful correction: those two numbers are not the same metric.

The 84% for the models is raw pairwise agreement. The 0.74 for humans is chance-corrected (Gwet's AC1), a stricter scale. On the comparable metric, expert raw agreement was 88%, slightly above the models' 84%.

So on available evidence, no. If anything the reverse, though I would not push even that: different record sets, different rater counts, never designed as a head-to-head test.

On domain variation: I have not analysed it and I am not going to. At 15 reviewers the subgroups are one to three people each, so any pattern would be noise. There is also a confidentiality problem, since a panel this small means a domain breakdown would effectively identify individuals, and reviewers consented to aggregate reporting only.

On scaling: genuinely unknown. That is what the interval is for. 82.8% with a CI of 71.0 to 94.6 is my honest statement of precision at this n. Larger samples narrow it. They do not guarantee the point estimate holds.

Thank you for pushing on this. These are better questions than the framework usually gets.
