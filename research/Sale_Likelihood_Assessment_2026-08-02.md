# Likelihood of selling the JRS/DRR asset at a low price (2026-08-02)

Direct assessment. Grounded in `IP_Asset_Transfer_Map.md`, `Path_to_Sale_Action_Plan.md`, and the current state of the three unfixed gates. Probability figures are reasoned estimates, not measured facts.

---

## 1. The direct answer

**Selling at a low price is more likely than selling at a good price, but less likely than intuition suggests, and the reason is not demand. It is transaction cost.**

My estimate for finding any corporate buyer within roughly 12 months, publication completed but the current gates unfixed:

| Scenario | Estimated probability |
|---|---|
| As-is today, marks unregistered, zero organizations using it, no buyer process | **10 to 15%** |
| Same, but priced deliberately low (content-and-brand deal, low five figures) | **20 to 25%** |
| Marks filed, one organization actually using it, active buyer outreach | **35 to 50%** |

The middle row is the one you asked about. Cutting the price roughly doubles the odds, and still leaves the more likely outcome as no sale.

## 2. Why dropping the price helps less than expected

This is the non-obvious part, and it is the thing most first-time IP sellers get wrong.

**Below roughly 25 to 50 thousand dollars, corporate buyers frequently will not transact at all.** Not because the asset is not worth it, but because the fixed cost of buying anything is high. A buyer's counsel must run a diligence review, confirm chain of title, check that contributors assigned their rights, draft an assignment agreement, and clear it internally. That work costs real money and does not shrink because the asset is cheap. When diligence costs a meaningful fraction of the purchase price, the deal stops making sense to the buyer's finance team regardless of merit.

The consequence is a gap in the market. Assets priced high enough to justify diligence get bought. Assets priced too low to justify diligence often find no institutional buyer at all, and the willing buyers who remain are individuals or very small consultancies with limited ability to pay or to do anything with it.

**A low price also forecloses the upside permanently.** Once the marks and copyrights are assigned, there is no second sale. If the standard gains traction two years from now under someone else's ownership, that value is theirs.

## 3. What is actually blocking a sale right now

Three gates, all named in your own playbook, all still open.

**Gate 1: the trademarks are not registered.** This is the largest single problem and the cheapest to fix. A buyer acquiring "JRS" and "Decision Reconstruction Risk" is principally buying a category name. Unregistered, what conveys is common-law rights: geographically limited, harder to enforce, and dependent on proving use. Registered, it is a listed asset with a serial number that a buyer's counsel can verify in minutes. The difference in how a diligence lawyer treats those two situations is substantial.

**Gate 2: no organization is using it.** Zero. This has been the binding constraint since July and it has not moved. Buyers of early-stage IP want revenue, users, or a defensible moat. Publication supplies credibility, which supports a price but is not itself any of those three.

**Gate 3: contributor rights are unassigned.** More than twenty international reviewers contributed data with no signed assignment. Diligence will find this. It is not fatal, because the reviews are aggregated and de-identified, but it produces exactly the kind of unresolved question that makes a cautious buyer either discount heavily or walk.

There is also a structural limit worth stating plainly: **a methodology is not protectable as such.** Copyright protects your specific written expression, the guides, the training, the papers, not the underlying system or method. Absent a patent, which you do not have and which would be difficult here, nothing stops a competitor from implementing the same five conditions under a different name. What is genuinely ownable is the brand, the written corpus, the validation dataset, the code, and the domain. Price the asset as that bundle, because that is what a buyer's counsel will conclude it is.

## 4. What publication does and does not do

Publication is worth having and it is not a demand-side event.

**What it does:** makes the standard citable, gives a buyer's marketing function something to point at, signals that the work survived outside scrutiny, and materially strengthens your personal credential independent of any sale.

**What it does not do:** create a customer, register a mark, assign contributor rights, or generate revenue. Your own action plan states the point precisely: the jump in sale probability comes from the demand-side gates, not from more artifacts. Three published papers with zero users is a stronger version of the same position, not a different one.

There is also a timing mismatch. Acceptance notification and actual publication are typically months apart, and buyer processes run three to six months on top of that.

## 5. The realistic buyer pool at a low price

| Buyer type | Fit | Realistic appetite |
|---|---|---|
| GRC and compliance software vendors | Want frameworks to embed and market | Will look, will want the mark registered, will ask about users |
| Training and certification companies | Your training, guide, and certificate infrastructure already exists and works | Best fit at a low price; they buy content and brand routinely |
| Consulting firms | Want a proprietary methodology to differentiate | Plausible, often prefer to license or hire rather than buy |
| Publishers | Want the written corpus | Small money, simplest transaction |
| Individual practitioners | Want the credential and the material | Can transact fast, cannot pay much |

The training and certification archetype is the most realistic at the price level you are describing. That transaction is closest to a content acquisition, the diligence is lighter, and what you have built maps directly onto what they sell.

## 6. What I would do, in this order

1. **File the trademark applications this month.** Before any buyer conversation. USPTO filing fees are a few hundred dollars per class; an attorney adds more but is not strictly required to file. A pending application is itself a listed asset and signals that the category claim is being defended. This is the single highest return on effort available to you, and it has been outstanding since July.
2. **Send the contributor assignment request** to the reviewers you are already emailing about name and title confirmation. You have an open channel to them right now for the results summary. Adding a short assignment consent to that same message costs nothing and closes Gate 3 while people are still engaged. Once the study closes and everyone disperses, this gets much harder.
3. **Submit the articles.** Continue as planned. They build the credential regardless of sale outcome.
4. **Consider licensing before selling.** A non-exclusive license to a training company generates revenue, creates the customer evidence you lack, and does not foreclose a later sale. It is the only move that improves both the price and the probability at the same time.
5. **Set a floor and hold it.** Decide now the number below which you would rather keep the asset. Selling a category name for a few thousand dollars to clear the decks is a decision that cannot be undone, and the marks alone are worth defending for longer than that.

## 7. The honest bottom line

At a deliberately low price, with publication done and the gates unfixed, I estimate roughly a **one in four to one in five chance** of finding a buyer within a year. The most likely single outcome remains no sale.

That probability roughly doubles if you file the marks and get one organization to actually use the standard, and those are the only two moves that shift it materially. Everything else, including further publication, adjusts the price you could ask rather than the likelihood anyone asks.

The asset is real but it is currently a brand without registration, a corpus without a customer, and a dataset with unassigned contributor rights. Those are all fixable, two of them cheaply and quickly. Fixing them is worth more than another article.
