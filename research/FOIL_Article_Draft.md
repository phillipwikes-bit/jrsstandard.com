## Abstract

**Background.** Public-records determinations under FOIA and state freedom-of-information laws are frequently reviewed: on administrative appeal, before an open-government body, or in Article 78 litigation. A determination can read as complete and still fail when a reviewer cannot reconstruct its basis from the record itself. As federal and state agencies modernize FOIA technology, an adjacent question is whether the records those tools help produce remain independently reviewable.

**Objective.** This pilot asks whether a structured, record-level review standard, the Justification Review Standard (JRS), identifies documentation characteristics associated with defensible public-records determinations, and whether it can serve as an outcome-quality metric that complements FOIA technology modernization rather than competing with it.

**Methods.** Real public determinations (state FOI appeal decisions, open-government opinions, and appellate rulings where the sufficiency of the record was at issue) are paired with their documented outcomes. For each case the JRS read (Ready, Needs work, or Gap) is recorded first, from the record alone and blind to the outcome; the documented outcome and a public citation are then recorded. Public material only; each case carries a citation. Analysis follows a pre-registered plan; no association is claimed until the sample reaches target with a spread of outcomes.

**Results (preliminary, descriptive; not for inference).** The current verified sample is seven cases from New York and Connecticut sources, collected 26 June to 6 July 2026. JRS reads to date are five Ready and two Needs work, with zero Gap. Documented outcomes span two recorded categories. Because there is no Gap read yet, the outcome spread is incomplete, and n is small, no association between JRS read and outcome is asserted here.

**Contribution.** DRR names a documentation-governance problem that FOIA technology inventories do not capture: whether a determination remains reconstructable and defensible on review. JRS offers a workflow-independent, outcome-quality control for it. The pilot design is offered as a template for evaluating documentation quality in public-records programs.

**Keywords:** FOIA; public records; documentation risk; decision defensibility; records governance; administrative review; responsible AI in government.

## 1. Introduction and Background

### 1.1 The defensibility gap in public-records determinations
A records officer who denies, redacts, or partially grants a request produces a written determination. That determination is the artifact a later reviewer reads: an appeals officer, an open-government commission, or a court on Article 78 review. The reviewer does not re-interview the officer's memory; the reviewer reads the record. A determination can be facially complete, professionally written, and still fail on review because the specific basis for each withholding, the exemption relied on, the records located and produced, and the reasoning that connects them, cannot be reconstructed from the record itself.

That gap is not a technology problem in the usual sense. It is a documentation-quality problem, and it decides real outcomes: whether a withholding is upheld or ordered disclosed, whether an agency's process reads as reasoned or as conclusory.

### 1.2 Why now: the modernization moment
On 28 May 2026, the Chief FOIA Officers Council announced a government-wide initiative to identify and improve FOIA technologies, document-review tools, and related applications. As agencies evaluate new tooling, including AI-assisted drafting and review, an equally important question runs alongside it: whether the records the tools help produce stay independently reviewable, evidentiary, and defensible. Better software that produces fluent but unreconstructable determinations does not reduce reversals; it can increase them.

### 1.3 The measurement gap
Technology inventories measure what software an agency uses. Very few measure whether the resulting records are actually reviewable. The distinction matters: two agencies can run the same case-management system and produce determinations of very different defensibility. JRS is aimed at that second question, the quality of the output, not the identity of the tool.

### 1.4 Positioning
JRS does not evaluate or rank FOIA software. It evaluates whether the documentation produced through any workflow, manual or AI-assisted, remains complete enough for an independent reviewer to reconstruct and assess the agency's reasoning. It is a governance layer that sits above the technology stack: complementary to case-management systems, e-discovery platforms, and AI document-review tools, not a competitor to them.

## 2. Purpose and Research Questions

**Primary purpose.** Evaluate whether a structured documentation-review standard identifies characteristics associated with public-records determinations that hold up on review.

**Secondary purpose.** Explore whether documentation quality can serve as an independent governance metric alongside existing FOIA technology-modernization efforts.

**Research questions.**
- RQ1. Is a record's JRS read (Ready, Needs work, or Gap), recorded before the outcome is known, associated with the documented review outcome?
- RQ2. Can the JRS conditions be applied consistently to real public-records determinations across cases and reviewers?
- RQ3. What documentation characteristics distinguish determinations that hold up on review from those that do not?

## 3. The Justification Review Standard

JRS asks one question of a record: can a later, independent reviewer reconstruct how the conclusion was reached from the record alone? It evaluates that question through five conditions and yields a three-level read.

**The five conditions.** RC1 Reconstructability (the conclusion can be rebuilt from the record alone); RC2 Basis Identification (the source of each characterization is identifiable); RC3 Chronological Integrity (dates, sequence, and sources hold together when read cold); RC4 Decision-Process Traceability (the reasoning from evidence to conclusion can be followed and the responsible parties are identifiable); RC5 Evidentiary Sufficiency (the record contains enough to support the weight of the decision).

**The three reads.** Ready (a later reviewer could reconstruct the conclusion from the record alone), Needs work (partly reconstructable, some basis visible with gaps), or Gap (the basis is not visible in the record). The named risk the standard detects is Decision Reconstruction Risk (DRR): the condition in which a record cannot, on its own terms, let an independent reviewer reconstruct the basis for a consequential decision.

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Design
Retrospective and records-based. For each case, a real public determination in which the sufficiency of the record was at issue (a state FOI appeal decision, an open-government commission ruling, or an appellate decision) is paired with its documented outcome. The JRS read is recorded first, from the record itself and blind to the outcome; the documented outcome and the public citation are then recorded. Recording the read before the outcome is what keeps the read independent of the result.

### 4.2 Materials
Public material only, de-identified as to any private individual, with each case carrying a public citation or source URL. No internal, confidential, or agency-privileged material is used.

### 4.3 Sample
Target: 20 to 30 cases with a deliberate spread of outcomes, collected in small batches. **Current verified sample: n = 7** (seven distinct public sources, collected 26 June to 6 July 2026, contributor E-08). The sample is being built toward target with attention to including determinations that were overturned or ordered disclosed, so that both outcome directions and all three JRS reads are represented.

### 4.4 Outcome coding
Documented outcomes are coded from the cited decision. The raw data store currently records working labels (`challenged`, `failed_appeal`) that will be mapped to a published, auditable upheld-versus-overturned scheme before any analysis. Each case's outcome will be verified against the cited decision at coding time. No outcome is inferred; each traces to the public record.

### 4.5 Reliability
Where a second reviewer scores a subset of cases, inter-rater agreement on the JRS read is reported (Gwet's AC1 as the primary chance-corrected coefficient, consistent with the broader JRS validation program). In that broader program, three AI systems from three vendors applied the JRS read to a constructed record set and agreed 84 percent of the time (a consistency signal, not accuracy), and independent expert reviewers reached Gwet's AC1 of 0.74 (interim, on 10 records). These are context for the method's consistency, not results of this pilot.

### 4.6 Analysis plan
The primary analysis is the association between JRS read and documented outcome, tested with an exact method appropriate to small cell counts (Fisher's exact), reported with an effect size and interval. A secondary, descriptive analysis characterizes the documentation features that recur in overturned determinations (which conditions among RC1 to RC5 are most often missing). Pre-registered thresholds govern any inferential claim; a null result is reported plainly as null, not reinterpreted.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Documentation quality as an independent governance metric
The reviewability of a determination is a property of the record, measurable regardless of the software or AI system used to produce it. That is what makes a documentation-quality metric portable: it applies to a hand-written denial and an AI-drafted one alike, and it measures the thing that decides appeals, whether the basis can be reconstructed.

### 6.2 Complementarity with FOIA technology modernization
A technology inventory answers "what tools are in use." A documentation-quality read answers "do the outputs hold up." The two sit naturally together: an agency can adopt new review software and, in parallel, sample its determinations for reconstructability, catching the case where better tooling produces fluent but unreconstructable records.

### 6.3 Practical implications for records officers
Determinations that reconstruct their own basis, that name each exemption, itemize the records located and produced, and connect reasoning to cited authority, are less likely to be reversed and more consistent across officers. The five conditions are usable as a pre-issuance checklist, not only as a research instrument.

## 7. Potential Contribution to Federal FOIA Modernization
Data from this pilot may inform later discussion of documentation-quality metrics, AI-assisted records-review governance, explainability of agency determinations, reviewer consistency, documentation-risk indicators, and quality-assurance and training for public-records professionals. These are offered as directions the evidence could support once the validation sample is complete, not as claims established here.

## 8. Limitations
Small and growing sample (n = 7 at this stage); single domain (public records / FOIL); cases are advisor-selected from published decisions rather than randomly sampled; outcome coding depends on the cited record and the mapping scheme is being finalized; the jurisdictions to date are limited to New York and Connecticut. Generalization beyond the sampled jurisdictions is not claimed. JRS remains in a validation phase and makes no proven-effectiveness claim.

## 9. Conclusion
The drafting tool and the technology stack will keep changing; the evidentiary test does not. A public-records determination that cannot be reconstructed from its own contents cannot be independently defended, whoever or whatever produced it. A structured documentation-review standard offers an outcome-quality metric that is complementary to FOIA technology modernization and applicable regardless of the workflow used. Whether JRS reads predict review outcomes is the empirical question this pilot is built to answer, and that answer is earned only when the pre-registered sample is complete.
