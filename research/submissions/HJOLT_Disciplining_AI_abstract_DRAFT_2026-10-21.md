**No Discipline Without a Record: Reconstructability as a Precondition for Sanctioning AI**

Proposals to discipline AI directly assume that someone can later establish what a system did and why. That assumption is rarely tested. Any disciplinary process, whether it targets an AI system, its developer or its deployer, depends on a record from which an independent reviewer can rebuild the basis of the decision under scrutiny. Where that record is missing or thin, liability standards cannot be applied, violations cannot be detected with confidence, and the subject of discipline cannot meaningfully contest the finding.

This essay argues that reconstructability should be treated as a threshold condition for AI discipline, and that it can be assessed before harm occurs, not only after. It draws on the Justification Review Standard (JRS), a record-level review method developed by the author, and on Decision Reconstruction Risk: the risk that the basis of a consequential decision cannot be rebuilt from the record alone. Empirical work applying the method to 32 public-records determinations, with each read recorded before the documented outcome was consulted, found that a structured read distinguished records whose basis could be rebuilt from those whose basis could not (Young and Wikes, 2026, SSRN).

The essay engages Subtheme 3 (Civil Liberties) and Subtheme 4 (Implementation). On civil liberties, it argues that due process in any sanction regime, whether the sanction falls on the AI or on those who deploy it, requires a reconstructable record, and that the cost of an unreconstructable record should not fall on the person affected by the decision. On implementation, it proposes that agencies and private actors require record-level reconstructability review at the point a consequential AI-assisted decision is finalised. That gives regulators an evidence-producing mechanism that operates before harm rather than after it, and gives the "detecting violations" stage of discipline something to work with.

The essay closes with the limits of the approach. Reconstructability is necessary but not sufficient for fair discipline, and the existing empirical base is small and drawn from public-records settings rather than from AI deployments.
