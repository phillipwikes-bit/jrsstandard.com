Subject: Your name is on this paper, and here is why

Gabi,

I am writing to tell you formally that you are a co-author on the research paper coming out of this work, and to explain properly why. Not as a thank-you. As an accounting of what you actually contributed, because you should know precisely what your name is going on and why it belongs there.

The byline reads Wikes, Cortez, Hossain. The paper is "Decision Reconstruction Risk Is Detectable: An International Expert Panel Evaluation of a Record-Level Review Standard," going to *AI and Ethics*.

**Here is exactly what is yours.**

Section 2.1, "The record and the person it describes," is your section. It is not a paragraph acknowledging your influence. It is a full section carrying an argument that the rest of the paper depends on, and it exists because of three ideas that came from you.

**First: that a record has to be understandable to the person it describes, not only to the file.** Almost everything written about documentation quality is written from the institution's side. Defensible files, auditable trails, records that survive litigation. Those are real interests and they are not the only ones. You were the one who kept insisting that the person the decision is about is also owed something, and that the record is often the only account of the decision they will ever receive. That reframing is now the paper's normative half. Without it, this is a risk-management paper. With it, it is a paper about accountability in both directions, to the institution and to the person, which is a considerably more serious piece of work.

**Second: the bilingual intake insight, which is the sharpest thing in the section.** You showed me that a determination can be technically complete in English and functionally opaque to the person it describes, and that when that happens the record has satisfied the file and failed the person. That observation does real work in the paper. It demonstrates that accessibility is not a courtesy sitting outside the reconstruction test, it is part of the test, because a record only its author can explain is unreconstructable whether the barrier is a missing citation or a language the reader does not speak. That is one test catching two failures, and it came from your intake work, not from a literature review.

**Third: that a principle is only as strong as the range of people who have pressure-tested it.** This one changed the study's design, not just its prose. It is the stated reason the reviewer panel is international and multilingual rather than a convenient group in one jurisdiction. In the restructured paper, the panel's composition is no longer reported as background demographic detail. It is a methodological argument in the Methods section, and the argument is yours: a standard validated by reviewers who share one legal culture and one first language has only been tested against a narrow slice of the conditions it will actually meet, because what an unreconstructable record depends on to look complete is precisely the set of assumptions a reader is presumed to already hold. If the standard holds across ten countries and five continents, it is being tested against something closer to real conditions. If it only held in one, that would be a finding about that jurisdiction and not about records.

That third idea is why the paper can claim what it claims. It is load-bearing.

And underneath all of it is "stand on business," which was yours before any of this had a name, and which is still the shortest and most accurate statement of what the whole standard is trying to enforce.

**What co-authorship means here, and what it requires of you.**

I hold authorship to a strict standard and I will not hand it out as a courtesy, including to you. So it comes with real conditions:

- Section 2.1 needs to be yours in fact, not only in credit. I have drafted it out to a full section using your ideas, and I want you to take it, rewrite it, cut what I got wrong, and put it in your own voice. It is your argument and it should sound like you making it.
- You need to read the whole manuscript and approve it. Not skim it. Your name is on the empirical claims too, and you should be comfortable defending all of them, including the parts that report where the study fell short.
- Tell me how you want to be named and described in the author line, and how you want to be described in the opening. The old draft called you "my mentee," which was accurate when I was the only author and is not the right register now that the paper is partly yours.

**One thing I have to disclose openly, because the paper does.**

You are also one of the reviewers whose reads are part of the study data. That dual role is stated plainly in the manuscript: your authorship rests on your conceptual and written contribution to Sections 1 and 2.1, not on your own reviews, and you took no part in the scoring or the analysis. I want you to see that language before it goes anywhere, because it protects you as much as it protects the study. If anyone ever asks how a data contributor came to be an author, the answer is on the page and it is clean.

**Where the paper actually stands, so nothing surprises you.**

The headline result is solid: the panel identified the unreconstructable records at 82.8 percent accuracy, clearing the threshold we set before we looked at any data. Three AI systems from three different vendors apply the review consistently. Reviewer agreement is substantial and reported as interim.

The comparison arm, the one testing whether the standard beats unaided judgment, did not reach a conclusive answer. We report it as a null with the sample size a real answer would need. I am telling you this because you are an author now and you should hear the weak part from me rather than find it in the draft. The paper is honest about it, and I would rather publish something true and incomplete than something tidy.

You taught me the standard this paper is trying to hold documentation to. It is right that your name is on it.
