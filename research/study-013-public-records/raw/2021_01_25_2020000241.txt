                                  ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

When discrimination is found, an agency must provide a complainant with a remedy that
constitutes full, make-whole relief to restore the complainant as nearly as possible to the position
she would have occupied absent the discrimination. See, e.g., Franks v. Bowman Transp. Co., 424
U.S. 747, 764 (1976); Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody, 422 U.S. 405, 418-19 (1975); Lazaro G. v.
Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC Appeal No. 0120170802 (May 17, 2019), req. for recon. den. EEOC
Request No. 2019004115 (Sept. 17, 2019); Adesanya v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
01933395 (July 21, 1994).

Compensatory Damages

In its final decision as noted above, the Agency awarded Complainant $12,000 in non-pecuniary
compensatory damages, which Complainant contends is inadequate. Pursuant to section 102(a) of
the Civil Rights Act of 1991, a Complainant who establishes unlawful intentional discrimination
under either Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e
et seq, or Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29
U.S.C. § 791 et seq. may receive compensatory damages for past and future pecuniary losses (i.e.,
out-of-pocket expenses) and non-pecuniary losses (e.g., pain and suffering, mental anguish) as part
of this “make whole” relief. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). In West v. Gibson, 527 U.S. 2012 (1999),
the Supreme Court held that Congress afforded the Commission the authority to award
compensatory damages in the administrative process. For an employer with more than 500
employees, such as the Agency, the limit of liability for future pecuniary and non-pecuniary
damages is $300,000. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3); Wilda M. v. Dep’t of Homeland Security, EEOC
Appeal No. 0120142660 (Dec. 2, 2016).

To receive an award of compensatory damages, Complainant must demonstrate that she had been
harmed as a result of the agency's discriminatory action; the extent, nature, and severity of the
harm; and the duration or expected duration of the harm. Rivera v. Dep't of the Navy, EEOC
Appeal No. 01934157 (July 22, 1994), req. for reconsideration denied, EEOC Request No.
05940927 (Dec. 11, 1995); Compensatory and Punitive Damages Available Under Section 102 of
the Civil Rights Act of 1991, EEOC Notice No. 915.002 (July 14, 1992), at 11-12, 14.

Non-pecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise quantification, i.e., emotional pain,
suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to professional standing,
injury to character and reputation, injury to credit standing, and loss of health. See EEOC Notice
No. 915.302 at 10 (July 14, 1992). There is no precise formula for determining the amount of
damages for non-pecuniary losses except that the award should reflect the nature and severity of
the harm and the duration or expected duration of the harm. See Loving v. Dep't of the Treasury,
EEOC Appeal No. 01955789 (Aug. 29, 1997). The Commission notes that non-pecuniary
compensatory damages are designed to remedy the harm caused by the discriminatory event rather
than punish the Agency for the discriminatory action.
                                                   9                      2019005970 & 2020000241

Furthermore, compensatory damages should not be motivated by passion or prejudice or
“monstrously excessive” standing alone but should be consistent with the amounts awarded in
similar cases. See Ward-Jenkins v. Dep't of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No. 01961483 (Mar. 4,
1999).

Evidence from a health care provider or other expert is not a mandatory prerequisite for recovery
of compensatory damages for emotional harm. See Lawrence v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal
No. 01952288 (Apr 18, 1996) citing Carle v. Dep't of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan.
5, 1993). Objective evidence of compensatory damages can include statements from Complainant
concerning his emotional pain or suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of
life, injury to professional standing, injury to character or reputation, injury to credit standing, loss
of health, and any other non-pecuniary losses that are incurred as a result of the discriminatory
conduct. Id.

Statements from others including family members, friends, health care providers, other counselors
(including clergy) could address the outward manifestations or physical consequences of
emotional distress, including sleeplessness, anxiety, stress, depression, marital strain, humiliation,
emotional distress, loss of self-esteem, excessive fatigue, or a nervous breakdown. Id.
Complainant's own testimony, along with the circumstances of a particular case, can suffice to
sustain his burden in this regard. Id. The more inherently degrading or humiliating the defendant's
action is, the more reasonable it is to infer that a person would suffer humiliation or distress from
that action. Id. The absence of supporting evidence, however, may affect the amount of damages
appropriate in specific cases. Id.

We note at the outset that, contrary to Complainant’s contention throughout these two proceedings,
the Agency did conduct an investigation of her claim for damages, as evidenced by the two
investigatory affidavits that Complainant had submitted in support of her claim. Beyond these
sworn declarations, however, Complainant has not presented any medical documentation of the
harm she claimed to have suffered or statements from friends, relatives, health care providers, or
others attesting to that harm. Complainant’s two affidavits therefore constitute the entirety of the
evidentiary record on her claim for compensatory damages.

Complainant averred that when her medical clearance for overseas duty was denied, she was
assigned to the El Paso Intelligence Center (DEA-EPIC) where she was forced to use a spare desk
in a shared office bullpen among former subordinates. She stated that was not given the resources
she needed to do her job and had to work with no privacy and constant distractions, and that she
found the situation to be “very degrading.” She averred that this work dynamic caused her to
experience “never-ending stress and anxiety.” She averred that she had developed insomnia from
staying up all night at home in order to communicate with personnel in Kabul, which was eleven
time zones away. She further averred that her work arrangement caused her undue financial strain
and physical distress, including migraine headaches. Supplemental Investigative Report –
Damages (SIR-D) 46-47. In addition, she averred that in January 2014, the DEA reassigned her
to its headquarters, forcing her to relocate to Virginia from Texas and leave her longtime home
and community. SIR-D 47-48.
                                                10                    2019005970 & 2020000241

She also averred that she experienced significant stress as a result of having to work as a program
analyst after thirty-years of law enforcement experience and not being able to use the skills she
had honed during those years. SIR-D 48. Finally, Complainant reported that she had retired three
years earlier than she had wanted to, and that she had obtained treatment for her symptoms, which
had stabilized. SIR-D 49-50. Complainant maintained that she was entitled to $150,000 in
damages for emotional distress. SIR-D 50-52. She neither requested nor documented damages
for out-of-pocket expenses. SIR-D 53-56.

We agree with the Agency that under the circumstances presented by this case, a $150,000 award
would be excessive. The discriminatory act in question was the decision by the Agency’s OMS to
deny her the medical clearance she needed to return to the U.S. Embassy in Afghanistan under the
mistaken assumption that Complainant posed a safety risk to herself due to the medication she was
taking. But according to Complainant’s own statements, much of her emotional distress was
attributed to her work conditions at the DEA-EPIC and later at DEA’s headquarters.
Consequently, there is no causation chain between the Agency’s discriminatory act and
Complainant’s emotional distress. Nevertheless, were it not for the denial of her medical
clearance, Complainant would have returned to Afghanistan and would not have been reassigned
to the EPIC and Headquarters, and thus would not have been subjected to those conditions she
found to be adverse. The Agency acknowledged as much by awarding Complainant $12,000
award for nonpecuniary damages. The question for us to resolve is whether this amount was
appropriate.

In a number of cases, the Commission has awarded $15,000 in nonpecuniary compensatory
damages for comparable harm suffered in terms of severity and duration. For example, in Sid E.
v. Dep’t of the Army, EEOC Appeal No. 0120172812 (Feb. 27, 2019), req. for recon. granted on
Commission’s own motion, but $15,000 compensatory damages award affirmed, EEOC Request
No. 2019003495 (Oct. 30, 2019), the Commission awarded $15,000 where management blatantly
disregarded the employee’s multiple requests for accommodation over nine months, causing the
employee to suffer anxiety, headaches, loss of confidence, hypertension and exacerbation of
multiple sclerosis. In addition to his own testimony, the employee provided sworn statements
from his wife and sister-in-law. In Dalton C. v. Dep’t of Health & Human Services, EEOC Appeal
No. 0120170077 (Mar. 27, 2018), the Commission awarded the employee $15,000 even though
he provided limited medical evidence regarding the worsening of his preexisting conditions as a
result of the Agency’s failure to reasonably accommodate him. Witness statements from the
employee’s mother and from several coworkers established that the employee had become more
depressed and anxious after the incident, and that he began to struggle with self-esteem and
confidence. In Margorie L. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0720170028 (Nov.
16, 2017), the employee’s treating psychiatrist showed that the employer’s discriminatory act
exacerbated her pre-existing post-traumatic-stress disorder. The employee presented evidence that
she experienced nightmares, insomnia, migraine headaches and other symptoms. On cross-
examination, however, the employee admitted that she had prior medical conditions that might
have impacted her symptoms, including high blood pressure, dizziness, and sleep apnea.
                                                  11                     2019005970 & 2020000241

In each of these prior decisions, as well as in the cases cited within these decisions, the person
seeking damages had presented at least some evidence of harm beyond his or her own statements.
Complainant has not done so in this case. Nevertheless, we find that an award $13,000 in
nonpecuniary compensatory damages is appropriate. It takes into consideration the nature of the
OMS’s unlawful denial of Complainant’s medical clearance to return to her overseas post, the
severity and duration of the harm she suffered as a result of that act as well as the intervening effect
of the working conditions imposed upon her by her employer-of-record, the DEA. We will
therefore enter an order directing the Agency to award Complainant $13,000 in nonpecuniary
compensatory damages.

Backpay

The purpose of a back pay award is to restore to Complainant the income she would have otherwise
earned but for the discrimination. See Albemarle Paper Co., 422 U.S. 405. Back pay should
include all forms of compensation and must reflect fluctuations in working time, overtime rates,
penalty overtime, Sunday premium and night work, changing rate of pay, transfers, promotions,
and privileges of employment to which Complainant would have been entitled but for the
discrimination. Potter v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 0720120029 (Sept. 10, 2013), req.
for recon. den. EEOC Request No. 0520140083 (May 9, 2014). See also Ulloa v. U.S. Postal
Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04A30025 (Aug. 3, 2004) citing Allen v. Dep't of the Air Force, EEOC
Petition No. 04940006 (May 31, 1996); Perez v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04A40041
(Mar. 3, 2005).

The Commission realizes that precise measurement cannot always be used to remedy the wrong
inflicted and that the computation of back pay awards inherently involves some speculation.
Hanns v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04960030 (Sept. 18, 1997). Nonetheless,
uncertainties involved in a back pay determination should be resolved against the Agency since it
has already been found to have committed the acts of discrimination. Id.; see also Kloock v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04A40012 (June 16, 2004).

Complainant stated in her affidavit on damages that while she was stationed at EPIC, she lost all
of her foreign pay benefits, including separate maintenance allowance, post differential, danger
pay and overtime pay since she was not physically present in Afghanistan. She also averred that
she was not entitled to EPIC locality pay because she was still technically assigned to the Embassy
in Kabul and was only receiving base pay at the foreign base pay scale. She maintained that
between May 2013 and May 2014, she lost approximately $5,268 per month in total pay
differential, another $2,500 in housing and food costs; and sixty days of accrued home leave worth
$37,200. Overall, Complainant averred that she lost roughly $142,468 per year which amounted
to $427,404 over three years, until her retirement. SIR-D 47, 49-50, 58-72.

Again, even though the Agency was not Complainant’s employer-of-record, and not responsible
for paying her salary, it was the Agency’s discriminatory act that caused her to lose the benefits
that came with her overseas assignment.
                                                 12                     2019005970 & 2020000241

The Agency therefore is liable to Complainant for the pay and benefits that would have accrued to
her had the OMS given her the medical clearance she needed to return to the Embassy in Kabul.
The record is not sufficient for us to make the determination as to the appropriate amount of back
pay. We will therefore direct the Agency to calculate and pay Complainant the appropriate amount
in back pay in our order below.

Offer of Reemployment

Commission regulations provide that full relief includes an unconditional offer to an identified
victim of discrimination of placement in the position the person would have occupied absent the
discrimination or a substantially equivalent position. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.501(a)(3). A substantially
equivalent position is one with compensation, promotional opportunities, job responsibilities,
working conditions, and job status which are similar to the position discriminatorily lost.
Hernandez v. Soc. Sec. Admin., EEOC Request No. 05A60218 (Dec. 29, 2005). citing Myers v.
U.S. Postal Service, EEOC Petition No. 04950028 (May 2, 1996) & Kerschner v. Dep't of Labor,
EEOC Appeal No. 01933023 (March 15, 1994).

As noted above, the first element of our order for relief in Request No. 0520170120 directed the
Agency’s OMS to reexamine Complainant in order that she obtain the medical clearance necessary
to return to her Administrative Officer position at the U.S. Embassy in Kabul, Afghanistan. There
are no indications anywhere in the record that the Agency ever did this. She averred in her damages
affidavit that she had served for two years and would have been appointed to a second two-year
term had she not been barred from returning. She also averred that she had intended to have her
contract extended until 2017, that she could have served for a maximum term of five years that
could have been extended, and that she was forced to retire in June 2015. SIR-D 49. As late as
September 2019, when Complainant resubmitted her petition for enforcement, she made clear that
she still desired to return to Kabul, as demonstrated by the reference in her petition to the Agency’s
ongoing failure to give her the necessary medical examination. It is unclear whether Complainant
still wants to go back to Afghanistan at this late date, whether she is time-barred from doing so by
term limits, or how her retirement status would affect her decision.

We also note that Complainant may be entitled to front pay. In general, front pay may be awarded
in lieu of reinstatement when no position is available or when a subsequent working relationship
between the parties would be antagonistic. Pachecano v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
01A32170 (May 20, 2004). The fact that front pay is awarded in lieu of reinstatement implies that
Complainant is able to work but cannot do so because of circumstances outside of her control. Id.
citing Goetze v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01991530 (Aug. 22, 2001). In Pachecano,
we found that the employee had not shown that the requirements for front pay in lieu of
reinstatement had been met because the employee’s medical condition had made him unavailable
to return to work. Id. citing Finlay v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01942985 (April 30,
1997) & York v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01930435 (February 25, 1994). Here,
Complainant’s rheumatoid arthritis did not preclude her from returning to work overseas.
                                                 13                    2019005970 & 2020000241

Had the Agency timely administered her clearance exam, she would have gone back to Kabul. See
also Breanne H. v. Dep’t of Transp., EEOC Petition No. 0420180015 (Nov. 30, 2018) (The
Commission has identified three circumstances where front pay may be awarded in lieu of
reinstatement: (1) where no position is available; (2) where a subsequent working relationship
between the parties would be antagonistic; or (3) where the employer has a record of long-term
resistance to anti-discrimination efforts [citations omitted]); Carmina E. v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC
Appeal No. 0720150011 (Jan. 9, 2018).

In our order for relief, we will direct the Agency to conduct the medical clearance examination in
order that DEA might reinstate Complainant to her post in Kabul, Afghanistan. If the position that
Complainant seeks to re-encumber is no longer available, or if returning to overseas duty is not
feasible for reasons other than Complainant’s unavailability, then we will direct the Agency to
award front pay for two years from the date the decision on this petition for enforcement is issued.3

Attorneys’ Fees

By federal regulation, the Agency is required to award attorney's fees for the successful processing
of an EEO complaint in accordance with existing case law and regulatory standards. 29 C.F.R. §
1614.501(e)(1)(ii). To determine the proper amount of the fee, a lodestar amount is reached by
calculating the number of hours reasonably expended by the attorney on the complaint multiplied
by a reasonable hourly rate. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886 (1984); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S.
424 (1983). The circumstances under which the lodestar may be adjusted are extremely limited
and are set forth in EEO Management Directive 110. Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 at 11-13 (EEO MD-110) (Aug. 15, 2015).
Complainant’s Counsel submitted documentation sufficient to establish that she is owed
$56,366.01 in attorneys’ fees and costs as of December 2018. Counsel also stated in the petition
for enforcement that she resubmitted in September 2019 that the Agency’s noncompliance with
our orders in Request No. 0120170120 and Compliance Order 2019000951 had resulted in
Complainant incurring additional attorneys’ fees. The Agency has not submitted any
documentation establishing that it had made a fee award to Complainant or her counsel. We will
therefore enter an order directing the Agency to issue a check to Complainant for the fees already
incurred and for Counsel to submit the required documentation for any additional fees.

Interest

Section 717(d) of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. 2000e-16(d), as amended
by section 114(2) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, Pub. L. 102-166 (Nov. 21, 1991), provides that
“the same interest to compensate for delay in payment shall be available [to federal employees] as
in cases involving nonpublic parties.”




3

                                               14                    2019005970 & 2020000241

By enacting the 1991 amendments, the Congress expressly waived the federal government's
sovereign immunity from interest for delay in the payment of all monetary awards to victims of
employment discrimination in the federal government to the same extent that any non-federal
entity would be subject to. Charles E. v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 07 20180006 (July
19, 1918).

Accordingly, Complainant is entitled to any interest that accrued because of the Agency’s delay in
awarding backpay, compensatory damages, and attorneys’ fees that resulted in a loss of value of
those obligations. Douglas v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01Aa52944 (June 28, 2005)
citing 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-16(d) (An interest award is necessary to fully compensate appellant for
the loss of his money). See also e.g. Kelly A. v Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No.
0120171256 (March 19, 2019) (interest on compensatory damages); Kessen v. Dep’t of Homeland
Sec., EEOC Appeal No. 0120072334 (April 8, 2008) (interest on back pay); Estate of Vernoff v.
U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 0420050020 (Jan 7, 2008) (interest on attorneys’ fees and
costs).

The Back Pay Act regulation at 5 C.F.R. § 550.806(d), provides that the rate or rates used to
compute the interest on a back pay award under 5 U.S.C.A. § 5596 shall be the annual percentage
rate or rates established by the Secretary of the Treasury as the Overpayment Rate under Internal
Revenue Service Regulation 26 U.S.C. § 6621(a), for the period or periods of time for which
interest is payable. Moreover, 5 C.F.R. § 550.806(e) provides that the interest rate shall be
compounded daily. Yovan v. Dep’t of Homeland Security, EEOC Appeal No. 0120083601 (Oct.
20, 2010).

If payments were made within a reasonable time, however, the Commission will not award interest.
Homer B. v. Dep’t of the Army, EEOC Appeal No. 0120161342 (May 24, 2018) citing
Complainant v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 0520140042 (Mar. 28, 2014) (substantial
compliance found and therefore no interest awarded); April v. Dep't of Agric., EEOC Appeal No.
01963775 (June 5, 1997)(two-year delay in payment of back pay found unreasonable and interest
awarded); Malladi v. Dep't of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 01961100 (Dec. 18, 1996)
(seven-month delay in payment of predetermined amount of attorney's fees not unreasonable);
Brown v. Dep't of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 01985765 (Dec. 1, 1999) (ten-month delay
unreasonable and interest awarded).

As noted above, the Commission issued its decision on EEOC Request No. 0520170120 on
November 21, 2018. According to the terms of the order, the Agency had 90 calendar days to
complete its supplemental investigation on Complainant's entitlement to compensatory damages,
or until February 18, 2019. Thereafter, it had 30 calendar days, or until March 19, 2019 to issue
its decision on compensatory damages. The Agency did not issue that decision until August 12,
2019 resulting in a delay of approximately five months. In light of our precedent, we do not find
this delay unreasonable and consequently, we decline to award interest on Complainant’s
compensatory damages award. As to backpay and attorneys’ fees, however, the order in Request
No. 0520170120 required the Agency to determine and award both within 60 calendar days, giving
the Agency until January 2019 to comply with those portions of the order.
                                                15                     2019005970 & 2020000241

But because January 19th fell on a Saturday, compliance should have been completed by Monday
January 21, 2019, the next business day. As of the date of this decision, both obligations remain
unsatisfied, and have been so for more than a year, a clearly unreasonable delay. We will therefore
direct the Agency to compute and award interest on Complainant’s backpay and attorneys’ fees
with the computation period beginning on January 21, 2019.

Sanctions

On appeal, Complainant contends that the Commission should impose sanctions on the Agency
for its willful and ongoing delay in compliance with our orders. The Commission has the inherent
power to control and prevent abuse of its orders, processes and procedures. The Commission can
and will impose sanctions for Agency malfeasance. Hashimoto v. Dep’t of Housing & Urban Dev.,
EEOC Petition No. 0420110011 (June 12, 2012) citing Turner v. Dep't of the Interior, EEOC
Petition No. 04980037 (Aug. 5, 1999) & Byrd v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Petition No. 04980004
(Dec. 12, 1997). To that end, should the Agency fail to comply with the actions mandated by the
Order set forth below, the Commission may issue a “Show Cause” notice to the Agency to appear
in person before the Commission to explain the Agency's continued failure to comply is authorized.
29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(e). Continued failure to comply also may result in this case being certified
to the Office of Special Counsel for enforcement action. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(f).
