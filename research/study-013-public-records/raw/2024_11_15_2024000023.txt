                         CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

On appeal, Complainant argues that the Agency’s award failed to fully remedy
Complainant for the harm she suffered as a result of the discrimination
because it did not award the full requested amount for her past medical
expenses, contending that the Agency’s reduction of the award to compensate
her medical expenses was arbitrary. Complainant also argues that she is
entitled to compensation for her lost wages and cost of future medical
treatment, arguing that she has been subjected to additional discrimination
when the Agency disciplined her for being absent while on FMLA leave and for
another incident in June 2023 when a male coworker told her that her non-
gender-conforming appearance made him uncomfortable, thereby triggering
her symptoms again. Complainant further argues that the Agency’s award of
non-pecuniary compensatory damages was inadequate and relied on cases
that were not comparable and that an award of $112,765.89 would be more
appropriate considering the extent of the distress she has suffered.

In response, the Agency argues that it appropriately considered the impact of
other stressors to reduce the amount of the award because Complainant’s
medical records contain ample evidence that other factors unrelated to the
Agency’s discrimination contributed to Complainant’s medical conditions. The
Agency also argues that the evidence indicates that part of Complainant’s
medical condition was due to stress over the EEO process, which is not
compensable. Furthermore, the Agency contends that Complainant is not
entitled to any damages as a result of any other allegedly discriminatory

actions which occurred in June 2023, four years after the discriminatory

                           STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29
C.F.R. § 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by
the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).            See Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9,
§ VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review
“requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the
factual and legal determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that
EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of record, including
any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its
interpretation of the law”).
                                  ANALYSIS

Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

When discrimination is found, an agency must provide the complainant with a
remedy that constitutes full, make-whole relief to restore him as nearly as
possible to the position he would have occupied absent the discrimination.
See, e.g., Franks v. Bowman Transp. Co., 424 U.S. 747, 764 (1976);
Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody, 422 U.S. 405, 418-19 (1975); Adesanya v.
U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01933395 (July 21, 1994). Pursuant to
section 102(a) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, a complainant who establishes
unlawful intentional discrimination under Title VII and the Rehabilitation Act
may receive compensatory damages for past and future pecuniary losses (i.e.,
out-of-pocket expenses) and nonpecuniary losses (e.g., pain and suffering,
mental anguish) as part of make-whole relief. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3).
Compensatory damages do not include back pay, interest on back pay, or any
other type of equitable relief. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(2). In West v. Gibson, 527
U.S. 212 (1999), the Supreme Court held that Congress afforded the
Commission the authority to award compensatory damages in the
administrative process.

Pecuniary losses are out-of-pocket expenses incurred because of the agency's
unlawful action, including job-hunting expenses, moving expenses, medical
expenses, psychiatric expenses, physical therapy expenses, and other
quantifiable out-of-pocket expenses. Past pecuniary losses are losses incurred
prior to the resolution of a complaint through a finding of discrimination, or a
                                       6                          2024000023

voluntary settlement. EEO MD-110, at Chap. 11, VII.B.2 (internal citations
omitted).

In a claim for pecuniary, compensatory damages, a complainant must
demonstrate, through appropriate evidence and documentation, the harm
suffered because of the agency’s discriminatory action. See Sheila O. v. Dep’t
of Health and Human Srvcs., EEOC Appeal No. 2021001250 (June 7, 2022).
Objective evidence in support of a claim for pecuniary damages includes
documentation showing actual out-of-pocket expenses with an explanation of
the expenditure. The agency is only responsible for those damages that are
clearly shown to be caused by the Agency’s discriminatory conduct. To recover
damages, a complainant must prove that the employer's discriminatory
actions were the cause of the pecuniary loss. Id. (citations omitted).

Complainant first argues that the Agency erred in not fully reimbursing her
claim for past medical expenses. We reject Complainant’s argument. To
begin with, we find that the Agency correctly concluded that Complainant had
not established that she was entitled to compensatory damages for the
medical expenses for her medication because Complainant did not provide any
documentation or receipts to support the cost of her medication. See Israel
F. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2024001719 (Aug. 6, 2024) (finding
the complainant not entitled to reimbursement where complainant did not
present documentary evidence to support claimed medical expenses). In
addition, to the extent Complainant sought to have her lost wages reimbursed
as part of her compensatory damages, we note that lost wages are considered
to be equitable relief and may not be included in an award for compensatory
damages. See Timothy M. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2019001562
(Feb. 4, 2020).       We further find that the Agency correctly denied
reimbursement for the TSP loans Complainant withdrew in October 2020, May
2022, and March 2023 because Complainant did not establish that the
financial hardship which necessitated the withdrawal of the loans were due to
the Agency’s discriminatory action.

We further find that the Agency correctly reduced the award of pecuniary
compensatory damages because the evidence in the record did not support
Complainant’s assertion that all of the claimed medical expenses were, in fact,
due to the Agency’s discriminatory action. We reiterate that it is complainant’s
burden to submit evidence to establish that the Agency’s discriminatory
conduct directly or proximately caused the losses for which damages are
sought. See Complainant v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 0120123467
(April 3, 2015). In this case, the evidence from Complainant’s counseling
records (Ex. A) and her March 2020 hospitalization (Ex. D) does not establish
                                      7                           2024000023

that the Agency’s discriminatory action caused all or even most of
Complainant’s mental health conditions. The notes from the counseling
sessions include a few mentions of the Agency’s discriminatory action for
sessions occurring between December 2019 through May 2020, for example
indicating that Complainant felt “severe anxiety” and had to take a break in
the bathroom when she saw the Manager’s car in the parking lot, a note from
December 30, 2019 noting that Complainant was feeling “anxiety and worry
regarding work incident,” and most recently in May 2020 when Complainant
stated “Work isn’t the same, how [she has been] treated.” See Ex. A. The
notes from Complainant’s counseling sessions from early 2020 through May
2023 indicate that beginning in approximately May 2020, the primary issues
Complainant was dealing with related to abuse from decades earlier, being
targeted by her union steward and being forced to file a grievance against
him, having issues with a co-worker who was “trying to get [Complainant]
angry/upset” in August 2020, past bullying, relationship issues, and grief over
the death of a co-worker.         See Ex. A.      In addition, Complainant’s
hospitalization records in March 2020 state that while she was being admitted
for “PTSD triggered from work-related issues,” the hospital records indicated
that Complainant stated that she had experienced a lot of past trauma and
“her anger has increased over the last two years,” pre-dating the Agency’s
discriminatory action. See Ex. D. We find that the Agency’s decision to
reimburse 50% of Complainant’s mental health treatment costs was
reasonable in light of the evidence indicating that the Agency was only
partially responsible for Complainant’s mental health conditions.

Future Pecuniary Damages

Future pecuniary damages are losses likely to occur after the resolution of the
complaint. While past pecuniary losses are not included in the $300,000.00
statutory cap, future pecuniary losses are subject to the statutory cap. See
Howen v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC Appeal No. 01A42618 (Dec. 13, 2004).
Where complainant has shown that her future earning power has been
diminished as a result of the agency’s discrimination, the Commission has
awarded future pecuniary damages for the loss of future earning capacity. See
Hernandez v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 07A3005 (July 16, 2004);
Brinkley v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05980429 (August 12, 1999);
Moore v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0720050084 (March 6, 2007).
Proof of entitlement to loss of future earning capacity involves evidence
suggesting that an individual’s injuries have narrowed the range of economic
opportunities available to her. Generally, the party seeking compensation for
loss of earning capacity needs to provide evidence to demonstrate with
reasonable certainty or reasonable probability that the earning capacity has
                                      8                          2024000023

been impaired and there must be evidence which will permit the fact finder to
arrive at a pecuniary value for the loss. See Welker v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC
Appeal No. 0120120330 (July 27, 2012).

In this case, Complainant sought future lost wages in the amount of
$19,840.00 for her future lost earning capacity while she was on FMLA leave
from June 6, 2023 to September 4, 2023. Complainant also argues that she
is entitled to the cost of future medical expenses because her mental health
conditions are chronic and ongoing and will necessitate future care.

We reject Complainant’s argument. To begin with, we note that Complainant
offered no documentation to support her claim to future medical expenses and
she has not provided any way to arrive at a reasonable estimate of what her
future medical expenses might be. We find that her assertion that she should
be reimbursed for future medical expenses is too speculative considering that
it does not take into account any projected improvement in Complainant’s
mental condition, nor does it consider the likely change in the cost of
counseling in future years. Under the circumstances, we do not find that
Complainant established that she is entitled to future pecuniary damages. See
Tien E. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 2020001343 (July 15, 2021). We
further find that Complainant is not entitled to future lost wages because of
her FMLA leave which, we note, occurred more than three years after the
Agency’s discriminatory action in December 2019. 3 While Complainant insists
that her PTSD flare-up in June 2023 can be attributed to the Agency because
of the incident where a male co-worker told her that her non-gender
conforming appearance made him uncomfortable, while we do not condone
her male co-worker’s statement, we note that it was an entirely discrete
incident from the incident in December 2019 for which the Agency was actually
found liable and the Agency is not required to compensate Complainant for
any consequences associated with the June 2023 incident.

Non-pecuniary Compensatory Damages

Nonpecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise quantification,
i.e., emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of
enjoyment of life, injury to professional standing, injury to character and
reputation, injury to credit standing, and loss of health. See EEOC Notice No.

3
 We further note that future lost earning capacity refers to a diminution in
Complainant’s future earning power and she has not established that merely
taking FMLA leave has somehow impaired her future earning capacity, e.g.,
by interfering in her ability to obtain future employment.
                                       9                           2024000023

915.302, Enforcement Guidance on Compensatory and Punitive Damages
Available Under Section 102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, at 10 (July 14,
1992). There is no precise formula for determining the amount of damages
for non-pecuniary losses except that the award should reflect the nature and
severity of the harm and the duration or expected duration of the harm. See
Loving v. Dep’t of the Treasury, EEOC Appeal No. 01955789 (Aug. 29, 1997).
The Commission notes that non-pecuniary compensatory damages are
designed to remedy the harm caused by the discriminatory event rather than
punish the agency for the discriminatory action. Furthermore, compensatory
damages should not be motivated by passion or prejudice or “monstrously
excessive” standing alone but should be consistent with the amounts awarded
in similar cases. See Ward-Jenkins v. Dep’t of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No.
01961483 (Mar. 4, 1999).

Evidence from a health care provider or other expert is not a mandatory
prerequisite for recovery of compensatory damages for emotional harm. See
Lawrence v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01952288 (Apr. 18, 1996)
(citing Carle v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan. 5, 1993)).
Objective evidence of compensatory damages can include statements from a
complainant concerning his emotional pain or suffering, inconvenience, mental
anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to professional standing, injury to
character or reputation, injury to credit standing, loss of health, and any other
non-pecuniary losses that are incurred as a result of the discriminatory
conduct. Id.

Statements from others including family members, friends, health care
providers, and other counselors (including clergy) could address the outward
manifestations or physical consequences of emotional distress, including
sleeplessness, anxiety, stress, depression, marital strain, humiliation,
emotional distress, loss of self-esteem, excessive fatigue, or a nervous
breakdown. Id. Complainant’s own testimony, along with the circumstances
of a particular case, can suffice to sustain his burden in this regard. Id. The
more inherently degrading or humiliating the defendant's action is, the more
reasonable it is to infer that a person would suffer humiliation or distress from
that action. Id. The absence of supporting evidence, however, may affect the
amount of damages appropriate in specific cases. Id.

Complainant has the burden of proving the existence, nature and severity of
the alleged emotional harm. Man H. v. Dep’t. of Homeland Sec’y, EEOC Appeal
No. 0120161218 (May 2, 2017). Complainant must also establish a causal
relationship between the alleged harm and the discrimination. Id. Absent such
proof of harm and causation, a complainant is not entitled to compensatory
                                      10                          2024000023

damages, even if there were a finding of unlawful discrimination. Id.; Wilda
M. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141087 (Jan. 12, 2017) (awards
for emotional harm are warranted only if complainant establishes a sufficient
causal connection between the agency’s illegal actions and her injury).

The Agency awarded Complainant non-pecuniary compensatory damages in
the amount of $10,000. Complainant argues that this amount was grossly
insufficient to compensate her for the nature, extent, severity, and duration
of the harm the Agency’s discriminatory action caused, arguing that she is still
suffering the effects of her PTSD as a result of the Agency’s discriminatory
action even now, more than four years after the Agency’s discriminatory
action. Complainant cites to her medical records and the fact that she was
hospitalized in March 2020 with suicidal ideations because of the Agency’s
discriminatory action and also that her relationship ended, which she
attributed to the discrimination, as well as citing the June 2023 incident with
her male co-coworker.

At the outset, we reiterate that Complainant is not entitled to compensatory
damages stemming from any flare-up or exacerbation of her pre-existing
conditions due to the incident in June 2023 as it was unrelated to the single
claim at issue in this case. We agree with Complainant, however, that the
Agency’s award of $10,000 was not sufficient to fully compensate Complainant
for the emotional pain and distress she suffered due to the Agency’s
discriminatory action. We find that an award of $30,000 is more appropriate
considering the evidence in Complainant’s counseling records indicating that
Complainant suffered from an exacerbation of her pre-existing PTSD, anxiety
and depression for at least six months following the incident in December 2019
and that she was hospitalized in March 2020 due, at least in part, to her
ongoing distress over the Agency’s discriminatory action. In doing so, we note
that the evidence in the record does not support Complainant’s assertion that
the Agency’s discriminatory action directly contributed to her ongoing and
lingering mental health conditions for years after the incident. As stated
supra, the evidence in Complainant’s counseling records indicate that after
May 2020, there are no real mentions of the December 2019 incident but
rather that Complainant was troubled due to being targeted by her union
steward, interpersonal issues with a co-worker, grief over the death of a co-
worker, among other issues which pre-date the Agency’s discriminatory
action. We emphasize that there is nothing in the record to indicate that either
Complainant’s grievance against the union steward or her contentious
relationship with a co-worker were at all related to the December 2019
incident for which the Agency was found liable.
                                       11                          2024000023

We find that an award of $30,000 considers the severity and the duration of
the harm done to Complainant by the Agency’s action and is also not
“monstrously excessive” standing alone and is consistent with the amounts
awarded in similar cases. See Michael G. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC
Appeal No. 2021002218 (June 29, 2022) (awarding a complainant $35,000
where the complainant suffered stress, moodiness, loss of appetite, weight
loss, and strained relationships with his wife and daughter over a period of 18
months as a result of the agency’s discriminatory harassment); Erik S. v. Dep’t
of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 2020004617 (Dec. 13, 2021) (affirming an award
of $30,000 where the complainant suffered increased depression and PTSD,
experiencing suicidal thoughts, sadness, irritability, agitation, paranoia, sleep
disturbances, lack of energy, and loss of interest in spending time with
family); Jill M. v. Dep’t of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 2021000550 (July 15,
2021) (awarding $30,000 where the complainant suffered anxiety,
depression, sleep disturbances, lowered self-confidence, weight gain and
other physical changes due to distress over the agency’s harassment);
Thaddeus G. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2019004733 (Sept. 29,
2020) (awarding $30,000 where the complainant established that he suffered
emotional harm and other problems including damage to professional standing
and reputation, increased anxiety and panic for which he was prescribed
medication, depression, stress, sleep disturbances, shortness of breath, and
marital problems).

Operations (OFO) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this
decision. If the party requesting reconsideration elects to file a statement or
brief in support of the request, that statement or brief must be filed
together with the request for reconsideration. A party shall have
twenty (20) calendar days from receipt of another party’s request for
reconsideration within which to submit a brief or statement in opposition. See
29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive
for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit their request for reconsideration, and any
statement or brief in support of their request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which
can be found at

filed after the deadline only in very limited circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. §
1614.604(f).

         COMPLAINANT’S RIGHT TO FILE A CIVIL ACTION (T0124)

This decision requires the Agency to continue its administrative processing of
your complaint. You have the right to file a civil action in an appropriate
United States District Court within ninety (90) calendar days from the date
that you receive this decision on both that portion of your complaint which the
Commission has affirmed and that portion of the complaint which has been
remanded for continued administrative processing. In the alternative, you
may file a civil action after one hundred and eighty (180) calendar days
of the date you filed your complaint with the Agency, or your appeal with the
Commission, until such time as the Agency issues its final decision on your
complaint. If you file a civil action, you must name as the defendant in the
complaint the person who is the official Agency head or department head,
identifying that person by their full name and official title. Failure to do so
may result in the dismissal of your case in court. “Agency” or “department”
means the national organization, and not the local office, facility or
department in which you work. If you file a request to reconsider and also file
a civil action, filing a civil action will terminate the administrative
processing of your complaint.

                   RIGHT TO REQUEST COUNSEL (Z0815)

If you want to file a civil action but cannot pay the fees, costs, or security to
do so, you may request permission from the court to proceed with the civil
action without paying these fees or costs. Similarly, if you cannot afford an
attorney to represent you in the civil action, you may request the court to
appoint an attorney for you. You must submit the requests for waiver of
court costs or appointment of an attorney directly to the court, not
the Commission. The court has the sole discretion to grant or deny these
types of requests. Such requests do not alter the time limits for filing a civil
action (please read the paragraph titled Complainant’s Right to File a Civil
Action for the specific time limits).
