                                DECISION

On January 7, 2024, Complainant filed an appeal, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. §
1614.403(a), from the Agency’s December 8, 2023 final decision concerning
the Agency's award of compensatory damages. For the reasons below, we
AFFIRM the Agency’s award of compensatory damages.

                            ISSUE PRESENTED

The issue presented before the Commission is whether Complainant has
shown that he is entitled to an increase in the Agency's award of
compensatory damages.

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a
Rigger/Material Equipment Operator, WG-10, at the Pearl Harbor Naval

Shipyard & Intermediate Maintenance Facility, located in Pearl Harbor,

The record reflects the following chronology of events.

Complainant alleged that he was subjected to race, national origin, and
reprisal discrimination between June and December of 2020, in connection
with training and selection for a WG-11 Crane Operator position.
Complainant alleged that his Branch Head gave him bad advice concerning
his application for the promotion, causing him not to make the Certificate of
Eligibles. Complainant further claimed that he was subsequently told he
would have to accept a WG-09 position in order to be considered for a WG-
11 Crane Operator position but that his WG-10 pay would not be saved.
Complainant also alleged that at various points his work on cranes was
restricted and eventually eliminated. In December of 2020, Complainant was
charged with being absent without leave (AWOL) for a date in October.

Following the conclusion of the investigation of his December 30, 2020
complaint, Complainant requested a final agency decision on the merits of
the complaint rather than requesting a hearing before an Administrative
Judge appointed by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC).
The requested final agency decision was issued on August 17, 2021, and
found that the Agency did not discriminate or retaliate against Complainant.
Complainant timely appealed the final agency decision to the EEOC. On
August 17, 2023, the Commission issued its decision in EEOC Appeal No.
2021004613, finding that the Agency had not discriminated based on race or
national origin but had subjected Complainant to reprisal, both with respect
to offering Complainant the lower graded position and marking him as
AWOL. Among other remedies, the Agency was to give Complainant an
opportunity to submit evidence in support of a possible entitlement to an
award of compensatory damages and to issue a final agency decision on that
subject. The record reflects that the report of the investigation of
Complainant's compensatory damages claim was submitted on October 10,
2023. The Agency subsequently issued a final decision.

In its final decision, the Agency noted that Complainant claims an
unspecified amount for pecuniary losses and $250,000.00 for non-pecuniary
losses. In support of the claim, Complainant submitted no documentary
evidence in support of his pecuniary losses. Complainant does submit his
affidavit and statements from his spouse, sister and mother in support of his
claim for non-pecuniary damages.

With respect to pecuniary losses, the Agency denied Complainant’s request.
In so doing, the Agency noted that Complainant did not submit testimony or
evidence requesting pecuniary compensatory damages. While his sister
references using the award of compensatory damages to pay for medical
and psychological therapy expenses, the record does not include any
evidence that Complainant has incurred any medical expenses associated
with the Agency's retaliation. The documentation submitted by Complainant
shows that the last treatment with a psychiatrist was in March of 2021. In
any event, the record does not include any evidence of payments to medical
providers or bills for medical treatments. Complainant mentions that he was
prescribed omeprazole (Prilosec) for his acid reflux and sertraline (Zoloft) for
depression and identifies his costs for a refill of each prescription but does
not present any receipts for out-of-pocket expenses for these medications.
The Agency stated that it would not speculate on those expenses based on
the scant information provided by Complainant. Complainant claims to have
borrowed money from his sister but does not present any evidence of the
amount borrowed, when that occurred, why the loan was required, or
whether he was obligated to pay interest. In addition, Complainant alleges
that he took a loan of $43,000.00 from his Thrift Savings Program (TSP)
retirement account. Once again, he does not present any evidence to show if
or when that occurred. If borrowing from his retirement account was
intended to cover normal living expenses, that would be remedied as part of
Complainant's back pay award. There is no evidence that any of
Complainant's financial difficulties are attributable to anything at work.
Complainant also references that he used leave to avoid having to report for
work at the shipyard during the time period covered by this complaint. An
award of compensatory damages does not include leave restoration.
Moreover, EEOC did not award leave restoration in its August 17, 2023
decision. The Agency found that based on the minimal evidence submitted
for the record, no award of pecuniary compensatory damages is warranted.

Concerning his claim for non-pecuniary losses, the Agency noted that to the
extent that Complainant and one of his witnesses are claiming that pursuing
his EEO claims has caused him aggravation and emotional distress, the
Commission has consistently held that an employee cannot recover
compensatory damages for emotional distress allegedly endured as a result
of participating in the EEO process. It bears mentioning that Complainant
first sought psychological counseling on December 21, 2020, approximately
one month after his initial interview with the EEO Counselor and one week
before he filed his formal complaint of discrimination.

Complainant's wife indicates that Complainant believed his colleagues
avoided him after he chose to file his complaint, and the day they received
the Agency's final decision was "one of the worst days for us as a family."
Again, the Agency maintains that any effects arguably attributable to
Complainant's participation in the EEO complaint process are not
compensable. Complainant, his wife, and his sister all state that Complainant
suffered financial distress and implied that it was related to the Agency's
discrimination. Complainant states in his testimony that his financial issues
began in 2019. However, the retaliation for which the Agency is liable did
not begin until June 2020. Complainant notes that he was unable to keep up
with rent payments “multiple times” between 2019 and 2023, used his credit
cards to their limits, borrowed from his sister, and took out a $43,000.00
loan from his TSP retirement account. There is no evidence to connect the
financial straits such as Complainant described to events occurring between
June and December of 2020, none of which changed his grade and pay. In
fact, Complainant told his General Foreman in March 2020 that he was
expecting a child in the fall and that he was in financial peril. Complainant
told him how much the promotion would help his family immensely. At all
times relevant to this complaint, Complainant was fully employed as a WG-
10 Rigger/Material Equipment Operator. Complainant's wife notes that they
had four children and a baby on the way and were "not doing well"
financially because they were $56,000.00 in debt. Complainant's sister
identifies as a contributory reason for the family's financial situation;
namely, Complainant's ex-wife's choice to seek, and presumably obtain,
greater child support payments. She also observes that Complainant's
overtime was reduced but fails to acknowledge that Complainant was
training as a Crane Operator, not functioning in his Rigger position. In short,
the Agency concluded there is no credible evidence that Complainant's
inability to make ends meet between June and December of 2020 had
anything to do with his failure to obtain a promotion or related issues.

The Agency emphasized that it is only potentially liable for medical expenses
if its discrimination has caused the health problem or if its discrimination has
exacerbated a pre-existing condition, but only to the extent of the
exacerbation. Complainant acknowledges that his alopecia is a pre-existing
condition and claims that the Agency's discrimination caused clumps of his
hair to fall out between October of 2020 and the present. However, he
makes no attempt to establish to what extent the condition was exacerbated
by the Agency's retaliation. Since the issues in this complaint ended in
December of 2020, only hair loss that occurred within a reasonable time
following the events of December of 2020 would be relevant to this claim for
compensatory damages.

the visit "chest pains." The report lists diagnosed conditions as

pain, and diarrhea. However, these appear to be the conditions reported by
Complainant in his pre-existing medical records since this was a
telemedicine visit and some of the conditions develop over a long period of
time and/or would require laboratory tests to confirm their existence. The
next appointment, according to the Note, was a twenty-minute telephone
call with a Psychiatrist on December 1, 2020, after which the Psychiatrist
diagnosed Complainant with post-traumatic stress disorder. There are no
medical notes produced by Complainant indicating the source, cause, or time
period when symptoms of this medical condition began. Complainant did not
submit any evidence of medical visits/treatments between August 7, 2020
and December 1, 2020. The documents produced for the three medical visits
also indicate that Complainant suffers from post-traumatic stress disorder,
but the records contain no explanation how or why the medical providers
arrived at their conclusions, how the condition manifests itself, what
limitations or effects it imposes on Complainant, or any specific information
about the nature and severity of the condition. There is no medical or other
plausible evidence of severe emotional distress as claimed by Complainant.
The record does not include any bona fide evidence that Complainant was

attendance was any worse while the discrimination was occurring than it was
previously. Complainant and his wife note in their testimony that
Complainant's supervisor told him that he had to improve his attendance
and accrue some leave. Complainant and his wife both state that his

Complainant admits that they were seeing a marriage counselor between

Complainant's sister indicates that her first reaction was to think that her
brother's situation was related to his relationship with his "long-time
girlfriend." The family's financial condition, unrelated to the issues in this
complaint, was obviously a cause of distress. Complainant's mother states
that the Agency's treatment of her son caused tension in his marriage.
Complainant indicates that he believes that an award of $250,000.00 would
adequately compensate his emotional distress and the exacerbation of his
physical conditions. While Complainant mentions "conditions" (plural), the
evidence only references a single physical or mental condition that could
have been exacerbated, his alopecia. The amount requested by Complainant
is excessive and is more punitive than compensatory. Punitive damages are
not available in the federal sector EEO complaint process. The Agency
concluded that the case law supports an award of $25,000.00 due to the
superficial medical evidence submitted and the conclusory nature of the
complainant's and witnesses' testimony.

Additionally, the Agency noted that with respect to Complainant being
marked 1.3 hours AWOL in October 2020, in Israel F. v. United States Postal
Service, EEOC Appeal No. 2020001565 (March 2, 2021), the Commission
awarded a complainant $1500.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory damages
resulting from a one time, retaliatory charge of 8 hours of AWOL, where the
complainant asserted emotional harm from the Agency's discriminatory act
and evidence in the record provided a modicum of support for this assertion.

Accordingly, based on the nature and severity of emotional harm to
Complainant, the duration of the harm, the amount of damage awards in
similar cases, and the fact that only some of Complainant's emotional harm
was attributable to the Agency’s discriminatory actions, the Agency found
that an award of $26,500.00 is reasonable for Complainant’s proven non-
pecuniary loss in connection with the Agency’s actions.

                           STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29
C.F.R. § 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by
the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9, §
VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review
“requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the
factual and legal determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that
EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of record, including
any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its
decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its
interpretation of the law”).

                                 ANALYSIS

Compensatory Damages

When discrimination is found, the agency must provide the complainant with
a remedy that constitutes full, make-whole relief to restore him as nearly as
possible to the position he would have occupied absent the discrimination.
See, e.g., Franks v. Bowman Transp. Co., 424 U.S. 747, 764 (1976);
Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody, 422 U.S. 405, 418-19 (1975); Adesanya v.
U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01933395 (July 21, 1994). Pursuant to
section 102(a) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, a complainant who establishes
unlawful intentional discrimination under either Title VII of the Civil Rights
Act of 1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq. or Section
501 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29
U.S.C. § 791 et seq. may receive compensatory damages for past and future
pecuniary losses (i.e., out-of-pocket expenses) and non-pecuniary losses
(e.g., pain and suffering, mental anguish) as part of this "make whole"
relief. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). In West v. Gibson, 527 U.S. 212 (1999), the
Supreme Court held that Congress afforded the Commission the authority to
award compensatory damages in the administrative process. For an
employer with more than 500 employees, such as the agency, the limit of
liability for future pecuniary and non-pecuniary damages is $300,000. 42
U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3)
                                       8                          2024001719

To receive an award of compensatory damages, a complainant must
demonstrate that he or she has been harmed because of the agency’s
discriminatory action; the extent, nature, and severity of the harm; and the
duration or expected duration of the harm. Rivera v. Dep’t of the Navy,
EEOC Appeal No. 01934157 (July 22, 1994), req. for reconsideration denied,
EEOC Request No. 05940927 (Dec. 11, 1995); Compensatory and Punitive
Damages Available Under Section 102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, EEOC
Notice No. 915.002 (July 14, 1992), at 11-12, 14.

Pecuniary Damages

Pecuniary damages are quantifiable out-of-pocket expenses incurred
because of the Agency’s discriminatory actions. Damages for past pecuniary
damages will not normally be sought without documentation such as
receipts, records, bills, cancelled checks, or confirmation by other individuals
of actual loss and expenses. Compensatory and Punitive Damages available
Under Section 102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 (July 14, 1992) (available
at www.eeoc.gov.)

Based on the nominal evidence submitted for the record, no award of
pecuniary compensatory damages is warranted. Because the record reflects
that Complainant failed to present documentary evidence, including medical
bills or receipts to support his claimed medical expenses, we agree with the
Agency and find that Complainant is not entitled to reimbursement for these
expenses.

Non-pecuniary Damages

Non-pecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise
quantification, i.e., emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental
anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to professional standing, injury to
character and reputation, injury to credit standing, and loss of health. See
EEOC Notice No. 915.002 at 10 (July 14, 1992). There are no precise
fonrmulae for determining the amount of damages for nonpecuniary losses.
In Carle v. Department of the Navy, the Commission explained that
“objective evidence” of non-pecuniary damages could include a statement by
the complainant explaining how he or she was affected by the
discrimination. EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (January 5, 1993). Statements
from others, including family members, friends, and health care providers
could address the outward manifestations of the impact of the discrimination
on the complainant. Id.
                                       9                          2024001719

The complainant could also submit documentation of medical or psychiatric
treatment related to the effects of the discrimination. Id. Non-pecuniary
damages must be limited to the sums necessary to compensate the injured
party for the actual harm and should take into account the severity of the
harm and the length of the time the injured party has suffered from the
harm. See Carpenter v. Department of Agriculture, EEOC Appeal No.
01945652 (July 17, 1995).

Here, we acknowledge that much of Complainant’s documentation refers to
harm which is simply not the result of the discriminatory actions that
occurred from June to December 2020. However, the parties agree, and the
record reflects, a nexus between the Agency’s discriminatory conduct and
Complainant’s emotional harm. Even if Complainant had pre-existing
conditions, there is little doubt that the Agency’s actions aggravated those
conditions. Therefore, the Commission finds that an award of $25,000.00 for
non-pecuniary, compensatory damages is appropriate. This amount takes
into consideration the severity of the harm suffered and is consistent with
prior Commission precedent. See Mindy O. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs,
EEOC Appeal No. 0120160586 (May 31, 2017) (awarding $25,000 in non-
pecuniary compensatory damages based on complainant’s assertion that she
suffered emotional distress, anxiety, panic attacks, high blood pressure,
withdrawal from social actives, lack of trust and martial problems and
medical records supported that complainant experienced increased blood
pressure and exacerbation of medical conditions); Martina S. v. Dep’t of
Homeland Security, EEOC Appeal No. 0120160001 (January 24, 2017)
($20,000 in non-pecuniary damages awarded where complainant and
daughter provided testimony that Complainant experienced familial strain,
stress, anger, frustration, sleep issues, eating issues, and ultimately, an
apparent heart attack, due at least in part to the Agency's non-selection of
her); Reid v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0720070077
(November 13, 2009) ($20,000 in non-pecuniary damages awarded where
complainant suffered damage to her professional reputation and emotional
distress that affected her family due to discriminatory non-selections); and
Flowers v. U.S. Postal Service, EEOC Appeal No. 01A43114 (October 7,
2004), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request No. 05A50243 (January 11,
2005) ($20,000 in non-pecuniary damages awarded where the agency’s
failure to hire complainant resulted in sleeplessness, depression, emotional
distress, anxiety, loss of enjoyment of life and strained family relationships).
The Commission finds that $25,000 is not “monstrously excessive” standing
alone, and is not the product of passion or prejudice.
                                      10                          2024001719

Also, we concur with the Agency’s assessment above regarding Complainant
being marked 1.3 hours AWOL in October 2020, and find that an award of
$1500.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory damages resulting from a one-
time, retaliatory charge of 8 hours of AWOL is appropriate.

After a review of the record in its entirety, including consideration of all
statements submitted on appeal, it is the decision of the Equal Employment
Opportunity Commission to affirm the Agency’s final decision awarding
Complainant $26,500 in non-pecuniary damages.
