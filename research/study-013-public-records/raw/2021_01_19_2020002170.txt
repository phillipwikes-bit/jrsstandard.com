                                          DECISION

Complainant filed a timely appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC
or Commission) from an Agency final decision, dated January 8, 2020, finding that it was in
compliance with the terms of a September 16, 2009 settlement agreement. The Commission
accepts the appeal. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.402; 29 C.F.R. § 1614.504(b); and 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405.

                                       BACKGROUND

During the relevant time, Complainant worked as an Information Technology Specialist, GS-2210-
14, for the Agency’s Office of the Chief Information Officer (OCIO), located in Washington D.C.
Believing that she was subjected to discrimination based on disability and in reprisal for prior
protected EEO activity, Complainant filed a formal EEO complaint on March 3, 2008.

The parties resolved the matter, on September 16, 2009, by executing a settlement agreement. The
settlement agreement provided, in pertinent part:

              described in Exhibit A, which is attached hereto. The Agency’s obligation
              to allow Complainant to work at home full time shall continue until one of
              the following events occurs: (1) Complainant no longer meets the definition

By letter to the Agency dated February 28, 2019, Complainant alleged breach. Specifically,
Complainant alleged that agreement was violated when the Agency reassigned her to a new
position with a different position description (PD) on February 14, 2019.

On March 18, 2019 the Agency issued a decision finding that Complainant’s breach allegation was
untimely raised. The Agency determined that Complainant “has not operated under the position
description in Exhibit A since at least 2015.” In support of its determination, the Agency provided
a copy of the September 2015 PD sent to Complainant, as well as her October 5, 2015 response.
The Agency reasoned that Complainant’s allegation was well beyond the thirty-day time
limitation. Complainant appealed the decision to the Commission.

In requesting that we affirm its decision, the Agency argued that as far back as June 2012, it
provided Complainant with a new position description for the OCIO Office of Planning and
Technology Assets Management. Then, in 2015, the Agency gave her another position description
for the OICIO Division of Enterprise Architecture and Policy Branch. The Agency determined
that by accepting these reassignments and not timely alleging breach, Complainant waived her
right to claim the agreement was breached. Even assuming arguendo that Complainant timely
alleged breach, the Agency asserted that Complainant has always worked in the IT Specialist, GS-
2210-14.

Complainant countered by asserting that it was not until 2019, that her position description was
changed, and the agreement breached. Complainant argued that she opposed the 2012 and 2015
realignments but that those actions did not result in a change to her duties or position descriptions.

In the prior decision, as an initial matter, the Commission found that the Agency did not support
its assertion that Complainant’s allegation was untimely. See Complainant v. Small Business
Administration, EEOC Appeal No. 2019003434 (Sept. 27, 2019). There was no evidence showing
that, prior to 2019, Complainant was “officially placed under or working under a different PD.”
See id. Turning to the merits of the breach allegation, the Commission found that the record was
unclear on how Complainant’s PD changed in 2019. See id. The record did not include the 2019
PD, nor did Complainant specifically describe how her duties and responsibilities were altered.
See id. Consequently, the Agency’s determination that it was in compliance with the agreement
was vacated and the matter was remanded for a supplemental investigation. See id. The Agency
was ordered to obtain evidence regarding the PD for the 2019 reassignment and determine how it
differs from the PD referenced in the 2009 agreement. See id.

On January 8, 2020, in compliance with the Commission’s order, the Agency issued a new decision
following its supplemental investigation. The Agency again focused on the timeliness of
Complainant’s breach claim. According to the Agency, Complainant had not worked under the
Exhibit A position description referenced in the settlement since 2015. Further, it noted that
Complainant admitted to performing enterprise architecture duties at that time, until she requested
a transfer in 2018. While acknowledging that it failed to formally issue an SF-50 for the
reassignment, the Agency concluded that Complainant had not been performing the 2009 PD
duties for years (2015-2018).

Complainant filed the instant appeal. It is her belief that the Commission’s prior decision
concluded that her allegation was timely, and the case was remanded for investigation and a new
decision on the merits. Instead, Complainant argues, the Agency “disregarded those instructions”
and again found the allegation of breach to be untimely. Complainant contends that because the
Agency did not request reconsideration of the Commission’s earlier decision, it is prohibited from
re-arguing the timeliness issue 2. Complainant argues that, instead, the Agency was limited to
determining whether the 2019 PD was materially different from the 2009 PD. Complainant argues
further that when it did not do so, the Agency failed to dispute Complainant’s allegation that the
contract was violated with the issuance of the 2019 PD and the Commission should find breach.

                                            ANALYSIS

EEOC Regulation 29 C.F.R. § 1614.504(a) provides that any settlement agreement knowingly and
voluntarily agreed to by the parties, reached at any stage of the complaint process, shall be binding
on both parties. The Commission has held that a settlement agreement constitutes a contract
between the employee and the Agency, to which ordinary rules of contract construction apply. See
Herrington v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Request No. 05960032 (December 9, 1996).

The Commission has further held that it is the intent of the parties as expressed in the contract, not
some unexpressed intention, that controls the contract’s construction. Eggleston v. Dep’t of
Veterans Affairs, EEOC Request No. 05900795 (August 23, 1990). In ascertaining the intent of
the parties with regard to the terms of a settlement agreement, the Commission has generally relied
on the plain meaning rule. See Hyon O v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05910787
(December 2, 1991). This rule states that if the writing appears to be plain and unambiguous on
its face, its meaning must be determined from the four corners of the instrument without resort to
extrinsic evidence of any nature. See Montgomery Elevator Co. v. Building Eng’g Servs. Co., 730
F.2d 377 (5th Cir. 1984).

Regarding the timeliness of the breach allegation, we first must correct Complainant’s
misunderstanding that the previous decision expressly determined her allegation to be timely. The
Commission did not do so. Rather, the Commission found that the Agency did not support its
decision finding the allegation was untimely. See EEOC Appeal No. 2019003434 (Sept. 27, 2019).
However, Complainant accurately observed that the Agency failed to abide by the Commission’s
orders regarding the subject of the supplemental investigation and subsequent agency decision.

As noted above, the Agency was explicitly ordered to conduct a supplemental investigation into
whether or not it breached provision (7), by gathering documentary evidence to include the
following: a copy of the 2019 PD associated with Complainant’s reassignment, an affidavit from
Complainant detailing the differences between the 2009 PD and 2019 PD, and affidavits from
management officials with information regarding any distinctions between the two PDs. See id.
Thereafter, the Agency was required to “issue a new final decision regarding whether it is in
compliance with provision (7) of the September 2009 settlement agreement, with appeal rights to the
Commission.” See id.

Instead, the Agency’s January 8, 2020 decision states that Complainant has not worked under the
agreed 2009 PD since 2015 and references the time frame for alleging breach set out in provision (9).
Rather than expressly state that it again finds the allegation of breach is untimely, the Agency instead
simply concludes that provision (7) has not been violated. Additionally, it notes that there is no
indication of bad faith. Nowhere in the decision does the Agency describe the 2009 PD and 2019 PD
or compare the job skills and duties set forth in each.

In response to the instant appeal, the Agency presents conflicting arguments. On the one hand, the
Agency argues the agreement was not violated because the supplemental investigation found that
Complainant has performed duties under three different position descriptions since the 2009 agreement
(2012, 2015 and 2019). This argument does not support the contention that no breach occurred, but
rather that breaches transpired years ago and so Complainant is now foreclosed from seeking
resolution. Yet the Agency fails to plainly reference timeliness, and instead conflates timeliness with
a determination on the merits of the breach allegation.

On the other hand, the Agency contends that “Complainant has always remained under the position
description of GS-2210-12 IT Specialist”. The Agency states that in 2012 and 2015 Complainant
worked the same job, but was moved to different offices. The Agency in essence argues that there was
no violation of the agreement in 2012 and 2015.
                                                5                                    2020002170

This undercuts the Agency’s timeliness argument, because if there was no breach there was no cause
for Complainant to allege a violation of the settlement at those earlier times.

As noted above, the Agency was ordered to compare the settlement PD with the 2019 PD, not
2012 or 2015. It did not do so in its January 8, 2020 decision, but does so for the first time in
response to Complainant’s appeal. According to the Agency, while Complainant refers to her
2009 PD as a “Project Manager” position, she is not an “IT Project Manager” and has always
maintained the position of “IT Specialist”. Similarly, the 2019 position is not one of a
“Cybersecurity Specialist,” and the reference to cybersecurity in 2019 PD is not dispositive, but
simply reflects updates to laws and policies. The Agency explains that the IT Specialist position,
is broadly defined by necessity “due to the ever-evolving needs of the federal information
technology workforce.” The Agency asserts that the position includes some project management
responsibilities, which are maintained in the 2019 PD. The Agency argues that Any differences
between the 2009 and 2019 PDs are superficial. Further, it contends that “Complainant confuses
specificity with material change.” The 2019 PD includes more detailed references to the Federal
Information Security Management Act of 2002 and includes references to cloud-based applications
that were not part of the IT infrastructure in 2009.

The Commission finds that the Agency has substantially complied with the September 16, 2009
settlement agreement. It was not until ten years later, in 2019, that Complainant alleged breach.
The Commission has held that in the absence of a specific time frame in a settlement agreement,
it is interpreted to be for a reasonable amount of time. Parker v. Dep't of Defense, EEOC Request
No. 05910576 (Aug. 29, 1991); Gomez v. Dep't of the Treasury, EEOC Request No. 05930921
(Feb. 10, 1994). For example, we have determined that an agency is not obligated to maintain
special schedules forever. Bax v. United States Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A31921 (Jan.
15, 2004) (finding no breach where a supervisor changed the scheduling system due to a decrease
in work volume, and as a result, changed the complainant's schedule, which had been established
as part of a settlement agreement over six years earlier.) We have also found that a managerial
decision to change existing standards in a way that conflicts with a pre-existing settlement
agreement is not necessarily a breach once a reasonable amount of time after the execution of a
settlement agreement has passed. Lasso v. United States Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
0120111023 (May 10, 2011) (finding no breach of a settlement provision requiring the agency to
place complainant's mail case closest to the door, when, two and a half years after entering the
agreement, the postmaster moved the mail case away from the door as part of an office
reorganization). Therefore, the Agency’s determination that it was in compliance with the
agreement was proper.

                                        CONCLUSION

The Agency’s decision finding no breach of the subject settlement agreement is AFFIRMED.

                            STATEMENT OF RIGHTS - ON APPEAL

                                  RECONSIDERATION (M0617)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider the decision in this case if the Complainant or
the Agency submits a written request containing arguments or evidence which tend to establish
that:

       1.     The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or
               law; or

       2.     The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies, practices, or
               operations of the Agency.

Requests to reconsider, with supporting statement or brief, must be filed with the Office of Federal
Operations (OFO) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. A party shall have
twenty (20) calendar days of receipt of another party’s timely request for reconsideration in
which to submit a brief or statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B
(Aug. 5, 2015). All requests and arguments must be submitted to the Director, Office of Federal
Operations, Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Complainant’s request may be
submitted via regular mail to P.O. Box 77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail to 131
M Street, NE, Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, the request to
reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if it is received by mail within five days of the expiration
of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604. The agency’s request must be submitted
in digital format via the EEOC’s Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. §
1614.403(g). The request or opposition must also include proof of service on the other party.

Failure to file within the time period will result in dismissal of your request for reconsideration as
untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the timely filing of the request. Any
supporting documentation must be submitted with your request for reconsideration. The
Commission will consider requests for reconsideration filed after the deadline only in very limited
circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(c).

If you want to file a civil action but cannot pay the fees, costs, or security to do so, you may request
