                                            DECISION

On January 23, 2022, Complainant filed an appeal, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the
Agency’s January 6, 2022 final decision concerning an equal employment opportunity (EEO)
complaint claiming employment discrimination in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of
1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act of
1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 791 et seq., the Age Discrimination in
Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq., and the Equal Pay Act of
1963, as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 206(d) et seq.

                                         BACKGROUND

During the relevant period, Complainant worked as a Senior Federal Air Marshal (FAM), SV-
1801-I-00, for the Federal Air Marshal Service at the Agency’s Field Office in Charlotte, North
Carolina.

There is an extensive procedural history leading up to the issuance of the instant formal complaint.
A recitation of the salient points of that history may be instructive prior to addressing the instant
matter.

On January 27, 2014, Complainant initiated contact with an Equal Employment Opportunity

EPA

The U.S. Supreme Court articulated the requirements for establishing a prima facie case of
discrimination under the EPA in Corning Glass Works v. Brennan, 417 U.S. 188, 195 (1974). To
establish a violation of the EPA, Complainant must show that he received less pay than a female
employee for equal work, requiring equal skill, effort and responsibility, under similar working
conditions within the same establishment. Id. at 195; Sheppard v. Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission, EEOC Appeal No. 01A02919 (Sept. 12, 2000); see also 29 C.F.R. § 1620.14(a).

Once Complainant has met this burden of establishing a prima facie case, the Agency may avoid
liability only if it can prove that the pay difference is justified under one of the four affirmative
defenses set forth in the EPA, namely: 1) a seniority system; (2) a merit system; (3) a system which
measure earnings by quantity or quality of productions of work (also referred to an incentive or
piecework system); or 4) a differential based on any other factor other than sex, 299 U.S.C. §
206(d)(1); Corning Glass Works, 417 U.S. at 196-97; Kouba v. Allstate Insur. Co., 691 F.2d 873
(9th Cir. 1982).


Complainant identified three female comparators (FAM1, FAM2, and FAM3) who he asserts
performed the same duties as he did but received a higher salary. FAM1 was a Caucasian female
over the age of 40; FAM2 was a Caucasian male over the age of 40; and FAM3 was an African
American female over the age of 40. However, even assuming Complainant could establish a
prima facie case with regard to FAM1 and FAM3, the evidence shows the pay difference for these
comparators was based on a factor other than sex.

Complainant and the alleged comparators had all originally been hired as FAMS by the
Department of Transportation’s Federal Aviation Authority (FAA). However, in November 2002,
the entire FAM division was transferred from the FAA to the Transportation Security
Administration (TSA) under the newly created Department of Homeland Security. At the time of
the transfer, TSA maintained their FAM salaries as established by FAA. FAA established FAM
salaries based on each employee’s salaries prior to joining FAA. As a result, Complainant’s
starting salary with TSA was lower than the alleged comparators because Complainant’s salary
prior to joining TSA was significantly lower than the alleged comparators.

Prior to joining FAA, Complainant was a Detention Enforcement Officer, grade 7, with the
Department of Justice at an annual salary of $38,758. Complainant joined FAA with an annual
salary of $40,740, before converting to TSA with an annual salary of $50,925.

In contrast, FAM1’s highest grade prior to joining FAA was as a Criminal Investigator, grade 12,
with the U.S. Immigration and Naturalization Service at an annual salary of $67,031.
                                               4                                       2022001484

This was almost $30,000 more than Complainant’s prior salary. FAM1 joined FAA with an annual
salary of $70,616. Likewise, prior to joining FAA, FAM3 was an Officer, LE-0083-01/06, with
the U.S. Secret Service at an annual salary for $50,737. FAM3 joined FAA with an annual salary
of $70,073. 2

Complainant and the comparators all received subsequent promotions resulting in increased pay
that was proportional to there salaries at the time. By 2014, Complainant was making $89,479. In
2014, FAM1’s salary was $114,916. By 2014, FAM3’s salary was $112,929.

In sum, the evidence clearly establishes that any pay disparity between Complainant and the female
comparators was the result of their beginning employment at the FAA at different pay levels based
on their prior salaries with other employers. This is the established reason for the disparity other
than sex. In making this finding, we also note that there has been no evidence presented, or even
argument made, that Complainant or the comparators’ prior salary history before coming to
FAA/TSA was the result of institutional sex discrimination.

Finally, we note that Complainant also argued that the granting of ISIs between 2010-2013 also
resulted in at least some of the pay disparity and was discriminatory. IPIs acknowledge special
circumstances, such as an employee’s significant professional growth, and are granted at the sole
discretion of an appropriately designated management official. In essence, they are awards based
on a percentage of the recipient’s current salary. To be eligible, an employee must be performing
at a minimum level of Achieved Expectations and must not have received an IPI within the
preceding 52-week period or been a subject of disciplinary action within the last two years. In
2012 and 2013, Complainant and the two female comparators received the same percentage ISIs
(1.2% in 2012 and 1% in 2013).

We determine that Complainant has not demonstrated a violation of the EPA.

Disparate Treatment

A claim of disparate treatment is examined under the three-part analysis first enunciated in
McDonnell Douglas Corporation v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). For complainant to prevail, she
must first establish a prima facie of discrimination by presenting facts that, if unexplained,
reasonably give rise to an inference of discrimination, i.e., that a prohibited consideration was a
factor in the adverse employment action. See McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802; Furnco
Construction Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567 (1978). The burden then shifts to the agency to
articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. See Texas Department of
Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981).

Once the agency has met its burden, the complainant bears the ultimate responsibility to persuade
the fact finder by a preponderance of the evidence that the agency acted on the basis of a prohibited
reason. See St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993).

This established order of analysis in discrimination cases, in which the first step normally consists
of determining the existence of a prima facie case, need not be followed in all cases. Where the
agency has articulated a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the personnel action at issue, the
factual inquiry can proceed directly to the third step of the McDonnell Douglas analysis, the
ultimate issue of whether complainant has shown by a preponderance of the evidence that the
agency’s actions were motivated by discrimination. See U.S. Postal Service Board of Governors
v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 713-714 (1983); Hernandez v. Department of Transportation, EEOC
Request No. 05900159 (June 28, 1990); Peterson v. Department of Health and Human Services,
EEOC Request No. 05900467 (June 8, 1990); Washington v. Department of the Navy, EEOC
Petition No. 03900056 (May 31, 1990).

As noted above, regarding the pay disparities between Complainant and the three named
comparators, the Agency provided legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons for its actions which
were not pretextual. Specifically, any pay disparities between Complainant and co-workers arose
from initial salary disparities when they entered FAMS with the FAA in 2002, which continued
with the transfer of FAMS to the Agency. Those disparities were due to each employee’s varied
federal experience prior to joining FAMS, and as the Agency noted, those “built-in” disparities
continued in subsequent years. The Agency also noted Complainant’s assertion that pay disparities
were further due to discriminatory IPI determinations made by an Agency supervisor from 2010-
2013. However, the record reflects that Complainant received substantially the same IPIs as other
comparators in 2012 and 2013.

Complainant failed to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the reasons proffered by the
Agency for the salary difference were a pretext masking discrimination or unlawful retaliation.

                                          CONCLUSION

We AFFIRM the Agency’s final decision because the preponderance of the evidence of record
does not establish that discrimination or unlawful retaliation occurred.

       1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or

       2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies, practices, or

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Operations (OFO)
within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party requesting reconsideration
elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request, that statement or brief must be filed
together with the request for reconsideration. A party shall have twenty (20) calendar days
from receipt of another party’s request for reconsideration within which to submit a brief or
statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment Opportunity Management
Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit his or her request for reconsideration, and any statement or brief in
support of his or her request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx

Alternatively, Complainant can submit his or her request and arguments to the Director, Office of
Federal Operations, Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed to
P.O. Box 77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street, NE,
Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s request to
reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFO receives it by mail within five days of the expiration
of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the EEOC’s
Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). Either party’s request and/or
statement or brief in opposition must also include proof of service on the other party, unless
Complainant files his or her request via the EEOC Public Portal, in which case no proof of service
is required.

Failure to file within the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s request for
reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the timely filing of the
request. Any supporting documentation must be submitted together with the request for
reconsideration. The Commission will consider requests for reconsideration filed after the
deadline only in very limited circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(c).
