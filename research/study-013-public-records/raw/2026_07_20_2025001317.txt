                                   DECISION

Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission
(EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
December 13, 2024, final decision concerning his equal employment opportunity
(EEO) complaint of employment discrimination in violation of Title VII of the Civil
Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq. For the
following reasons, the Commission AFFIRMS the Agency’s final decision.

                                BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a Senior
Officer Specialist (SOS) at the Agency’s Metropolitan Detention Center in Los
Angeles, California.

On April 10, 2023, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging that the Agency
discriminated against him. The Agency framed Complainant’s claims as follows:

   1. Whether the Agency is liable for subjecting Complainant to a hostile work
      environment based on his sex (sexual orientation) when on November 17,
      2022, Coworker 1 shouted at him, threatened him, struck him in the throat,
      and used homophobic slurs, and another co-worker (Coworker 2) repeated the
      homophobic slurs and laughed; and
   2. on November 17, 2022, and April 10, 2023, the Agency subjected
      Complainant to disparate treatment based on reprisal when management
      denied him continuation of pay (COP) benefits, coded him as being absent
      without leave (AWOL), conducted a welfare check, and requested additional
      medical documentation.
At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided Complainant with a
copy of the report of investigation and notice of his right to request a hearing before
an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). When Complainant did not request a hearing
within the time frame provided in 29 C.F.R. § 1614.108(f), the Agency issued a final
decision pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b), which concluded that Complainant
failed to prove that the Agency subjected him to discrimination or harassment as
alleged. Complainant appealed this decision to the Commission.

In Clement D. v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 2024003112 (Aug. 29, 2024),
the Commission remanded the case after finding that the Agency’s investigation was
inadequate because the record lacked evidence from 1) the Threat Assessment
Committee; and 2) the Office of Internal Affairs (OIA) investigation into the
November 17, 2022 alleged assault. The Commission explained that the missing
documentation could help determine whether the altercation and alleged slurs were
motivated by discriminatory animus or merely stemmed from a workplace dispute.
The Commission noted that several management officials claimed Complainant
never contemporaneously reported homophobic slurs, while Complainant insisted
that he did in fact report such comments and that the withheld records would
corroborate his claims. The Commission ordered a supplemental investigation and
production of the missing records.

Following a supplemental investigation, the Agency issued a new final decision on
December 13, 2024, which also concluded that Complainant failed to prove that the
Agency subjected him to discrimination or harassment as alleged.
                                          3                              2025001317

Regarding claim 1, the Agency acknowledged that an assault at work occurred on
November 17, 2022. However, the Agency found that there was insufficient
evidence to establish that the assault was the result of Complainant’s protected class.
The Agency noted that neither Complainant’s contemporaneous reports to
management, the Threat Assessment Committee, law enforcement, nor his medical
providers included allegations that Coworker 1 or Coworker 2 used homophobic
slurs or comments during the incident. Further, both Coworker 1 and Coworker 2
denied making such remarks, denied knowledge of Complainant’s sexual
orientation, and no corroborating evidence established that either employee acted
because of Complainant's protected class. Rather, the Agency found that the
evidence reflected a work-related dispute arising from disagreements concerning
workplace duties and reporting responsibilities. The Agency further found that even
assuming the conduct occurred as alleged, liability could not be imputed because
management responded promptly upon learning of the incident by interviewing
witnesses, conducting a threat assessment, issuing professionalism letters, and
referring the matter for further investigation. The Agency acknowledged that the
OIA investigation remained ongoing and emphasized that no completed OIA
investigative findings existed at the time of the supplemental investigation.
However, the Agency concluded that the existing record was sufficient to adjudicate
the merits.

Regarding claim 2, the Agency asserted that its actions were based on legitimate,
nondiscriminatory reasons. Regarding COP benefits, the Agency explained that
Complainant’s workers’ compensation claim was initially denied by OWCP on
January 9, 2023. Because OWCP denied the claim, management maintained that
Complainant was not entitled to COP benefits and was required to use available sick
and annual leave to cover his absences. The Agency further noted that entitlement
to COP is determined through the OWCP process, not by Agency officials.
Regarding AWOL coding, management stated that after Complainant exhausted his
available leave balances, Agency policy and Office of Personnel Management
regulations required management to code his absences as AWOL. While AWOL
codes were entered, Complainant was not disciplined or charged with misconduct
based on those codes. When OWCP later accepted Complainant’s claim on appeal
in August 2023, management changed the AWOL entries to leave without pay
(LWOP). As to additional medical documentation, the Agency asserted that
Complainant had been absent from work since November 2022, had provided
limited information concerning his medical condition, and had not consistently
communicated with management.
                                          4                              2025001317

Management stated that the Agency needed updated medical information to
determine whether Complainant could safely perform the essential functions of his
Correctional Officer position and whether any restrictions existed. Accordingly,
management sent multiple letters to Complainant and his healthcare provider
requesting clarification regarding his diagnosis, treatment plan, prognosis, duty
limitations, and ability to return to work.

The Agency denied that these requests constituted a fitness-for-duty examination or
were motivated by retaliation, contending instead that they were necessary to assess
Complainant's ability to perform his job after a lengthy absence. Regarding the
welfare check, this was initiated pursuant to standard procedure after management
was unable to contact Complainant. Based upon the record, the Agency concluded
that Complainant failed to establish discrimination or harassment as alleged. The
instant appeal followed.

                          CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

On appeal, Complainant argues that the Agency's second final decision, issued after
remand, failed to substantially comply with the Commission’s prior order because it
was largely identical to the original final decision and did not meaningfully address
or analyze the critical Threat Assessment Committee and OIA evidence.
Complainant contends that the Agency continues to withhold essential evidence,
failed to obtain testimony from key witnesses, and deprived him of a meaningful
opportunity to challenge the Agency’s explanations. He further asserts that the
Agency improperly attacks the validity of an OWCP determination. Complainant
also challenges the credibility of Agency officials, points to alleged inconsistencies
in the Agency's evidence and witness statements, and questions whether any genuine
OIA investigation occurred. Based on these alleged procedural and evidentiary
deficiencies, Complainant requests that the Commission reject the revised final
decision, find discrimination, and award appropriate remedies and attorney's fees.

The Agency did not submit a response.

                            STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the
Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).

See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614,
at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review

assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).

                                    ANALYSIS

As a preliminary matter, the Commission has carefully considered Complainant’s
arguments that the Agency failed to comply with the prior remand order and that the
absence of a completed Office of Internal Affairs (OIA) investigation precludes a
merits determination. We disagree. The prior appellate decision ordered additional
development of the record because documentation from the Threat Assessment
Committee and OIA investigation could potentially shed further light on the
circumstances surrounding the November 17, 2022, incident. However, the remand
order did not conclude that any missing documentation would necessarily
corroborate Complainant’s allegations of discriminatory animus. Following remand,
the Agency produced the Threat Assessment Committee materials and explained that
the OIA investigation remained pending and therefore no completed investigative
report existed. The Commission recognizes that the absence of a final OIA report is
not ideal. Nevertheless, administrative adjudication does not require the
development of every conceivable piece of evidence where the existing record is
sufficient to permit a reasoned determination. After reviewing the supplemental
record, including the Threat Assessment documentation, witness statements,
management affidavits, and the parties’ submissions, we find that the evidentiary
record is adequate to resolve the claims presented.

Harassment

In order to establish a prima facie case of harassment, Complainant must prove, by
a preponderance of the evidence, the existence of five elements: (1) that he is a
member of a statutorily protected class; (2) that he was subjected to unwelcome
conduct related to his protected class; (3) that the harassment complained of was
based on his protected class; (4) that the harassment had the purpose or effect of
unreasonably interfering with his work performance and/or creating an intimidating,
hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) that there is a basis for imputing
                                         6                              2025001317

liability to the employer. See Celine B. v. Dep’t of Navy, EEOC Appeal No.
2019001961 (Sept. 21, 2020)

In cases of coworker harassment, the Agency is responsible for harassment in the
workplace where it (or its agents) knew or should have known of the conduct, unless
it can be shown that it took immediate and appropriate corrective action. The Agency
can raise an affirmative defense when it shows that it took immediate and appropriate
corrective action. What is appropriate remedial action will necessarily depend on the
particular facts of the case, such as the severity and persistence of the harassment
and the effectiveness of any initial remedial steps. Taylor v. Dep’t Of Air Force,
EEOC Request No. 05920194 (July 8, 1992).

Based on the record, the Commission finds that Complainant failed to establish
Agency liability for hostile work environment harassment. The Commission does
not dispute that a workplace confrontation occurred. However, the record contains
ample evidence indicating that the dispute arose from disagreements concerning
workplace duties, staffing assignments, and reporting responsibilities. Although
Complainant maintains that homophobic slurs were used during the incident, the
record reflects that the alleged slurs were not documented in several
contemporaneous reports generated immediately after the event, including reports
provided to management and other officials responsible for reviewing the incident.
In addition, the employees accused of making such remarks denied doing so and
denied knowledge of Complainant’s sexual orientation. Based on the record, the
evidence regarding discriminatory motive is conflicting and does not weigh
sufficiently in Complainant’s favor to satisfy his burden to establish, by a
preponderance of the evidence, that the challenged conduct was motivated by a
protected class.

Moreover, even assuming, arguendo, that the alleged comments occurred, the
Commission finds insufficient evidence to impute liability to the Agency. Here, the
record demonstrates that management responded promptly upon learning of the
incident. Management separated the employees involved, documented the
allegations, photographed Complainant’s injury, convened a Threat Assessment
Committee, obtained witness statements, and undertook additional investigative and
corrective measures. Title VII does not impose strict liability upon an agency
whenever coworker misconduct occurs. Rather, an employer is liable where it knew
or should have known of the alleged harassment and failed to take prompt and
appropriate corrective action.
                                        7                             2025001317

Here, the record reflects that management acted immediately and took measures
reasonably calculated to address the reported misconduct. Accordingly,
Complainant has not established a basis for imputing liability to the Agency.

Reprisal

To the extent Complainant alleges that he was subjected to reprisal, the Commission
finds that the Agency articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for each
action and that Complainant has not demonstrated those reasons were pretextual.
The record reflects that management's decisions regarding continuation of pay
benefits, leave coding, requests for medical information, and welfare checks were
tied to OWCP determinations, leave administration requirements, workplace safety
concerns, and management’s inability to contact Complainant during an extended
absence. Although Complainant disagrees with those actions and believes they were
retaliatory, the Commission’s role is not to determine whether management’s
decisions were correct, prudent, or ideal, but whether they were motivated by
retaliatory animus. The record contains insufficient evidence from which a
reasonable factfinder could conclude that retaliation, rather than the explanations
articulated by management, motivated the challenged actions. Accordingly,
Complainant has failed to establish reprisal.

                                 CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions raised on appeal,
including those not specifically addressed herein, the Commission concludes that
Complainant failed to establish that the Agency subjected him to discrimination as
alleged. Accordingly, the Agency’s final decision is AFFIRMED.

                       RECONSIDERATION (M1125)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if
Complainant or the Agency submits a written request that contains arguments or
evidence that tend to establish that:
      1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of
         material fact or law; or
                                           8                               2025001317

      2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies,
         practices, or operations of the agency.

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Sector
(OFS) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party
requesting reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request,
that statement or brief must be filed together with the request for
reconsideration. A party shall have twenty (20) calendar days from receipt of
another party’s request for reconsideration within which to submit a brief or
statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B
(Aug. 5, 2015).
Complainant should submit their request for reconsideration, and any statement or
brief in support of their request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx. Alternatively, Complainant can
submit their request and arguments to the Director, Office of Federal Sector, Equal
Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed to P.O. Box
77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street, NE,
Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s
request to reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFS receives it by mail within
five days of the expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the
EEOC’s Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). Either
party’s request and/or statement or brief in opposition must also include proof of
service on the other party, unless Complainant files their request via the EEOC
Public Portal, in which case no proof of service is required. Failure to file within
the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s request for
reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the
timely filing of the request. Any supporting documentation must be submitted
together with the request for reconsideration. The Commission will consider
requests for reconsideration filed after the deadline only in very limited
circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(f).
