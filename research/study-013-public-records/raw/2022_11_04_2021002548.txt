                                          DECISION

On March 18, 2021, Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
February 16, 2021, final decision concerning his equal employment opportunity (EEO) complaint
alleging employment discrimination in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964
(Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq. For the following reasons, the Commission
AFFIRMS the Agency’s final decision.

                                       BACKGROUND

In 2010, Complainant’s then supervisor assigned him the collateral duties of Controlled
Substance Coordinator (CSC). He believed the duties were temporary, and he performed them
for six years. Report of Investigation (ROI) 1 at 126, 129, and 208.

According to the Medical Center Director, Complainant’s second-line supervisor (Supervisor 2),
during that six-year period, Complainant did not request overtime to perform any of the assigned
duties; nor did Complainant’s overall work product suggest that he worked additional hours each

The Agency consolidated both complaints and accepted them for investigation. Of the 14
allegations, the Agency accepted claims 3, 4, and 8 as independently actionable claims. The
Agency accepted the remaining claims only as claims of harassment/hostile work environment.
At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided Complainant with a copy of the
report of investigation and notice of his right to request a hearing before an Equal Employment
Opportunity Commission Administrative Judge (AJ). Complainant timely requested a hearing
but subsequently withdrew his request. Consequently, the Agency issued a final decision (FAD)
pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b). The Agency concluded that Complainant failed to prove
that the Agency subjected him to discrimination as alleged.

In the FAD, the Agency addressed Complainant’s disparate treatment claims under the three-
part evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green,
411 U.S. 792 (1973). The Agency subsequently determined that management had provided
legitimate nondiscriminatory reasons for the alleged actions. Regarding claims 3 and 4,
Supervisors 1 and 2 asserted that Complainant did not receive additional compensation for the CSC
duties because they were collateral duties; and because he did not request overtime as required
under agency policy; nor did the volume of his work suggest he was working more than 40 hours
a week. ROI 1 at 165, 169, and 196-98.

The Agency also found that Complainant made changes to the CSC role, which increased the
amount of time required. Moreover, included in the record are emails in which Complainant
pushed back on Supervisor 1’s efforts to streamline the CSC duties and assign them to other
employees. ROI 1 at 264-68.

Regarding claim 8, the Agency found that there was no evidence in the record to suggest that
the COR duties assigned to Complainant were overly burdensome or that Complainant was not
capable of performing them. According to the Agency, throughout a facility there are dozens of
individuals assigned to various contracts as CORs at any given time, and such employees are of
several different races and genders. All of these employees perform the COR duties as collateral
duties; and none of the employees performing COR duties are provided with differential pay for
doing so.

The Agency analyzed Complainant’s remaining claims under the legal standard for harassment.
The Agency ultimately determined that Complainant could not prevail on claims 1, 5, and 6
because his allegations were insufficiently severe or persuasive to rise to the level of a hostile
work environment given the Agency articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for the
alleged actions in claims 3, 4, and 8.

The Agency also determined that management either denied Complainant’s remaining alleged
incidents of harassment, that those incidents did not occur or that they did not occur as
Complainant described them. Regarding claim 2, Complainant admitted he never bothered to
apply for the training opportunities he alleged he missed out on. ROI 1 at 128 and 136-38, and
SROI at 46-8.

Regarding claim 7, Complainant’s position was not removed from the ECF position list. Rather,
a named HR employee stated that Complainant did not receive appropriate performance pay for
fiscal year 2010-2014 due to administrative errors that were corrected in the first pay period
after the appropriate amount was calculated; and Complainant was reimbursed for the unpaid
amounts. ROI 1 at 148-49 and 253-54.

Regarding claims 13 and 14, Complainant attempted to receive workers’ compensation
(Workers’ Comp) benefits. Complainant provided Supervisor 1 with a work status note. The
work status note contained the name of Complainant’s private psychiatrist. Supervisor 1 later
learned of the identity of Complainant’s worker’s comp psychiatrist verbally from his Workers’
Comp Case Manager (Case Manager) while seeking status on a return to work plan for
Complainant in order to ensure ongoing staffing coverage of his duties and workload. Supervisor
1 knew both psychiatrists and accordingly became aware that the Worker’s Comp psychiatrist
was a subordinate of Complainant’s private psychiatrist. Supervisor 1 found this concerning as
she perceived it to be a conflict of interest. She raised her concern regarding the conflict with
Case Manager. When Complainant learned of this, he felt that his HIPAA rights were violated.
Management investigated the matter and concluded that a violation did not occur; and that
Supervisor 1 had not received any of Complainant’s confidential medical information. ROI 2 at
68-9 and 122.

Regarding claims 9-12, the Agency concluded that a “look of disgust,” a feeling of being
“isolated,” and a comment by a coworker that he did not like Complainant are merely casual
comments or rude remarks and do not meet the threshold necessary to establish a hostile work
environment.

                                 CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

On appeal, among other things, Complainant reiterates his allegations and contests the Agency’s
decision. He asserts that the Agency’s stated reasons for the challenged actions are pretextual;
and that management provided no reasons for the professional isolation he endured. Complainant
states that procedural violations occurred. He alleges that deficiencies existed in the investigation
into his complaints, asserting that the EEO Investigator failed to obtain key testimony from key
                                              6                                     2021002548

witnesses including HR staff from the Classification Department regarding the classification of
the CSC position. Complainant also alleges that the Agency’s February 2021 FAD was untimely.

Complainant argues that accepted claims 12 through 14 (regarding Workers’ Comp) fall within
an exemption to the Commission’s collateral attack doctrine and should be considered as
independently actionable discrete acts.

Complainant requests that the Commission reverse the Agency’s FAD and issue a finding of
hostile work environment based on race and reprisal in his favor.

On appeal, among other things, the Agency reiterates stated reasons for the challenged actions
and alleges that Complainant included in his Appeal Statement documents that were not contained
in the investigative file and subsequently not considered in the FAD. The Agency therefore asks
that the Commission not accept or consider the 34 pages of enclosures included in Complainant’s
appeal.

Regarding Complainant’s requests for sanctions against the Agency for failure to adequately
develop the record and for failure to timely issue a FAD, the Agency argues that Complainant
had since received the FAD and exercised his appeal rights. The Agency admits to the delay in
FAD processing, attributing it to a transition of its Office of Employment Discrimination
Complaint Adjudication to an all virtual workplace due to the COVID-19 pandemic and dramatic
workload increase. According to the Agency, Complainant suffered no harm beyond delayed
appeal rights as a result of the delayed FAD. The Agency requests that the Commission uphold
its FAD.

                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

Preliminary Matters

Regarding Complainant’s argument that investigation deficiencies into his complaints exist, it is
the Commission’s position that, as a general rule, no new evidence will be considered on appeal
unless there is an affirmative showing that the evidence was not reasonably available prior to or
during the investigation. Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 CFR
Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Ch. 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015). See also Godsey v. Dept. of the
Treasury, EEOC Appeal No. 01A24901 (Mar. 5, 2003). We find no such showing in the instant
complaint. Besides, Complainant failed to explain how any additional testimony from the
Classification Department would lead to a different outcome in this case.

There is also no evidence that Complainant was denied Worker’s Comp as a result of Supervisor
1’s expressed conflict of interest concerns or that Complainant suffered any adverse employment
action as a result. Therefore, we decline to address his collateral attack doctrine arguments.
                                              7                                       2021002548

Complainant’s Request for Default Judgment as a Sanction for Untimely Issuance of FAD

We note that our regulations require agency action in a timely manner at many points in the EEO
process. Tammy S. v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 0120084008 (June 6, 2014).
Compliance with these timeframes is not optional; as the Commission stated in Royal v. Dep’t
of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Request No. 0520080052 (Sept. 25, 2009), “the Commission has
the inherent power to protect its administrative process from abuse by either party and must
insure that agencies, as well as complainants, abide by its regulations.” Because of the length
of time it can take to process a federal sector EEO complaint, any delays in complying with the
time frames in the regulations can impact the outcome of the complainant’s claims. Id.

Here, we find that the Agency failed to comply with the Commission’s regulations. The record
reveals that the AJ remanded the complaints for a final agency decision on August 3, 2020,
following Complainant’s hearing request withdrawal. The Agency conceded that it had taken
more than a year to issue its final decision on complaints (1) and (2), but attributed the delay to
a transition of its Office of Employment Discrimination Complaint Adjudication to an all virtual
workplace due to the COVID-19 pandemic and dramatic workload increase.

Although the Agency failed to timely issue a final decision as required by our regulations, it did
not act in a manner to warrant the sanction of a default judgment against it. See, e.g. Josefina
L. v. Soc. Sec. Admin., 0120142023 (July 19, 2016), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request
No. 0520170108 (Feb. 9, 2017) (finding that the Agency’s 571-day delay in issuing the decision
did not warrant sanctions, as complainant did not show she was prejudiced by the delay); Abe
K. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141252 (Nov. 4, 2016) (declining to sanction an
agency that issued a decision after approximately 326 days when complainant failed to show that
he was prejudiced by the delay); Jocelyn R. v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 0120152852
(Mar. 11, 2016) (citing Vunder v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A55147 (May 12,
2006) (declining to sanction an agency that issued a decision after approximately 371 days));
Anthony M. v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Appeal No. 2019003380 (Sept. 22, 2020). The
Commission finds that there is no evidence that the Agency’s delay in this case is attributable to
contumacious conduct or bad faith. As such, under the specific circumstances present, we
decline to sanction the Agency for its delay in issuing the final decision.

While we will not impose a sanction in the present case, we do find the Agency’s failure to abide
by the regulations reflects negatively on the Agency’s support for the integrity of the EEO
process. Beatrice B. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 2019001641 (Sept. 17,
2020) (The Commission declined to issue a sanction where following a supplemental
investigation, the Agency delayed in issuing a final decision for over eight months). As a result,
we will notify Federal Sector Programs (FSP) which monitors the federal agencies’ EEO
programs of the Agency’s failure to comply with the regulations regarding the timely issuance
of final agency decisions.

The Merits of the Agency’s Final Decision

Having reviewed the record, we find that the Agency correctly analyzed the facts and law of this
case to determine that Complainant did not establish that the Agency subjected him to
discrimination with respect to claims 3, 4, and 8. The Commission has made it clear that a
change in job duties is not sufficient to establish an adverse employment action. Leming v.
Dep’t of Transp., EEOC Appeal No. 0120072692 (Aug. 7, 2007).

We also find that the Agency correctly analyzed the facts and law regarding Complainant’s
harassment allegations with respect to claims 1, 5, and 6 because those allegations were
insufficiently severe or persuasive to rise to the level of a hostile work environment given the
Agency articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for the alleged actions in claims 3, 4,
and 8. See Doe v. U.S. Postal Service, EEOC Appeal No. 0120130491 (Dec. 18, 2014) citing
Oakley v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01932923 (Sep. 21, 2000) (A finding of hostile
work environment is precluded where the complainant fails to establish that any of the actions
taken by the Agency were motivated by discriminatory or retaliatory animus).

Finally, the Agency correctly analyzed the facts and law regarding Complainant’s remaining
harassment allegations. We note that the Commission has posited that for a hostile work
environment claim to be actionable, the conduct must be more than merely offensive, it must
exceed “casual comments, rude or derogatory remarks, and conduct motivated by personal
animosity or personal feud” and the conduct must materially change the terms and conditions of
employment and be more than an inconvenience or a change in job responsibility. Sealey v.
Affiliated Computer Servs., Inc., No. 11-cv-489, 2012, W.D.N.Y. (Mar. 6, 2012;

In reaching this conclusion, we note that Complainant has not offered any substantive arguments
as to why the Agency erred in finding no discrimination or cited to evidence that he believes
substantiates his allegations.

                                        CONCLUSION

As we see no independent basis for finding in favor of Complainant, we AFFIRM the Agency’s
final decision.

                              RECONSIDERATION (M0920)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if Complainant or the
Agency submits a written request that contains arguments or evidence that tend to establish that:
       1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or
          law; or
       2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies, practices, or
          operations of the agency.
                                              9                                       2021002548

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Operations (OFO)
within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party requesting
reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request, that statement or
brief must be filed together with the request for reconsideration. A party shall have twenty
(20) calendar days from receipt of another party’s request for reconsideration within which to
submit a brief or statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B
(Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit his or her request for reconsideration, and any statement or brief in
support of his or her request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at

https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx

Alternatively, Complainant can submit his or her request and arguments to the Director, Office
of Federal Operations, Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed
to P.O. Box 77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street,
NE, Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s request to
reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFO receives it by mail within five days of the
expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the EEOC’s
Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). Either party’s request
and/or statement or brief in opposition must also include proof of service on the other party,
unless Complainant files his or her request via the EEOC Public Portal, in which case no proof
of service is required.

Failure to file within the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s request for
reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the timely filing of the
request. Any supporting documentation must be submitted together with the request for
reconsideration. The Commission will consider requests for reconsideration filed after the
deadline only in very limited circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(c).
