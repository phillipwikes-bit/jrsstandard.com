                                           DECISION

On August 23, 2018, the Agency issued a final Agency decision (FAD) finding that Complainant
had not shown she was subjected to harassment (sexual or non-sexual) based on sex, but that she
had demonstrated that she was subjected to reprisal, in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights
Act of 1964, as amended (Title VII). The August 23, 2018 decision concluded that Complainant
may be entitled to an award of backpay, attorneys’ fees, and compensatory damages. On December
31, 2018, upon receipt of a damages petition by Complainant, the Agency issued its decision.

On January 24, 2019, Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
damages decision dated December 31, 2018.

For the following reasons, the Commission MODIFIES the Agency’s final decision.

                                     ISSUES PRESENTED

The issues presented are whether the Agency properly determined Complainant’s entitlement to
back pay, compensatory damages, and attorneys’ fees and costs.

                                    STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b),
the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).
See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, (EEO MD-
110) at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires
that the Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of
the previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony
of record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).

                                  ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

Back Pay

The purpose of a back-pay award is to restore to a complainant the income she would have
otherwise earned but for the discrimination. Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody, 442 U.S. 405, 418-
19 (1975); Davis v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04900010 (Nov. 29, 1990).
Gross back pay should include all forms of compensation and must reflect fluctuations in working
time, overtime rates, penalty overtime, Sunday premium and night work, changing rate of pay,
transfers, promotions, and privileges of employment to which the petitioner would have been
entitled but for the discrimination. Ulloa v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04A30025 (Aug.
3, 2004) (citing Allen v. Dep't of the Air Force, EEOC Petition No. 04940006 (May 31, 1996)). In
computing the net amount of back pay payable, an agency is required to offset any outside earnings
received by an employee for other employment undertaken to replace the employment from which
the employee was separated. See 5 C.F.R. § 550.805(e)(1). Specifically, § 706(g) of Title VII, 42
U.S.C. § 2000e-5(g), provides that “interim earnings or amounts earnable with reasonable
diligence by the person or persons discriminated against shall operate to reduce
the backpay otherwise allowable.”

Here, the record demonstrated that the Captain made it a point during a discussion with
Complainant on December 21, 2014, that she should speak with a supervisor at East Coast
Observers (ECO) to ensure that her ECO supervisor understood the situation, and that the Agency
was not terminating, or suggesting her termination. The Captain testified that he wanted to make

sure Complainant would not lose her private sector position. There is no evidence that the Captain,

A complainant generally has a duty to mitigate damages. An agency has the burden to establish,
by a preponderance of the evidence that a complainant has failed to mitigate her damages. See 29
C.F.R. §1614.501(d); see also McNeil v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Petition No. 04990007 (Dec. 9,
1999). The Commission has generally held that an agency must satisfy a two-prong test to meet
its burden of proof. This test requires the agency to show that: (1) complainant failed to use
reasonable care and diligence in seeking a suitable position; and (2) there were suitable positions
available which complainant could have discovered and for which he or she was qualified. See
Beaton v. Dep't of Justice, EEOC Petition No. 04A30044 (Jan. 13, 2004).

While the Agency determined in its August 23, 2018 decision that Complainant might be entitled
to back pay, its order also noted that Complainant would need to provide supporting
documentation. Based on information submitted by Complainant, the Agency determined, and we
agree, that Complainant failed to meet her burden to establish that she is entitled to recover back
pay. Complainant was an ECO employee on contract with the Agency and based on the Wheeler.
When Complainant began having issues aboard the Wheeler, she contacted ECO management to
relay her concerns, but she did not reveal to ECO management what her exact concerns were. ECO
management informed Complainant that ECO would assist her in anyway, and that she need only
to contact them. When Complainant then first relayed her sexual harassment allegations to the
Captain, an Agency employee, he ensured her that her employment with ECO would be safe.
Shortly after, the Captain subjected Complainant to involuntary confinement in her quarters.

Once Complainant was removed from the Wheeler, ECO management attempted several times to
contact Complainant. Complainant acknowledged ECO’s attempts to contact her and also
acknowledged that she chose not to respond. The Agency determined that Complainant failed to
make any reasonable efforts to mitigate her losses. More importantly, it was determined that
Complainant was not terminated from ECO based on her protected EEO activity, but due to her
repeated refusals to work with ECO on how to complete her contract and/or next steps. The Agency
noted that evidence demonstrated that Complainant could have completed her contract with ECO
without necessarily returning the Wheeler. There is no evidence that Complainant would have
been forced to return to the Wheeler to finish her ECO contract. Accordingly, we agree with the
Agency and affirm its decision that Complainant is not entitled to back pay as she failed to mitigate
her damages.

Compensatory Damages

To receive an award of compensatory damages, Complainant must demonstrate that she has been
harmed as a result of the Agency's discriminatory action; the extent, nature, and severity of the
harm; and the duration or expected duration of the harm. Rivera v. Dep't of the Navy, EEOC
                                                10                                     2019002357

Appeal No. 01934157 (July 22, 1994), req. for reconsideration denied, EEOC Request No.
05940927 (Dec. 11, 1995); Compensatory and Punitive Damages Available Under Section 102 of
the Civil Rights Act of 1991, EEOC Notice No. 915.002 (EEOC Notice No. 915.002) (July 14,
1992), at 11-12, 14. Compensatory damages may be awarded for past and future pecuniary losses
(i.e., out-of-pocket expenses) and non-pecuniary losses (e.g., pain and suffering, mental anguish)
which are directly or proximately caused by the agency's discriminatory conduct. EEOC Notice
No. 915.002 at 8. The amount awarded should reflect the extent to which the agency's
discriminatory action directly or proximately caused harm to the complainant and the extent to
which other factors may have played a part. Id. at 11-12. The amount of non-pecuniary damages
should also reflect the nature and severity of the harm to the complainant, and the duration or
expected duration of the harm. Id. at 14, see Goetze v. Dep't. of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No.
01991530 (Aug. 23, 2001).

Non-Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

Non-pecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise quantification, including emotional
pain and injury to character, professional standing, and reputation. Id. There is no precise formula
for determining the amount of damages for non-pecuniary losses except that the award should
reflect the nature and severity of the harm and the duration or expected duration of the
harm. See Loving v. Dep't of the Treasury, EEOC Appeal No. 01955789 (Aug. 29, 1997). The
Commission notes that non-pecuniary compensatory damages are designed to remedy the harm
caused by the discriminatory event rather than to punish the agency for the discriminatory action.
Furthermore, compensatory damages should not be motivated by passion or prejudice or be
““monstrously excessive” standing alone but should be consistent with the amounts awarded in
similar cases. See Ward-Jenkins v. Dep't of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No. 01961483 (Mar. 4,
1999).

In Carle v. Dep’t of the Navy, the Commission explained that “objective evidence” of non-
pecuniary damages could include a statement by the complainant explaining how he or she was
affected by the discrimination. EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan. 5, 1993). Statements from
others, including family members, friends, and health care providers could address the outward
manifestations of the impact of the discrimination on the complainant. Id. Complainant could
also submit documentation of medical or psychiatric treatment related to the effects of the
discrimination. Id. Non-pecuniary damages must be limited to the sums necessary to compensate
the injured party for the actual harm and should take into account the severity of the harm and the
length of the time the injured party has suffered from the harm. Carpenter v. Dep’t of Agric.,
EEOC Appeal No. 01945652 (July 17, 1995).

In requesting an award for $300,000, Complainant cited to several cases in support. For example,
Complainant cited to Juli Z. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120102789 (Apr. 8, 2016),
a complaint where the Commission altered an initial award from $35,000 to $150,000.
Complainant argued that her case was similar to Juli Z. since both her and the complainant suffered
severe mental issues following the harassing incidents. Id. However, the situations are not
comparable. We note that in Juli Z. the complainant suffered extreme, prolonged emotional
                                                11                                     2019002357

distress and harm because of the Agency's seven-year failure to promptly and effectively address
the harassment. Id. Additionally, the focus in Juli Z. was the long-term harassment. Id. We remind
Complainant that we can only provide an award based on the after effects of the reprisal she was
subject to on December 21, and 22, 2014.

Based on the record the Agency awarded Complainant $12,000 in non-pecuniary compensatory
damages. In arriving at this award amount, the Agency asserted that Complainant was repeatedly
confusing how much she could obtain. The Agency noted repeatedly that Complainant could not
obtain an amount for both the alleged harassment, and the determined reprisal actions that occurred
on December 21 and 22, 2014. We agree with the Agency that Complainant’s request for $300,000
in non-pecuniary compensatory damages is inappropriate given the circumstances.

However, we find the award of $12,000 to be insufficient. Here, the record contains hundreds of
pages of medical documentation. The medical documentation lists numerous stressors in
Complainant’s life, but it is also clear that the reprisal actions of the Agency on December 21 and
22, 2014 have had a clear and lasting impact on Complainant. We note that following a July 15,
2016 medical exam, the psychiatrist wrote the following regarding Complainant’s exam:

       The captain of the ship responded to circumstance as if she enabled it and possibly
       encouraged it. Since she was out to in middle of ocean she had nowhere to go, made
       her experience even more traumatizing. Her perpetual request for relief of this
       experience, only made matters significantly worse as well as her description of the
       gross negligence of how the governing authorities handled this situation once
       notified. Their response and decisions made seemed to cause an imposition on
       [Complainant] in a dehumanizing way. She has significant symptoms of PTSD, of
       qualifying level of severe, which include [but] not limited to: nightly nightmares
       effecting her sleep, hypervigilance and high strung pervasively all the time causing
       her to easily startle, emotional, foreshortened future, avoidance, reliving
       experience, distrust of others and environment, impacting her ability to work and
       relationships with others.

Complainant went to the Wheeler’s highest ranking official, the Captain, and asserted serious
sexual and physical assault allegations. In return, she was involuntarily confined to her room,
without access to a toilet, and without initially understanding how long the confinement would
last. Furthermore, when she broke her confinement, she was threatened with arrest and removal
by the Coast Guard. Complainant provided testimony from herself, her father, and a coworker
aboard the Wheeler demonstrating how her demeanor changed, how she became severely
depressed, hyper vigilant, paranoid, suicidal, and unable to maintain employment or relationships.
Additionally, despite a desire to return to her marine career, she asserted a severe fear of being
trapped on a ship again. Considering the unique circumstances of this situation, the egregiousness
and severity of the Captain’s reprisal actions, we find the $12,000 award to be wholly insufficient.

In determining a more adequate amount, the Commission has taken into consideration the evidence
submitted by the Complainant, other evidence in the record directly related to the finding including
                                                  12                                     2019002357

the duration, severity, and egregious nature of the reprisal; medical documentation showing that
Complainant, while severely impacted by the reprisal, also faced other stressors unrelated to the
incidents of reprisal; and, relevant case law. Additionally, we considered the circumstances here;
that while the reprisal only occurred over two-days, the severity and the egregiousness of the
Captain’s order to involuntarily confine Complainant following her allegations cannot be taken
lightly. Based on the record and the Commission’s research, we conclude that an award of
$75,000.00 would better compensate Complainant for the harm she suffered as a result of the
reprisal. While the duration of the reprisal was short, the egregiousness of the action is particularly
troubling and warrants such an award. We do not find this amount to be motivated by passion or
prejudice or be “monstrously excessive”. Ward-Jenkins v. Dep't of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No.
01961483 (Mar. 4, 1999).

This award is consistent with similar awards approved by the Commission. For example,
Coffee v. Dep't of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 0720090012 (Mar. 13, 2009) (complainant was
awarded $75,000.00 when the AJ determined that the agency’s lack of response was egregious.
The AJ noted that the complainant was isolated on a temporary duty assignment, living in a hotel
room next to the alleged offending co-worker, and away from her family during much of the time
giving rise to these discriminatory events, which lasted over the course of three months);
Brown v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 01983712 (June 22, 2000) ($75,000.00 in non-
pecuniary damages where discrimination caused sleeplessness, insomnia, argumentative behavior,
depression, anxiety, low self-esteem, and increased irritability); Pendelton v. U.S. Postal Serv.,
EEOC Appeal No. 0720090054 (Sept. 21, 2011) (Commission awarded $80,000.00 for emotional
distress for a single instance of discrimination -- a failure to promote); Complainant v. Dep’t. of
the Navy 10, EEOC Appeal No. 0120150940 (Apr. 13, 2016) (three complainants each awarded
$110,000 for the suffering, fear, and anxiety endured when it was discovered that a high-ranking
government official installed hidden cameras in the restrooms. The hidden cameras were in place
for approximately a month, and once discovered the agency moved quickly to rectify the situation.
Agency supervisor’s video voyeurism determined to have long lasting impacts).

We also note that Complainant requested an award based on the stress and anxiety that she endured
waiting for the completion of her EEO complaint. But as noted by the Agency, a complainant it
not entitled to recover non-pecuniary damages for pain or stress associated with prosecution of an
EEO complaint. Alexander v. Dep't of the Army, EEOC Appeal No. 0720060050 (Apr. 15, 2010)
(citing Appleby v. Dep't of the Army, EEOC Appeal No. 01933897 (Mar. 4, 1994)). Therefore,
Complainant is not eligible for an increase based on the stress and anxiety she endured awaiting
the results of the complaint.

Since the Agency had previously paid Complainant $12,000.00 in non-pecuniary damages,
Complainant is now entitled to an additional $63,000.00 in non-pecuniary damages. This brings
the total compensation to $75,000.00.

Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

For claims seeking pecuniary damages, such objective evidence should include documentation of
out-of-pocket expenses for all actual costs and an explanation of the expense, e.g., medical and
psychological billings, other costs associated with the injury caused by the agency's actions, and
an explanation for the expenditure. EEOC Notice No. 915.002 at 9.

In her request for pecuniary damages, Complainant requested that she be reimbursed for $4,373.14
in moving expenses to and from Hawaii 11 after being removed from the Wheeler, and medical bills
related to treatment following her removal, a total of $23,432. Complainant also sought future
pecuniary damages arguing that her future earning power was greatly diminished by the events.
As a reminder, we note that the pecuniary damages would be limited only to those losses connected
to injury caused by the finding of unlawful retaliation which Complainant experienced over a two-
day time period.

In this matter, the Agency determined that Complainant failed to establish a connection between
the retaliation, and her relocations, her extensive medical costs, and her diminished ability to work.
The Agency determined that given the medical documentation, it was clear that much of
Complainant’s ailments were attributed to other major stressors in her life, and not entirely
connected to the reprisal. However, the Agency acknowledged that at least some part of
Complainant’s mental health issues was attributed to the two-day reprisal. Therein, the Agency
determined that Complainant should be awarded $1,000.00 in pecuniary damages. In determining
this amount, the Agency cited to Watkins v. Dept. of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No.
01A40502 (Feb. 11, 2005) (the complainant suffered from serious, long lasting psychological
problems from an array of causes; but that neither the complainant nor his physician differentiated
which symptoms, or increase in symptoms, could be specifically associated with the single finding
of discrimination in the case. In fairness, it was nonetheless presumed that some part of the
complainant’s psychiatric symptoms must be attributed to his emotional distress from the agency’s
discriminatory actions. Considering the lack of evidence to demonstrate the required nexus, the
agency awarded $150.00 in pecuniary compensatory damages, and $1,000.00 in non-pecuniary
compensatory damages).

Based on the record, we find that Complainant failed to provide evidence that clearly connects the
Agency’s two-day reprisal actions to Complainant’s subsequent relocations to Hawaii and
Virginia, her extensive medical bills, and her diminished capacity to work. There is no supportive
evidence to demonstrate that the Agency’s reprisal action caused Complainant to relocate to
Hawaii and then to Virginia. Additionally, while there is extensive medical documentation
demonstrating Complainant’s array of mental health needs and issues, much of the medical record
discusses Complainant’s mental health issues as being related to events outside of the reprisal. For
example, several medical professionals noted Complainant’s history of alleged physical, sexual,
and mental abuse that seemed to be the root of her mental health issues. Additionally, Complainant


11

has not provided sufficient documents connecting the Agency’s reprisal actions to her alleged
diminished capacity to work. Complainant did not establish a nexus between the losses she
suffered and the Agency’s two-day reprisal actions.

We agree with the Agency that at least some portion of Complainant’s mental health issues could
be attributed to the two-day reprisal. Therefore, we find that the $1,000.00 in pecuniary damages
was sufficient, and no further action is required of the Agency.

Attorneys’ Fees and Costs

The Commission's regulations authorize the award of reasonable attorney's fees and costs to a
prevailing complainant. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.501(e); see also EEO MD-110, at Chap. 11, § I. Fee
awards are typically calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended times a
reasonable hourly rate, an amount also known as a lodestar. See 29 C.F.R. §1614.501(e)(ii)(B);
Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983).

All hours reasonably spent in processing the complaint are compensable, but the number of hours
should not include excessive, redundant or otherwise unnecessary hours. EEO MD-110 at Chap.
11, § VI. F. A reasonable hourly rate is based on prevailing market rates in the relevant community
for attorneys of similar experience in similar cases. Id. An application for attorney's fees must
include a verified statement of attorney's fees accompanied by an affidavit executed by the attorney
of record itemizing the attorney's charges for legal services. Id.

There is a strong presumption that the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by a
reasonable hourly rate, the lodestar, represents a reasonable fee, but this amount may be reduced
or increased in consideration of the degree of success, quality of representation, and long delay
caused by the agency. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.501(e)(2)(ii)(B). The circumstances under which the
lodestar may be adjusted are extremely limited, and are set forth in EEO MD-110, at Chap. 11,
§VI. F. 1. A fee award may be reduced: in cases of limited success; where the quality of
representation was poor; the attorney’s conduct resulted in undue delay or obstruction of the
process; or where settlement likely could have been reached much earlier, but for the attorney’s
conduct. Id. The party seeking to adjust the lodestar, either up or down, has the burden of justifying
the deviation. Id. at Chap. 11, § VI. F. 2.

Additionally, a prevailing complainant is entitled to recovery of her costs in prosecuting the claims
on which she prevailed. These include copying, postage, travel expenses, and so forth. 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.501(e)(2)(ii)(C). Reasonable costs incurred directly by the prevailing complainant are
compensable. Costs must be proved in the same manner as fees, and the complainant must provide
documentation, such as bills or receipts. See EEO MD-110, Chap. 11, § VI.E.

Attorney’s Fees and Costs for Attorney1

Attorney fees may not be recovered for work on unsuccessful claims. The fact that a complainant
did not prevail on every aspect of her complaint does not, in itself however, justify a reduction in
                                                15                                     2019002357

the hours expended where the claims are intertwined, making it impossible to segregate the hours
involved in each claim. Hensley, at 433, 448. Courts have held that fee applicants should exclude
time expended on “truly fractionable” claims or issues on which they did not prevail. Nat’l Ass. of
Concerned Veterans (NACV) v. Sec. of Defense, 675 F.2d 1319, 1337 n. 13 (D.C. Cir. 1982).
Claims are fractionable or unrelated when they involve “distinctly different claims for relief that
are based on different facts and legal theories.” Hensley, 461 U.S. at 434-35.

Here, the Agency reduced Attorney1’s fee and costs with an across the board reduction of 50%.
We agree with this assessment as Complainant was only successful on the reprisal portion of her
claims. Additionally, the Agency determined that not all of Attorney1’s hours were eligible for
compensation as several were conducted during the pre-complaint stage. As a result, the Agency
had reduced pre-complaint hours but added two eligible hours for the time spent in consultation
with Complainant regarding whether he would take on Complainant’s case. We agree with the
Agency's reduction in fees in this regard. See Vincent v. Dep't of the Army, EEOC Request No.
05941012 (Feb. 27, 1996). The Commission has previously found that an attorney may reasonably
expend up to two hours to determine whether to represent a complainant. See Nita S. v. Dep’t of
Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120151925 (May 23, 2017); Walker v. Dep't of Agric.,
EEOC Appeal No. 01944314 (Aug. 4, 1995); Flora v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No.
01943058 (May 26, 1995); Floxie v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01940384 (Oct. 14,
1994); Stauner v. Dep't of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 01890678 (April 13, 1989).

Furthermore, we also agree with the Agency’s assessment that Attorney1’s documentation
regarding his law firm’s costs were inadequate, and therefore would not be awarded. Lastly, we
note that Attorney1 did not contest the Agency’s determination on his fees and costs petition on
appeal. The record demonstrated that the Agency issued Attorney1 a check for $5,987.50 on
January 25, 2019. Accordingly, no further action is required.

Attorney’s Fees for Attorney2

In the instant case, we find that the Agency erred in determining that it was inappropriate for
Complainant to hire counsel from the Metro D.C. area, and therein reducing Attorney2’s requested
fee of $450.25 to $250 an hour. Here, Complainant explained that upon her relocation to Virginia,
she had difficulty reaching Attorney1, and wanted to have a local attorney that she could meet
personally to discuss her complaint. The Commission has previously held that if a higher-priced,
out-of-town attorney renders services which local attorneys could do as well, and there is no other
reason to have them performed by the out-of-town attorney, then it may be appropriate to allow
only the hourly rate which local attorneys would have charged for the same service. Southerland
v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A05403 (Oct. 16, 2002). However, the Commission has
also held that the burden is on the Agency, not the complainant, to demonstrate that the decision
to retain out-of-town counsel was unreasonable. Id. In this matter, we find that the Agency has
failed to meet its burden. Complainant explicitly stated her difficulties in reaching Attorney1, and
desire to have in-person counsel to guide her through her complaint, especially as she was nearing
several deadlines during the time she switched counsel.
                                                 16                                     2019002357

The Agency did not contest Attorney2’s number of hours worked, only the hourly rate. Based on
the record, we determine that the Agency shall pay Attorney2 his asking rate of $450.25 at 39.50
hours for a total of $17,785.00 12. This amount will be reduced by $9,875.00, which the Agency
had previously paid for services rendered. Accordingly, the Agency shall pay Attorney2 $7,910.00
for the remaining portion of his fees. Attorney2 did not requests any costs. Accordingly, none were
awarded.
