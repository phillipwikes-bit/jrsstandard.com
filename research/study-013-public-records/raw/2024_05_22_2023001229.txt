                                            ANALYSIS

The Commission’s regulations allow an AJ to issue a decision without a hearing upon finding that
there is no genuine issue of material fact. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.109(g). EEOC’s decision without a
hearing regulation follows the summary judgment procedure from federal court. Fed. R. Civ. P.
56. The U.S. Supreme Court held summary judgment is appropriate where a judge determines no
genuine issue of material fact exists under the legal and evidentiary standards. Anderson v. Liberty
Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). In ruling on a summary judgment motion, the judge is to
determine whether there are genuine issues for trial, as opposed to weighing the evidence. Id. at
249. At the summary judgment stage, the judge must believe the non-moving party’s evidence and
must draw justifiable inferences in the non-moving party’s favor. Id. at 255. A “genuine issue of
fact” is one that a reasonable judge could find in favor for the non-moving party. Celotex v. Catrett,
477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986); Oliver v. Digital Equip. Corp., 846 F.2d 103, 105 (1st Cir. 1988). A
“material” fact has the potential to affect the outcome of a case.

An AJ may issue a decision without a hearing only after determining that the record has been
adequately developed. See Petty v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 01A24206 (July 11, 2003).
We carefully reviewed the record and find that it is adequately developed. To successfully oppose
a decision without a hearing, Complainant must identify material facts of record that are in dispute
or present further material evidence establishing facts in dispute. Here, Complainant argues that
the that the Agency did not adequately accommodate her. Complainant also argues that there are
material facts in dispute regarding whether she could perform the essential functions of her
position. We find that these purported disputed facts relate to conclusions of law, and not facts.
With the complete record, including witness statements, we find that additional evidence is not
necessary for the claims at issue and that Complainant’s arguments did not successfully establish
a genuine issue of material fact.

Disparate Treatment (Claims 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 14)

The Commission has found that a discrete action states a claim outside of the framework of a
harassment analysis and can also be reviewed within the disparate treatment context. See Moylett
v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120091735 (Jul. 17, 2012); Sedlacek v. Dep’t of Army,
EEOC Appeal No. 0120083361 (May 11, 2010). Claims 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 14 involve timely
discrete acts that independently state claims outside of the harassment framework. Accordingly,
we will analyze those claims in the context of disparate treatment.
                                                 7                                     2023001229

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim, Complainant must satisfy the three-part evidentiary
scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792
(1973). First, she must generally establish a prima facie case by demonstrating that she was
subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances that would support an inference
of discrimination. Furnco Constr. Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576 (1978). Proof of a prima facie
case will vary depending on the facts of the particular case. McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802
n. 13.

To establish a prima facie case of disparate treatment discrimination based on disability, a
complainant generally must prove the following elements: (1) they are an individual with a
disability as defined in 29 C.F.R. §§ 1614.203(a) and 1630.2(g); (2) they are “qualified” as defined
in 29 C.F.R. §§1614.203(a) and 1630.2(m); (3) the agency took an adverse action against them;
and (4) there was a causal relationship between their disability and the agency’s actions. See
Annamarie F. v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Appeal No. 2021004539 (August 17, 2023).

A complainant may establish a prima facie case of reprisal by showing that: (1) she engaged in a
protected activity; (2) the agency was aware of the protected activity; (3) subsequently, she was
subjected to adverse treatment by the agency; and (4) a nexus exists between the protected activity
and the adverse treatment. Whitmire v. Dep't of the Air Force, EEOC Appeal No. 01A00340 (Sept.
25, 2000).

The Agency does not dispute that Complainant is a qualified individual with a disability. A fair
reading of the record reveals that claim 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 14 involved adverse actions. Complainant
argued that there is a causal relationship between her disability and the Agency’s adverse actions
because it was her medical condition that made her use unscheduled sick leave, get placed on a
medical certification due to missed work time, charged as being AWOL, and receive a poor
performance appraisal. Complainant also argued a causal relationship existed between her
disability and getting assigned to cover two additional groups of patients and getting written up by
a management doctor because these actions occurred while she was out of the office under her
doctor’s care. We find that Complainant has established a prima facie case of disability
discrimination in these claims.

Regarding her retaliation claim, the record reveals that the RMO1 and RMO2 became aware of
Complainant’s protected activity in late March to early April 2020, when Complainant submitted
her reasonable accommodation request and was later allegedly denied her requests. 4 In
Complainant’s claims of reprisal, the incidents occurred in May 2020 through September 2020.
The Commission has found that a nexus is established when an agency action followed a
complainant's participation in protected activity in a relatively short period of time. See Whitmere
v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Appeal No. 01A00340 (Sept. 25, 2000).



4

Here, we find that the short timeframe between Complainant’s requests for accommodation and
the matters at issue are sufficient to establish a prima facie inference of unlawful retaliation.

After establishing a prima facie case, the burden then shifts to the Agency to articulate a legitimate,
nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affs. v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253
(1981). Should the Agency carry its burden, Complainant must then prove, by a preponderance of
the evidence, that the Agency’s explanation is a pretext for discrimination. Reeves v. Sanderson
Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000); St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502,
519 (1993); Burdine, 450 U.S. at 256.

Here, we find that the Agency proffered non-discriminatory reasons for its actions. In claim 1,
RMO1 stated that Complainant was given a memorandum for excessive use of unscheduled sick
leave after Complainant was counseled on more than one occasion for excessive leave usage. ROI
at 167. RMO1 also stated that he discussed with Complainant how her fear of COVID-19 was
affecting her work performance and recommended her to seek a reasonable accommodation for a
position with telework, if that was what she needed, but that telework was not an option at that
point in time. ROI at 171.

In claim 4, RMO1 stated that he placed Complainant on medical certification due to excessive
unscheduled leave because it is standard for management to monitor an employee’s leave if there
is a pattern of leave usage or absences. ROI at 177. He stated that he first had a conversation with
Complainant and gave her an opportunity to change her behavior. Id. When it persisted, he placed
her on medical certification. Id. In the sick leave certification memorandum, RMO1 also stated
that failure to present medical documentation upon return to duty after unscheduled leave would
result in an AWOL charge. ROI at 273.

In claim 5, RMO1 stated that Complainant was placed in an AWOL status because she had no
leave balance at the time of certifying timecards to cover her being absent. ROI at 179. RMO1
stated that it is Complainant’s responsibility to manage her leave and that he even provided a report
from their system that displayed her absences. ROI at 184.

In claim 6, RMO1 states that Complainant was assigned to cover two additional panel groups of
patients because it is common for each team to have a surrogate to cover when another RN Care
Manager is out. ROI at 180. RMO1 stated that it was for a limited period and that other Care
Managers covered more than one team as needed as well. Id.

In claim 7, RMO3 stated that he did not write Complainant up nor did he request a new nurse. ROI
at 202. However, RMO3 indicated that he had concerns that his orders were being delayed and
patient care was being affected, so he spoke with RMO1 about the situation. ROI at 202-203.
RMO3 stated that he was also getting complaints from his other staff members about Complainant.

Id. Adding to RMO3’s testimony, RMO1 stated that he received numerous complaints from RMO3
regarding Complainant’s quality of patient care. ROI at 181. RMO1 stated that RMO3 did not have
the authority to removed Complainant from the panel, so RMO1 removed her for not accepting
accountability for the responsibilities of the team and for Complainant’s refusal to accept

In claim 14, RMO1 testified that Complainant was counseled on numerous occasions which

We find that Complainant has not established pretext in any of her disparate treatment claims.
Indicators of pretext include, but are not limited to, discriminatory statements or past personal
treatment attributable to those responsible for the personnel action that led to the filing of the
complaint, comparative or statistical data revealing differences in treatment across various
protected-group lines, unequal application of Agency policy, deviations from standard procedures
without explanation or justification, or inadequately explained inconsistencies in the evidentiary
record. Mellissa F. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141697 (Nov. 12, 2015). At all
times, the ultimate burden remains with Complainant to demonstrate by a preponderance of the
evidence that the Agency's reasons were not the real reasons, and that the Agency instead was
motivated by a prohibited reason.

In an attempt to establish pretext in claim 1, Complainant stated that RMO1 knew of her medical
condition and how it caused her to be absent from work; however, RMO1 gave her a “hard time.”
ROI at 127. It should be noted that Complainant herself testified that she was not subjected to any
epithets, slurs, negative stereotyping, or threatening, intimidating, or hostile acts due her disability.
See Comp. Resp. to MSJ at 2.

In an attempt to establish pretext in claim 4, Complainant stated that another Registered Nurse
(C1) was absent from the clinic the majority of the summer in 2019, and she asserts that C1 was
not placed on medical certification due to her absences. In response, RMO1 stated that C1’s
extended leave was approved through HR, while Complainant’s was not, which was the reason
why the medical certification was needed in the first place. ROI at 195. Complainant also argued
that management should have waited for FMLA approval or disapproval prior to placing her on
medical certification. However, a review of the relevant leave policies and procedures reveals that
management is permitted to request a medical certificate for absences in excess of three
consecutive workdays, which Complainant’s leave record supports that she had in fact had more
than three consecutive workdays of absences at the time. See ROI at 250-252; 343.

Additionally, employers are entitled to make their own business judgments and the reasonableness
of the employer’s decision may be probative of whether it is pretext. However, the focus is on the
employer’s motivation, not its business judgment.
                                                 10                                     2023001229

See Matilda C. v. Equal Emp’t Opportunity Comm’n, EEOC Appeal No. 0720140027 (Jul. 31,
2018); Eric M. v. Dep’t of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No. 0120162148 (Feb. 13, 2018); Glass v.
U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 07A50068 (Jun. 15, 2006). We note that the Agency could
have waited for her FMLA determination, but that the Agency was not required to do so.
Ultimately, in this claim, there is no evidence of unlawful motivation.

In an attempt to establish pretext in claim 5 Complainant argues that she had never been placed in
AWOL status until she engaged in EEO activity, requested reasonable accommodations, and
FMLA for her medical condition. However, the record reveals that Complainant’s AWOL status
occurred only when she took unscheduled leave and did not have any existing leave to cover it.
Complainant also stated that she should not have been charged with AWOL because management
knew that her absence was related to her medical condition, but we find Complainant’s assertion
to be contrary to the Agency’s leave policies and procedures. Article 11, Section 1(C) of the
Agency’s Leave and Absences Policy states, “RNs are responsible for planning and managing their
leave.” See ROI at 337. We note that Complainant’s leave for May 14, 2020 was ultimately
changed to a combination of “other leave” and LWOP. ROI at 253.

In an attempt to establish pretext in claim 6, Complainant again cites C1 as a comparator employee
who she alleges, in this claim, was not assigned an additional group of patients while she was
absent. RMO1 refuted Complainant’s argument and testified that C1 was in fact assigned to a team
and was a surrogate, which means she, at point, would have also covered more than one team. ROI
at 196.

In claim 7, Complainant stated that her medical condition and EEO activity was a factor because
after requesting a reasonable accommodation, RMO1 displayed harassing behaviors and he should
have known it was his responsibility to ensure there was proper coverage for panels to ensure
patient safety. We do not find Complainant’s assertion to be a persuasive argument for pretext.
We also find Complainant’s assertion to be in direct conflict with Complainant’s testimony that
she was not subjected to any epithets, slurs, negative stereotyping, or threatening, intimidating, or
hostile acts due her disability or EEO activity. See Comp. Resp. to MSJ at 2.

In claim 14, Complainant asserted that her medical condition and EEO activity were factors
because her previous year evaluation was rated high satisfactory and receive excellent reviews
regarding her performance and the care provided to patients. In raising this, Complainant does not
provide any evidence to establish that the Agency’s proffered reasons for her performance rating
is pretextual. Additionally, the Commission has long held that an Agency has broad discretion to
set policies and carry out personnel decisions, such as performance appraisals, and it should not
be second-guessed by the reviewing authority absent evidence of unlawful motivation. See Texas
Dep't of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 259 (1981); Vanek v. Dep't of the Treasury,
EEOC Request No. 05940906 (Jan. 16, 1997). In this case, there is no evidence of unlawful
motivation in the Agency’s actions.
                                                 11                                     2023001229

Additionally, the Commission has long held that “[p]articipation in the EEO process does not
shield employees from uniformly applied standards of conduct and performance; nor are the
statutory anti-retaliatory provisions a license for employees to engage in misconduct.” Berkner v.
Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC Petition No. 0320110022 (June 23, 2011).

In these claims, Complainant did not show that the proffered reasons are not worthy of belief and
her bare assertions that management officials discriminated against her are insufficient to prove
pretext or that their actions were discriminatory. See Erby v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
0120064377 (Feb. 12, 2008). Therefore, we find that Complainant failed to meet the burden of
proving the Agency discriminated against her on the basis of her medical condition or in reprisal
for protected activity.

Reasonable Accommodation - Telework

In order to establish that she was denied a reasonable accommodation, Complainant must show
that: (1) she is an individual with a disability as defined by 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(g); (2) she is
“qualified” as defined by 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(m); and (3) the Agency failed to provide a reasonable
accommodation. See EEOC Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable Accommodation and Undue
Hardship Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable
Accommodation), No. 915.002 (Oct. 17, 2002). The Agency does not dispute that Complainant is
an individual with a disability and is qualified. 5 As such, an agency is required to make reasonable
accommodation to the known physical and mental limitations of a qualified individual with a
disability unless the Agency can show that accommodation would cause an undue hardship. See
29 C.F.R. §§ 1630.2(o), (p).

The term “reasonable accommodation” means, in pertinent part, modifications or adjustments to
the work environment, or to the manner or circumstances under which the position held is
customarily performed that enable a qualified individual with a disability to perform the essential
functions of the position in question. See 29 C.F.R. §1630.2(o)(1)(ii). Reasonable
accommodations may include but are not limited to job restructuring; part-time or modified work
schedules; reassignment to a vacant position; acquisition or modifications of equipment or devices;
appropriate adjustment or modifications of examinations, training materials, or policies; the
provision of qualified readers or interpreters; and other similar accommodations for individuals
with disabilities. 29 C.F.R. §1630.2(o)(2)(ii).

Complainant alleged that the Agency denied her reasonable accommodation request for fulltime
telework. Complainant testified that on March 31, 2020, she made a reasonable accommodation
request for fulltime telework and to be removed from screening patients with respiratory
symptoms. Complainant’s reasonable accommodation was accompanied by information from her
medical provider. See ROI at 363.


5
  In her appellate brief, Complainant makes several arguments regarding the Agency’s failure to
explicitly state whether she was determined to be a qualified individual with a disability. However,
since this issue is not disputed by the Agency, we decline to address this at length in this decision.
                                                 12                                     2023001229

On April 1, 2020, RMO2 responded and granted Complainant exclusion from screening patient,
but stated that she was unable to approve the request for fulltime telework because Registered
Nurses were not at that time approved for telework. ROI at 362. Complainant later submitted a
formal reasonable accommodation request and received additional accommodations in the form of
changes to her tour of duty, removal from front-line screening of patient and COVID-19 testing
and screening as well as annual leave/sick leave from 2:30pm- 3:30pm to accommodate her
childcare needs. ROI at 365. In this formal decision, Complainant’s request for fulltime telework
was also denied due to the same reasons as earlier stated. Id. However, in this denial, RMO1 added
that Complainant could not be granted fulltime telework because “a great percentage of
[Complainant’s] daily requirements is face to face patient interactions and interactions with [her]
team.” Id.

The Commission has recognized that an agency is not required to remove any of the essential
functions of a position as a reasonable accommodation. See Enforcement Guidance, General
Principles. See also Larraine S. v. Dep’t of Agriculture, EEOC Appeal No. 0120180647 (Aug. 15,
2019); Carlton T. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 0120151566 (Feb. 7, 2018); Timika O.
v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 0220140008 (Mar. 9, 2017). Further, an agency does not
unreasonably deny the reasonable accommodation request where the only effective
accommodation is to work at home and an essential function of the position requires that at least
some of the work be performed in the office. See Petzer v. Secretary of Defense, EEO Appeal No.
01A50812 (Mar. 21, 2006). Therefore, we do not find that the Agency was required to
accommodate Complainant’s reasonable accommodation request regarding fulltime telework
given that an essential function of her position was to be in person and provides services in person.
Nonetheless, management later approved Complainant to telework twice a week. ROI at 130.
When Complainant stated that this accommodation was ineffective, the Agency searched for
another position that would allow Complainant to fulltime telework. In August 2020, Complainant
was reassigned Complainant to a Nurse Triage position where fulltime telework was permitted.
ROI at 130-131. Complainant stated that this position fully accommodated for her medical
condition. ROI at 131.

In Complainant’s submissions, Complainant argued that the Agency failed to engage in the
interactive process and grant her accommodation request for telework. We do not find
Complainant’s assertion to be supported by the record regarding her formal request for reasonable
accommodations. It is clear that the Agency initially granted all of Complainant’s
accommodations, except telework, and continued to work with Complainant through granting
partial telework and then ultimately reassigning Complainant to a fulltime telework position. We
note that Complainant does not contest the reassignment. Therefore, we find that Complainant
failed to show that she was denied a reasonable accommodation by the Agency in claim 2 as the
record reflects that management took reasonable actions to accommodate Complainant’s request.
We find no violation of the Rehabilitation Act with regard to the Agency’s decision not to initially
grant Complainant’s request for a telework position.

Reasonable Accommodation - Leave
                                                 13                                     2023001229

However, in reviewing the record, we find that the Agency failed to accommodate Complainant
regarding her use of leave. We understand that Complainant did not submit a formal reasonable
accommodation request regarding her leave usage but note that an employee is not required to use
the magic words “reasonable accommodation” in order to make a request. Instead, the employee
need only inform the Agency that they need an adjustment or change at work for a reason related
to a medical condition. See Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable Accommodation and Undue
Hardship Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (Enforcement Guidance), EEOC Notice No.
915.002 (Oct. 17, 2002) at Q.1. As previously stated, it is undisputed that both RMO1 and RMO2
were aware of Complainant’s disability. See ROI at 361—362. The record and Complainant’s
testimony make it clear that many of Complainant’s unscheduled leave absences were because of
her medical condition and that these management officials were notice of it. See, e.g., ROI 369-
370, and 377. At this point, the Agency had a duty to engage in the interactive process and grant
Complainant an accommodation, absent undue hardship.

We do not find that the Agency engaged in the interactive process with Complainant regarding a
possible accommodation to her leave usage. Instead, the Agency managers, while knowing at least
some of Complainant’s absences were due to her medical condition, issued her a memorandum
regarding excessive leave usage (claim 1), placed her on medical certification (claim 2), charged
her with AWOL (claim 5), and denied her leave requests (claim 13). In these claims, the Agency
argued that approving Complainant’s unscheduled leave, including the use of LWOP, was contrary
to Agency policies and procedures. However, the duty to provide reasonable accommodation may
require an agency to modify a workplace policy when necessitated by an individual’s disability-
related limitations, absent undue hardship. Enforcement Guidance, at Question 24. The record does
not contain any evidence of undue hardship on the part of the Agency if it had granted
Complainant’s requests for leave or LWOP or allowed her to take unscheduled leave. Also, we
note that unpaid leave is a form of reasonable accommodation, whether or not provided under the
FMLA. See Enforcement Guidance, at questions 16-21, 28.

Since there is no evidence of the Agency engaging in an interactive process with Complainant
regarding her leave usage or a showing of undue hardship in granting Complainant unscheduled
leave, we find that the Agency denied Complainant a reasonable accommodation regarding leave.
The Commission has stated that, because an employer has an ongoing obligation to provide a
reasonable accommodation, failure to provide such accommodation constitutes a violation each
time the employee needs it. See EEOC Compliance Manual Section 2: Threshold Issues, No.
915.003, at 2-IV (May 12, 2000, revised in part, July 21, 2005). In this case, this violation applies
to each time the Agency denied Complainant unscheduled leave, including paid leave or LWOP,
when her absence was because of her disability and any other adverse actions that resulted of such
leave. 6


6
  A finding of discriminatory intent is not an element of a failure to accommodate. See Brittney B.
v. Dep’t of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 2021002613 (Sept. 2, 2021); Susan B. v. Dep’t of the
Army, EEOC Appeal No. 2020001632 (June 16, 2021). Therefore, our finding that the Agency
failed to accommodation Complainant’s leave does not disrupt our finding on Complainant’s
disparate treatment and harassment claims.

Hostile Work Environment (Claims 3, 8, and 10-13)

As discussed above, Complainant has not provided sufficient arguments or evidence that claims 1,
2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, or 14 were motivated by discrimination or reprisal. As such, we do not find these
claims to be supportive of Complainant’s harassment claim. See Oakley v. U.S. Postal Serv.,
EEOC Appeal No. 01982923 (Sept. 21, 2000). Complainant, therefore, remains with claims 3, 8,
10, 11, 12, and 13 to support her harassment claim.



In order to establish a prima facie case of harassment, Complainant must prove, by a
preponderance of the evidence, the existence of five elements: (1) that she is a member of a
statutorily protected class; (2) that she was subjected to unwelcome conduct related to her protected
class; (3) that the harassment complained of was based on her protected class; (4) that the
harassment had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with her work performance and/or
creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) that there is a basis for
imputing liability to the employer. See Celine B. v. Dep’t of Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2019001961
(Sept. 21, 2020); Humphrey v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01965238 (Oct. 16, 1998).
See also Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982); Flowers v. Southern Reg’l
Physician Serv. Inc., 247 F.3d 229 (5th Cir. 2001).

In other words, to prove his hostile work environment claim, Complainant must establish that she
was subjected to conduct that was either so severe or so pervasive that a “reasonable person” in
Complainant’s position would have found the conduct to be hostile or abusive. Complainant must
also prove that the conduct was taken because of a protected basis; in this case, her disability and
reprisal for her protected activity. Only if Complainant establishes both of those elements –
hostility and motive – will the question of Agency liability present itself.

The Commission notes that reprisal claims are considered with a broad view of coverage and that
the threshold for establishing retaliatory harassment is different than for discriminatory
harassment. To ultimately prevail in her claim of retaliatory harassment, Complainant must show
that she was subjected to conduct sufficient to dissuade a “reasonable person” from making or
supporting a charge of discrimination. See id. at 57; EEOC Enforcement Guidance on Retaliation
and Related Issues, EEOC Notice No. 915.004, § II(B)(3) & n. 137 (Aug. 25, 2016); Carroll v.
Dep't of the Army, EEOC Request No. 05970939 (Apr. 4, 2000).

In these claims, we find that Complainant’s claims of harassment did not occur as Complainant
described, or were related to the management of Complainant’s assignments, performance, and
conduct. For instance, in claim 3, Complainant alleged that RMO1 attempted to discourage
Complainant from applying for advanced sick leave when he said it would create “financial
implications” for her.
                                                15                                     2023001229

In response, RMO1 stated that he expressed to Complainant she may request advanced leave but
cautioned her to remember it is a debt and should exercise every option before using this method.
ROI at 175. RMO1 stated that Complainant still requested advanced leave and he forwarded it to
HR for consideration. Id.

In claim 8, Complainant alleged that RMO1 failed to respond to Complainant’s inquiry regarding
leave issues. In response, RMO1 stated that he did respond to Complainant’s inquiries about leave,
but that she was “not receptive to hearing any explanation” regarding the use of annual leave, sick
leave, and charges of AWOL. ROI at 182. In claim 10, Complainant alleged RMO2 stated she
could not assist Complainant with resolving her concerns about RMO1. However, Complainant
herself testified that RMO2 requested to meet with her and RMO1, but Complainant refused. ROI
at 146-147. Complainant stated that RMO2 should have investigated her concerns. RMO2 stated
that she recommends group discussion to resolve any concerns between an employee and
management. ROI at 214.

In claim 11, Complainant alleged that RMO1 charged her with AWOL. RMO1 stated that
Complainant was charged with AWOL because she was absent from work and had exhausted her
leave balance. Complainant argued that RMO1 should have changed the charges to LWOP, but
the relevant policies and procedures regarding LWOP state that approval of LWOP is a matter of
administrative discretion and that despite Complainant’s argument, employees cannot demand it.
ROI at 277. Nonetheless, the record reveals that Complainant’s leave for June 9, 2020 was changed
to a combination of LWOP and “other leave,” not AWOL. ROI at 256. In claim 12, Complainant
alleged RMO1 refused to provide Complainant with a copy of a complaint that was filed against
her regarding the complaints described in claim 7. RMO1 testified that he is unaware of any
complaint filed against her where she did not receive a copy. ROI at 186. RMO1 also stated that
Complainant was counseled on these issues and refused to sign the report of discussion. Id.

In claim 13, Complainant alleged that RMO1 denied Complainant’s request for leave submitted
on August 11, 2020 for August 13, 2020. RMO1 stated that Complainant requested LWOP on
August 11, 2020, but her request was denied because her FLMA was revoked which according to
HR, she was no longer eligible to use FMLA or LWOP. ROI at 188. RMO1 stated that LWOP
requests are approved by the second-line supervisor, not him, and that if the request was denied, it
could have been due to an incomplete form. Id. We note again that the Agency’s policies and
procedures regarding leave state that LWOP is a matter of administrative discretion and not
something Complainant can demand. ROI at 277.

In these claims, there is no evidence that the work-related incidents, even if occurred as alleged,
were taken in order to harass Complainant on the basis of a protected class. Accordingly, we find
that Complainant failed to meet the burden of proving the Agency subjected her to harassment
based on disability or in reprisal for prior protected activity.
