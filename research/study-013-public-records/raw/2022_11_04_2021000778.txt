                                  ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b),
the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).
See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9,
§ VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of
record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).

Disparate Treatment

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim such as this, Complainant must satisfy the three-part
evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411
U.S. 792, 802 (1973). His first step would generally be to establish a prima facie case by
demonstrating that he was subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances that
would support an inference of discrimination. Furnco Const. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576
(1978). The prima facie inquiry may be dispensed with in this case, however, since the
management officials involved in the January 2017 transfer decision and the August 2019
promotion decisions articulated legitimate and nondiscriminatory reasons for those decisions. See
U.S. Postal Service Bd. of Governors v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 713-17 (1983).

Regarding allegation (1), RMO2 and RMO3 averred that they were not involved. RMO2 also
stated that Complainant had rescinded his transfer request. IR 122, 130. In a January 21, 2017
email, in response to an inquiry from Complainant, RMO1 stated that he did not have the final
authority to make selections to fill personnel slots at Pearl Harbor. RMO1 also mentioned that he
was in the process of recommending other candidates for the various vacancies there. IR 80.

As to allegations (5) and (8), RMO2 acknowledged that he was the selecting official and that
Complainant was not interviewed for either position. The various individuals involved with the
selection processes for these positions all stated, in essence, that while Complainant met the basic
qualifications, the selectees had the edge in terms of previous experience. IR 118-20, 133, 143-
45. For example, Complainant received a score of 230 on his application package for the
Driver/Operator position while the scores of those who were chosen for interviews ranged from
236 to 273. IR 264-65, 267-78, 390-404, 411-21, 426-37, 443-54. Similarly, although
Complainant was well-qualified by virtue of his experience to be a Lieutenant, the selectees were
considered to be the most qualified candidates. Complainant received a score of 223 while the
selectees received scores of 298 and 286 respectively. As a result, Complainant was not selected
for an interview and did not receive further consideration. IR 120-21, 133, 143-47, 157, 260, 464-
501.

To ultimately prevail, Complainant must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the
Agency's explanation is a pretext for discrimination.
                                                  4                                      2021000778

Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000); St. Mary's Hon. Ctr. v.
Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993); Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256
(1981).     Pretext can be demonstrated by showing such weaknesses, implausibilities,
inconsistencies, incoherencies, or contradictions in the Agency's proffered legitimate reasons for
its action that a reasonable fact finder could rationally find them unworthy of credence. Opare-
Addo v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120060802 (Nov. 20, 2007), req. for recon. den.
EEOC Request No. 0520080211 (May 30, 2008).

In non-selection cases, Complainant could demonstrate pretext by showing that his qualifications
for the position were plainly superior to those of the selectee. Hung P. v. Dept. of Veterans Affairs,
EEOC Appeal No. 0120141721 (Dec. 3, 2015). When asked by the investigator why he believed
he was qualified for the positions he applied for, Complainant responded that he had 15 years of
federal fire department experience, 20 years of fire emergency service experience, and had been
licensed as an emergency management technician for over 18 years. IR 67, 72-95. Also, as
referenced in allegation (7), RMO1 admitted that Complainant was well-qualified to be a
Lieutenant. One of Complainant’s coworkers, himself a Lieutenant, averred that Complainant was
“no less a firefighter because of his past injury, he exceeds all minimum expectancies.” IR 174.
However, Complainant has not presented any documentary or testimonial evidence tending to
show that he was so much better qualified than any of the selectees that passing him over would
in and of itself constitute evidence of discriminatory intent. We therefore find that Complainant’s
qualifications for the Driver/Operator and Lieutenant positions were not plainly superior to those
of the selectees.

Other indicators of pretext include discriminatory statements or past personal treatment
attributable to those responsible for the personnel action that led to the filing of the complaint,
comparative or statistical data revealing differences in treatment across various protected-group
lines, unequal application of Agency policy, deviations from standard procedures without
explanation or justification, or inadequately explained inconsistencies in the evidentiary record.
Mellissa F. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141697 (Nov. 12, 2015). Complainant’s
assertions in allegations (2), (3), (4), (6), and (7), in addition to constituting incidents of alleged
discriminatory harassment, could be viewed as evidence of possible discriminatory intent on the
part of RMO1. Various witnesses averred that they heard RMO1 express concerns about
Complainant’s ability to handle the physical demands of the position at Pearl Harbor when he
requested a transfer in 2017 and the positions he applied for in 2019. Most of those witnesses
averred that they did not consider RMO1’s remarks to be derogatory or discriminatory. None of
them recalled RMO1 mentioning Complainant’s age. IR 147-49, 155, 161, 166-67, 171-73, 178-
80. Moreover, it is not even clear what influence, if any, RMO1 had in either the 2017 transfer
request or the 2019 selection processes. The evidence presented by Complainant does not
contradict or undercut the explanations provided by RMO1, RMO2, or RMO3, cause us to question
the truthfulness of these witnesses, or establishes the existence of at least one of the indicators of
pretext listed above. Ultimately, we agree with the Agency that the evidentiary record is
insufficient to support Complainant’s claim of disparate treatment with respect to allegations (1),
(5) and (8).

Hostile Work Environment

Next, we address Complainant’s hostile work environment claim, which covers Allegations (2)-
(4), (6), (7), (9) and (10). To establish a claim of hostile work environment, Complainant must
show that: (1) he belongs to a statutorily protected class; (2) he was subjected to harassment in the
form of unwelcome verbal or physical conduct involving the protected class; (3) the harassment
complained of was based on his statutorily protected classes; (4) the harassment affected a term or
condition of employment and/or had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with the
work environment and/or creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5)
there is a basis for imputing liability to the employer. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d
897 (11th Cir. 1982). Further, the incidents must have been “sufficiently severe or pervasive to
alter the conditions of [complainant's] employment and create an abusive working environment.”
Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993).

Applying the first Henson factor, we find that Complainant is a member of protected classes based
on the fact that he is over 40 years old and that he experiences long-term residual effects of knee,
wrist, and back injuries sustained on the job. Applying the second Henson factor, we find that the
alleged harassing conduct consists of remarks by RMO1 and possibly other officials to the effect
that Complainant is too prone to injury to be able to perform firefighter duties either at Pearl Harbor
or in the two positions for which he applied in August 2017. There are no indications anywhere
in the record that RMO1 directed these remarks at Complainant personally or directly.
Nevertheless, from Complainant’s subjective perspective, they would certainly be unwelcome.
Complainant thus satisfies the first two prongs of the Henson test for a hostile work environment.

The antidiscrimination statutes the Commission enforces are not civility codes. Rather, they forbid
“only behavior so objectively offensive as to alter the conditions of the victim's employment.”
Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Serv., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 81 (1998). Therefore, to prove his
harassment claim, Complainant must establish that he was subjected to conduct that was either so
severe or so pervasive that a “reasonable person” in Complainant's position would have found the
conduct to be hostile or abusive. Complainant must also prove that the conduct was taken because
of his protected classes. Only if Complainant establishes both of those elements, hostility and
motive, will the question of Agency liability present itself.

In applying the third Henson factor, we find the record to be insufficient to demonstrate that RMO1
or any other management officials harbored unlawful discriminatory animus towards Complainant
based on his age or disabilities. Many of the witnesses averred that they did not witness RMO1
making derogatory remarks about Complainant’s age or disabilities. IR 122-24, 130-32, 148-50,
155-57. As noted above, what these witnesses heard was that RMO1 had expressed concerns about
Complainant’s documented record of being injury prone and whether or not Complainant could
handle the physical demands of the positions he was seeking. IR 65, 148-49, 166, 171-72. 178.
At the same time, these witnesses stated that they never heard RMO1 make derogatory remarks
about Complainant’s age or disabilities or were otherwise unaware of such remarks.
                                                 6                                     2021000778

IR 149. 161. 166-67, 173, 179. We find the evidentiary record on this point to be ambiguous at
best, and therefore insufficient to satisfy the third element of the Henson analysis.

Assuming otherwise, we find in applying the fourth Henson factor that the harassment complained
of consists of a series of remarks allegedly made by RMO1 in the presence of individuals other
than Complainant over a period of several years. It is long-settled Commission precedent that,
unless the conduct complained of is pervasive and severe, such a single incident or group of
isolated incidents will generally not be regarded as discriminatory harassment. Mikki P. v. Dep’t
of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120182183 (Feb. 19, 2020) citing Walker v. Ford Motor
Co., 684 F.2d 1355, 1358 (11th Cir. 1982); Frye v. Dep't of Labor, EEOC Request No. 05950152
(Feb. 8, 1996); Backo v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05960227 (June 10, 1996). The
Commission recognizes only a few scenarios in which single incidents or groups of isolated
incidents could be severe or pervasive enough to give rise to a hostile environment. In this case,
the remarks attributed to RMO1 were isolated incidents and insufficiently severe or pervasive to
establish a hostile work environment. As noted above, none of RMO1’s remarks were ever
directed at Complainant. We therefore find that the record is not sufficient to demonstrate that
RMO1’s remarks about Complainant’s physical condition were severe or pervasive enough to
satisfy the fourth prong of the Henson test. As a result, the Commission finds that Complainant
was not subjected to discrimination or a hostile work environment as alleged.

                                         CONCLUSION

After a review of the record in its entirety, including consideration of all statements submitted on
appeal, it is the decision of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission to AFFIRM the
Agency’s final decision because the preponderance of the evidence of record does not establish
that discrimination occurred.

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if Complainant or the

       1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Operations (OFO)
within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision.

If the party requesting reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request,
that statement or brief must be filed together with the request for reconsideration. A party
shall have twenty (20) calendar days from receipt of another party’s request for reconsideration
within which to submit a brief or statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal
Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at
Chap. 9 § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit his or her request for reconsideration, and any statement or brief in
support of his or her request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx. Alternatively, Complainant can submit his or her
request and arguments to the Director, Office of Federal Operations, Equal Employment
Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed to P.O. Box 77960, Washington, DC 20013,
or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street, NE, Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a
legible postmark, a complainant’s request to reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFO
receives it by mail within five days of the expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the EEOC’s
Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). Either party’s request and/or
statement or brief in opposition must also include proof of service on the other party, unless
Complainant files his or her request via the EEOC Public Portal, in which case no proof of service
is required.

Failure to file within the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s request for
reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the timely filing of the
request. Any supporting documentation must be submitted together with the request for
reconsideration. The Commission will consider requests for reconsideration filed after the
deadline only in very limited circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(c).
