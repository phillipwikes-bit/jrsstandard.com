                                        BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as an IT (Information
Technology) Project Manager, GS-14, at the Agency’s Program Control Branch (PCB) within
the Mission System (MS), National Counterterrorism Center (NCTC), in Washington, D.C.

On October 13, 2016, Complainant contacted an EEO Counselor concerning her complaint.
Unable to resolve the matter informally, on December 30, 2016, Complainant filed her complaint
alleging discrimination based on age (over 40), disability, and in reprisal for prior EEO activity

                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part
1614 (EEO MD-110), Chap. 9 § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of
review "requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal
determinations of the previous decision maker," and that EEOC "review the documents,
statements, and testimony of record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the
parties, and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission's own assessment of the record and
its interpretation of the law").

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim such as this, complainant must satisfy the three-part
evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411
U.S. 792 (1973). Complainant must initially establish a prima facie case by demonstrating that
he or she was subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances that would
support an inference of discrimination. Furnco Construction Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576
(1978). Proof of a prima facie case will vary depending on the facts of the particular case.
McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 804 n. 14. The burden then shifts to the agency to articulate a
legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. Texas Department of Community Affairs v.
Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Once the agency has met its burden, the complainant bears
the ultimate responsibility to persuade the fact finder by a preponderance of the evidence that the
agency acted on the basis of a prohibited reason. See St. Mary's Honor Center v. Hicks, 509
U.S. 502 (1993).

This established order of analysis in discrimination cases, in which the first step normally
consists of determining the existence of a prima facie case, need not be followed in all cases.

Where the agency has articulated a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the personnel action
at issue, the factual inquiry can proceed directly to the third step of the McDonnell Douglas
analysis, the ultimate issue of whether complainant has shown by a preponderance of the
evidence that the agency's actions were motivated by discrimination. See U.S. Postal Service
Board of Governors v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 713-714 (1983); Hernandez v. Department of
Transportation, EEOC Request No. 05900159 (June 28, 1990); Peterson v. Department of Health
and Human Services, EEOC Request No. 05900467 (June 8, 1990); Washington v. Department
of the Navy, EEOC Petition No. 03900056 (May 31, 1990).

The Rehabilitation Act of 1973 prohibits discrimination against qualified disabled individuals.
See 29 C.F.R. § 1630. In order to establish that complainant was denied a reasonable
accommodation, Complainant must show that: (1) he or she is an individual with a disability, as
defined by 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(g); (2) he or she is a qualified individual with a disability pursuant
to 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(m); and (3) the agency failed to provide a reasonable accommodation. See
Enforcement Guidance: Reasonable Accommodation and Undue Hardship under the Americans
with Disabilities Act, EEOC No. 915.002 (Oct. 17, 2002) (“Enforcement Guidance”). Under the
Commission’s regulations, an agency is required to make reasonable accommodation to the
known physical and mental limitations of a qualified individual with a disability unless the
agency can show that accommodation would cause an undue hardship. See 29 C.F.R. §§
1630.2(o) and (p).

The Commission shall assume without deciding (for the purposes of this decision) that
Complainant is an individual with a disability.

In the instant case, Complainant alleged that on September 28, 2016, she received from her
supervisor (S1) a “Marginal” rating on her FY16 Performance Evaluation Review (PER)
containing a narrative critical of her performance between October 1, 2015, and June 24, 2016.
During this rating period, Complainant was an IT Project Manager, GS-14, at the PCB within the
MS, NCTC. Complainant had been working in her position in the MS’s PCB since June 14,
2015, and in the MS since December 14, 2014. The record indicates that on June 26, 2016,
Complainant, upon her request, was reassigned to the NCTC office of the Director, Enterprise
Management Office (EMO), under a new supervisor.

The record indicates that Complainant received a “Successful” rating from her former supervisor
for her FY15 overall performance evaluation for the rating period December 16, 2014, through
September 30, 2015.

S1 was detailed to the position of PCB Branch Chief, GS-15, and became Complainant’s
supervisor on or around October 1, 2015.

Complainant indicated that she had depression, anxiety and panic attacks, and Post Traumatic
Stress Disorder (PTSD), migraines/headaches, scoliosis, and fibromyalgia. She indicated that
she requested a chair in December 2014, and a varidesk in December 2015, as reasonable
accommodations and they were provided to her.
                                                4                                    2020000370

Complainant stated that she did not take medication for her disabilities. We note that
Complainant does not allege that she was denied a reasonable accommodation. S1 indicated that
he was not aware of Complainant’s disabilities until he read her sworn statement during the
investigation of the instant complaint.

S1 stated that on November 2, 2015, he notified all PCB employees, including Complainant, of
their performance objectives for the new PER and asked for their draft performance objectives.
On November 9, 2015, Complainant sent S1 her draft performance objectives and S1, in
response, sent a revised set of objectives. Complainant further revised the objectives indicating
that she would be responsible to “ensure that all projects are delivered within the constraints of
cost, schedule and technical scope.” When S1 expressed his concerns about the foregoing
objectives, Complainant responded “Understood. Thank you for the update!” Later that day,
Complainant initialized her FY16 PER, including the performance objectives described above.

S1 stated that on March 23, 2016, he gave Complainant her mid-cycle feedback identifying
positive behaviors because he believed that a negative feedback would demoralize her.
However, S1 indicated that he advised her: to provide him with tangible results (documents,
briefings, discussions, approvals) of her completed objectives; to increase and demonstrate
positive leadership and control of her projects; to improve the Configuration Management
posture including better communication and collaboration with other teams; to focus on maturing
the Integrated Master Schedule (IMS) reporting capability; and to improve her performance
regarding “Programmatic Metrics” along with the “Program Performance Metrics;” and to
expand her collaboration efforts with her colleagues. S1 stated that at the conclusion of the mid-
cycle feedback review, Complainant evidenced no concerns, questions nor confusion with his
feedback provided. Thus, stated S1, he expected that for the remainder of the performance
period, she would be working to address the feedback as advised.

On August 2, 2016, Complainant provided S1 with a draft list of FY16 accomplishments. S1 did
not agree with the draft as written. S1 found that Complainant’s listed accomplishments were
inaccurate and that she took credit for other’s work. Specifically, S1 disputed her assertions and
stated budget management was not within her responsibilities; her attending the governance
boards and forums did not constitute an oversight role; she failed to deliver the majority of the
accomplishment on her Configuration Master Plan; and she did not execute MS new
procurement process. S1 noted that Complainant’s true performance lagged significantly behind
the accomplishments as she indicated.

On September 28, 2016, S1, considering Complainant’s draft accomplishments and after talking
with his superior and Human Resources, issued Complainant an overall rating of “Marginal” for
FY16 PER, with which the rating official concurred. There were five possible overall
performance ratings, from the lowest to the highest, “Unacceptable,” “Marginal,” “Successful,”
“Excellent,” and “Outstanding.”

The FY16 PER consisted of performance objectives 1 – 5 and six performance elements.
Specifically, as described below, Complainant received three “Marginal” ratings for performance
objectives 1 - 3 and two “Successful” ratings for performance objectives 4 and 5; and three
“Marginal” ratings for three performance elements (accountability for results, communication,
and personal leadership and integrity) and three “Successful” ratings for three other performance
elements (critical thinking, engagement and collaboration, and technical expertise).

Specifically, regarding performance objective 1, S1 stated that although Complainant
successfully delivered an updated draft of the MS Configuration Management Plan, her
performance was “Marginal” because she failed to coordinate and approve plans and procedures
for integrating Software and Hardware and the MS Integrated Master Schedule within the span
of Configuration Control within MS with prospective dates or a schedule by which she and her
contract team would have these in place.

Regarding her performance objective 2, S1 stated that Complainant’s performance for this
objective was “Marginal” because she failed to meet the expectation of maturing the IMS
reporting capability and IMS Business Rhythm. Specifically, S1 noted that the IMS team was
pitted against the Program Control Officers to the detriment of the Program Managers; and that
Complainant failed to gain the approval of project plans with concrete deliverables (reporting
capabilities, maturation goals, etc.).

Regarding her performance objective 3, S1 stated that Complainant’s performance was
“Marginal” because she failed to produce preliminary draft deliverables and plans for the
programmatic side of the metrics reporting and analysis.

Regarding her performance objective 4, S1 indicated that Complainant’s performance was
“Successful” although little progress was made to meet the expectations of the objective given
the PCB was initiated in July 2015, which was less than 12 months old. S1 also noted
Complainant’s improper adversarial relationship with the MS Project Manager assigned to run
the Sharepoint upgrade project.

Regarding her performance objective 5, S1 indicated Complainant’s performance was
“Successful” for completing a minimum of 40 hours of training.

We note that Complainant acknowledged that she improperly processed a transfer clearance on
November 13, 2015. S1 indicated that during the rating period, Complainant particularly had
issues about delivering her assignments. When he asked her about the status of her assignment,
she would respond to him saying that she was “working on it” and when he asked her for the
expected completion date she would say “soon.” Also, S1 noted that Complainant’s December
9, 2015 meeting to discuss Performance Metrics was not well organized by her, and the topics
were too broad for the hour allotted. Furthermore, S1 stated that in her February 26, 2016
Enterprise Risk Update, Complainant improperly indicated “risk burndown as dates for
mitigation.”

From March 11 – 15, 2016, indicated S1, Complainant improperly tried to proceed with the
Sharepoint Site upgrades project although it had been already assigned to another officer, a
Government Project Manager.

After her receipt of the Marginal rating of FY16 PER on September 28, 2016, Complainant did
not “close” that PER, but rather she contacted her Employee Management Relations (EMR)
Officer on October 19, 2016, to reconsider the rating noting that she received an overall
Successful rating for FY15 from her former supervisor. In order to resolve the matter, the EMR
Officer contacted S1 and the rating official and in the end, Complainant was allowed to update
her accomplishments to better support her performance objectives.

On February 14, 2017, Complainant submitted her updated accomplishments.                  After
consideration, on the same day, S1 modified his narratives in the PER and issued Complainant
an overall FY16 PER rating as “Successful,” including “Successful” ratings for all performance
elements.

The record indicates that Complainant’s “Marginal” PER rating of September 28, 2016, at issue,
was not placed in her official record as it was not changed to “close” by her. Her Successful
rating was entered into her official record as her FY16 PER when she initialized/closed that PER
on February 14, 2017. Complainant does not dispute this.

After a review of the record, we find that Complainant failed to show that the Agency’s
articulated reasons were a mere pretext for discrimination. Complainant has not claimed that she
was denied a reasonable accommodation. Furthermore, we find that Complainant failed to show
that there were any similarly situated employees not in her protected groups who were treated
differently under similar circumstances. Based on the foregoing, we find that Complainant has
failed to show that the Agency’s action was motivated by discrimination as she alleged.

                                        CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Agency’s final decision finding no discrimination is AFFIRMED.

Requests to reconsider, with supporting statement or brief, must be filed with the Office of
Federal Operations (OFO) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. A party
shall have twenty (20) calendar days of receipt of another party’s timely request for
reconsideration in which to submit a brief or statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405;
Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110),

20013, or by certified mail to 131 M Street, NE, Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a
legible postmark, the request to reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if it is received by mail
within five days of the expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604. The
agency’s request must be submitted in digital format via the EEOC’s Federal Sector EEO Portal
(FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). The request or opposition must also include proof of
service on the other party.



Failure to file within the time period will result in dismissal of your request for reconsideration
as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented the timely filing of the request. Any
supporting documentation must be submitted with your request for reconsideration. The
Commission will consider requests for reconsideration filed after the deadline only in very
limited circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(c).


               COMPLAINANT’S RIGHT TO FILE A CIVIL ACTION (S0610)


You have the right to file a civil action in an appropriate United States District Court within
ninety (90) calendar days from the date that you receive this decision. If you file a civil action,
you must name as the defendant in the complaint the person who is the official Agency head or

national organization, and not the local office, facility or department in which you work. If you
file a request to reconsider and also file a civil action, filing a civil action will terminate the
administrative processing of your complaint.

request the court to appoint an attorney for you. You must submit the requests for waiver of
court costs or appointment of an attorney directly to the court, not the Commission. The
