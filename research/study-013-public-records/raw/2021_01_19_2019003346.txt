                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b),
the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).
See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9,
§ VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of
record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).

Harassment Claim

To establish a claim of harassment a complainant must show that: (1) they belong to a statutorily
protected class; (2) they were subjected to harassment in the form of unwelcome verbal or physical
conduct involving the protected class; (3) the harassment complained of was based on their
statutorily protected class; (4) the harassment affected a term or condition of employment and/or
had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with the work environment and/or creating
an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982). Further,
the incidents must have been "sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of
[complainant's] employment and create an abusive working environment." Harris v. Forklift
Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The harasser's conduct should be evaluated from the
objective viewpoint of a reasonable person in the victim's circumstances. Enforcement Guidance
on Harris v. Forklift Systems Inc., EEOC Notice No. 915.002 at 6 (Mar. 8, 1994).

Complainant has not established a prima facie case of harassment. Specifically, Complainant has
not established, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the Agency’s actions were based on his
protected classes. Complainant asserts that he was treated differently than C1 (female, Caucasian)
by management with respect to breaks, tasks etc.. Some of Complainant’s co-workers also testified
that C1 was treated better than other staff. However, we note that one of Complainant’s co-workers
(Caucasian, female, no prior EEO activity) (C2) also stated that management treated C1 better than
her. Report of Investigation (ROI), Exhibit (Ex.) 7-12. Specifically, C2 stated that “my rotation
to the heart station was moved forward out of sequence to accommodate [C1’s] call week. [C1]
did not work as many weeks as other staff members did in the heart station.” ROI Ex. 7-12 at 9.
Because C2 shares the same protected classes as C1 and asserts that management also treated C1
better than her, this testimony contradicts Complainant’s argument that management treated him
differently based on his protected classes.
                                                 5                                     2019003346

The record reflects further that C1 had an on-the job-injury and had medical restrictions for a
period of time. S1 and S2 asserted that C1 had strict restrictions from her injury for a period of
time which management adhered to and which may have been perceived by other staff as if
management favored C1. 4 Supplemental Report of Investigation (Supp. ROI) at 41, 43. Thus,
management’s treatment of C1 may have stemmed from her past on-the-job injury and prior
medical restrictions. While management may have extended this treatment of C1 beyond the date
of her official restrictions, this does not establish that Agency management subjected Complainant
to discrimination based on sex, national origin, and/or reprisal for prior protected EEO activity.
See McClinton v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Request No. (05921032 (May 6, 1993) (finding
that employment decisions based on favoritism is not an EEO violation, as long as they are not
premised on an EEO protected class).

The record reflects that the Agency articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its
actions. For example, regarding claim (5), that S1 and S2 did not allow Complainant to attend
monthly EBP meetings. In an affidavit, S2 asserts that that these meetings occurred via email
calendar invitation to individual volunteer members, and that team members who volunteer for
needs will allow their committees should clearly communicate the dates and times to managers to
see if patient care priority needs will allow their absence. Report of Investigation (ROI) Ex. 7-2
at 16-17. S2 stated that Complainant was not on the calendar invitation for these meetings from
July 28, 2017 and onward and that he did not ask S1 or her permission to attend these meetings.
Id. S1 also asserted that Complainant did not ask him for time to attend these meetings. ROI Ex.
7-3 at 7. Complainant failed to establish that the Agency’s reasons were pretext for discrimination
and/or retaliation.

Constructive Discharge Claim

The central question in a constructive discharge case is whether the employer, through its unlawful
discriminatory behavior, made the employee's working conditions so difficult that any reasonable
person in the employee's position would feel compelled to resign. Carmon-Coleman v. Dep’t of
Def., EEOC Appeal No. 07A00003 (Apr. 17, 2002). The Commission has established three
elements which a complainant must prove to substantiate a claim of constructive discharge: (1) a
reasonable person in the complainant's position would have found the working conditions
intolerable; (2) conduct that constituted discrimination against the complainant created the
intolerable working conditions; and (3) the complainant's involuntary resignation resulted from
the intolerable working conditions. See Walch v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Request No. 05940688
(Apr. 13, 1995).

We do not find that Complainant established his constructive discharge claim. The record reflects
that Complainant submitted his resignation to the Agency. Specifically, Complainant initially
submitted his resignation after the altercation with C1 on September 15, 2017 via email. 5 ROI Ex.
7-24. He subsequently informed S2 that he was retracting his September 15, 2017 resignation and
wanted to give it more thought per S2’s suggestion. Id. On September 24, 2017, Complainant
submitted his resignation again. ROI Ex. 7-24. While Complainant asserts, in his EEO complaint,
that he resigned because the Agency was issuing him a proposed removal which was
discriminatory 6 and due to the hostile work environment, we find that Complainant has not
established that the Agency issued him a proposed removal and as discussed above, Complainant
has not established that he was subjected to a hostile work environment . We acknowledge that
the EEO Counselor’s Report lists the proposed removal as one of several documents received by
the EEO Counselor. ROI Ex. 2-1 at 4. However, the record does not contain a copy of the proposed
removal. In addition, S1 and S2 assert that after the incident between Complainant and C1,
Complainant did not return to work and that they were unable to conduct a fact-finding. Supp.
ROI at 40-42. Thus, S1 and S2 assert that they did not issue discipline to Complainant. 7 Id.

We acknowledge, as set forth above, that the record contains some conflicting evidence with
respect to certain incidents. However, the record reflects that Complainant, subsequent to the
Agency’s supplemental investigation, requested a final agency decision rather than a hearing by
an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). As a result of Complainant’s decision not to elect a hearing
before an AJ, we do not have the benefit of an AJ’s credibility determinations or an AJ overseeing
the further development of the record. Complainant bears the burden to prove, by a preponderance
of the evidence, that the alleged discrimination occurred. When the evidence is at best equipoise,
Complainant fails to meet that burden. See Brand v. Dep’t Of Agriculture. EEOC Appeal No.
0120102187 (Aug. 23, 2012).

Accordingly, we AFFIRM the Agency’s final decision finding no discrimination.
