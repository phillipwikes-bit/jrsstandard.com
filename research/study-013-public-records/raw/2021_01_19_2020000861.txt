                    DECISION ON REQUEST FOR RECONSIDERATION

The Agency timely requested that the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC or
Commission) reconsider its compensatory decision in EEOC Appeal No. 0120181473
(September 19, 2019). For the reasons that follow, the Commission DENIES the Agency’s
request for reconsideration for failure to meet the regulatory criteria.

                                        BACKGROUND

On September 22, 2010, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging that the Agency
discriminated against him on the bases of race (Asian), national origin (Taiwan), age (61), and
reprisal for prior protected EEO activity when, on September 7, 2010, he was not selected for a
GS-13 Statistician position. At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided
Complainant with a copy of the report of investigation and notice of his right to request a hearing
before an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). Complainant timely requested a hearing.

On August 29, 2011, the AJ assigned to the case granted the Agency’s motion for summary
judgment, finding that Complainant was not subjected to discrimination as alleged. The Agency
subsequently issued a final order adopting the AJ’s decision.

On appeal, the Commission reversed the Agency’s final order and found that Complainant
established that his nonselection was based on reprisal. EEOC Appeal No. 0120120184 (Aug. 6,
2015). The Commission ordered various remedies, including ordering the Agency to offer
Complainant the Statistician position or a substantially similar position, to pay Complainant back
pay and benefits with interest, and to conduct a supplemental investigation into Complainant’s
entitlement to compensatory damages and attorney’s fees and costs. The Commission
subsequently denied the Agency’s request for reconsideration. EEOC Request No. 0520160050
(Feb. 2, 2016).

On June 23, 2016, the Agency filed a request to vacate the Commission’s decisions in Appeal
No. 0120120184 and Request No. 0520160050. The basis for the request was the Agency’s
discovery of a November 15, 2010, global settlement agreement that resolved matters including
all of Complainant’s pending EEO complaints filed with the Agency. On May 2, 2017, the
Commission docketed a petition for enforcement to examine the Agency’s compliance with the
Order set forth in Appeal No. 0120120184 and Request No. 0520160050. The Commission
granted the petition for enforcement and denied the Agency’s request to vacate. EEOC Petition
No. 0420170020 (Nov. 22, 2017). In its decision, the Commission found that the Agency had
waived its untimely raised argument that the November 2010 settlement agreement barred the
Commission from ordering relief by not raising it earlier in the EEO process and restated the
Order set forth in Appeal No. 0120120184 and Request No. 0520160050.

On February 22, 2018, the Agency issued its final decision on compensatory damages.
Complainant appealed this final decision to the Commission. In a decision modifying the
Agency’s compensatory damages award, the Commission again found that the Agency waived
the argument that the matter was precluded by the 2010 settlement agreement by not raising the
argument during the hearing stage, during the processing of Appeal No. 0120120184, or in its
subsequent request for reconsideration. EEOC Appeal No. 0120181473 (Sept. 19, 2019). The
Agency subsequently filed the instant request for reconsideration.

                 CONTENTIONS ON REQUEST FOR RECONSIDERATION

On request for reconsideration, the Agency contends that Complainant is not entitled to a
compensatory damages award of any amount because Complainant settled the underlying EEO
complaint when he signed the November 15, 2010, global settlement agreement. According to
the Agency, the Commission did not have jurisdiction to award damages because the settlement
agreement was valid and binding. The Agency also argues that Complainant has abused the EEO
process.

In response to the Agency’s request for reconsideration, Complainant argues that the Agency’s
request for reconsideration is an invalid attempt to relitigate matters addressed by the

                                  ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

EEOC Regulations provide that the Commission may, in its discretion, grant a request to
reconsider any previous Commission decision issued pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a), where
the requesting party demonstrates that: (1) the appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous
interpretation of material fact or law; or (2) the appellate decision will have a substantial impact
on the policies, practices, or operations of the agency. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c). After
reviewing the previous decision and the entire record, the Commission finds that the request fails
to meet the criteria of 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c), and it is the decision of the Commission to deny
the request for reconsideration of the decision in Appeal No. 0120181473. The Agency’s request
for reconsideration expresses disagreement with the compensatory damages decision and
reiterates arguments previously raised and considered on appeal. We emphasize that a request for
reconsideration is not an appeal to the Commission. See Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Ch. 9, § VII.A (Aug. 5,
2015). Rather, a reconsideration request is an opportunity to demonstrate that the appellate
decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or law or will have a
substantial impact on the policies, practices, or operations of the Agency.

The Agency argues that Complainant is not entitled to compensatory damages because he signed
the November 15, 2010, global settlement agreement. The Agency also contends that, because of
the settlement agreement, the Commission does not have the authority to award compensatory
damages.

In a number of decisions, the Commission has found that the dismissal of claims was appropriate
where there was an applicable settlement agreement. See, e.g., Lyle S. v. U.S. Postal Serv.,
EEOC Appeal No. 2019002466 (June 13, 2019) (dismissing settled claims pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.107(a)(5) as moot); Green v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120101695 (Aug. 9,
2010) (settlement agreement rendered claims moot); Johnson v. Tennessee Valley Authority;
EEOC Appeal No. 0120092209 (Sep. 15, 2009) (settlement agreement rendered complaint
subject to dismissal under 29 C.F.R. § 1614.107(a)(1) for failure to state a claim); Tittle v. Dep’t
of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 01A44708 (April 13, 2005) (after signing settlement
agreement, complainant lacked standing, and his claim failed to state a claim); Hedman v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A32743 (Sep. 23, 2003) (settled claim failed to state a claim).
                                                 4                                     2020000861

However, even assuming for the purposes of this decision that the November 15, 2010, global
settlement agreement applied to the EEO complaint at issue in the instant request for
reconsideration, 2 we decline to find that the complaint should be dismissed as moot or for failure
to state a claim. In the cases cited above, the existence of a settlement agreement was raised prior
to a finding of liability by the Commission. In the instant case, the Agency did not disclose the
existence of the global settlement agreement until June 23, 2016, well after the Commission had
found the Agency liable for discrimination and denied the Agency’s first request for
reconsideration. The Agency also subsequently issued its February 21, 2018, decision awarding
Complainant $96,976.81 in compensatory damages and its August 10, 2018, decision awarding
Complainant reasonable attorney’s fees and costs. Because the Agency did not raise the 2010
settlement agreement until after the Commission found that the agency had subjected
Complainant to discrimination, we find that equitable considerations weigh against dismissing
the underlying EEO complaint and that Complainant is still entitled to compensatory damages,
attorney’s fees, and other relief.

Although generally a valid, binding settlement agreement would be grounds for dismissing the
EEO complaint, under the specific circumstances of this case, the equitable doctrine of laches
precludes dismissing the complaint and vacating the Commission’s prior decisions. Under
laches, if a party neglects to assert rights against an adverse party over a period of time, the
delaying party can be precluded from asserting the neglected rights. Laches generally requires
proof of two elements: (1) lack of diligence by a party; and (2) proof of prejudice to the adverse
party. See Costello v. United States, 365 U.S. 265, 282 (1961). Proof of prejudice includes
consideration of whether the adverse party changed their position or detrimentally relied upon
the party that delayed asserting its rights. See Fulps v. City of Springfield, Tenn., 715 F.2d 1088,
1096 (6th Cir. 1983). The Commission has consistently held that a complainant must act with
due diligence in the pursuit of his claim or the doctrine of laches may apply. See O’Dell v. Dep’t
of Health and Human Serv., EEOC Request No. 05901130 (Dec. 27, 1990); see also Elayne C. v.
Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2020001137 (Feb. 14, 2020); Fonda M. v. Dep’t of
Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 2019005651 (Feb. 6, 2020). The Commission has also found that
laches can apply where an agency neglects to timely assert a defense. See Bornemann v. Dep’t of
Defense, EEOC Request No. 05890989 (Dec. 21, 1989).

Here, we find that laches applies and that equitable considerations weigh against vacating our
decisions finding that Complainant was subjected to discrimination and ordering relief. In the
instant case, there was a nearly six-year delay in asserting that the complaint was settled, and the
Agency did so only after the Commission had found the Agency liable for discrimination and
ordered the Agency to award compensatory damages and other relief. The Agency contends that
it was not aware of the settlement agreement because the Agency does not maintain a central
repository for settlement agreements.

We find that the lack of a recordkeeping system to facilitate discovery of relevant settlement
agreements involving EEO matters shows that the Agency did not act with due diligence when it
failed to raise the settlement agreement until this matter had been litigated for years.

We further find that Complainant was prejudiced by the Agency’s delay. According to the
record, Complainant was pro se when he filed his EEO complaint on September 22, 2010, during
the investigation, during the hearing process, and when he filed EEOC Appeal No. 0120120184
on October 3, 2011. 3 Complainant did not hire an attorney until after the Commission found that
he had been subjected to discrimination by the Agency and ordered relief. Even after the Agency
produced the settlement agreement, Complainant continued to incur litigation costs based on his
detrimental reliance on the Agency’s actions, and the Agency recognized Complainant as a
prevailing party by awarding him compensatory damages in its February 22, 2018, decision and
by awarding him attorney’s fees and costs in its August 10, 2018, decision. We therefore find
that equitable considerations weigh in favor of Complainant, and we decline to vacate our prior
decisions finding discrimination and ordering relief, including compensatory damages, for
Complainant.

After reviewing the previous decision and the entire record, the Commission finds that the
Agency’s request fails to meet the criteria of 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c), and it is the decision of
the Commission to DENY the request. The decision in EEOC Appeal No. 0120181473 remains
the Commission's decision. There is no further right of administrative appeal on the decision of
the Commission on this request. The Agency shall comply with the ORDER as set forth below.

            IMPLEMENTATION OF THE COMMISSION’S DECISION (K0719)

Under 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c) and § 1614.502, compliance with the Commission’s corrective
action is mandatory. Within seven (7) calendar days of the completion of each ordered
corrective action, the Agency shall submit via the Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP)
supporting documents in the digital format required by the Commission, referencing the
compliance docket number under which compliance was being monitored. Once all compliance
is complete, the Agency shall submit via FedSEP a final compliance report in the digital format
required by the Commission. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). The Agency’s final report must
contain supporting documentation when previously not uploaded, and the Agency must send a
copy of all submissions to the Complainant and his/her representative.

If the Agency does not comply with the Commission’s order, the Complainant may petition the
Commission for enforcement of the order. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(a). The Complainant also has
the right to file a civil action to enforce compliance with the Commission’s order prior to or
following an administrative petition for enforcement. See 29 C.F.R. §§ 1614.407, 1614.408, and
29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(g). Alternatively, the Complainant has the right to file a civil action on the
underlying complaint in accordance with the paragraph below entitled “Right to File a Civil
Action.” 29 C.F.R. §§ 1614.407 and 1614.408. A civil action for enforcement or a civil action
on the underlying complaint is subject to the deadline stated in 42 U.S.C. 2000e-16(c) (1994 &
Supp. IV 1999). If the Complainant files a civil action, the administrative processing of the
complaint, including any petition for enforcement, will be terminated. See 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.409.

Failure by an agency to either file a compliance report or implement any of the orders set forth in
this decision, without good cause shown, may result in the referral of this matter to the Office of
Special Counsel pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(f) for enforcement by that agency.
