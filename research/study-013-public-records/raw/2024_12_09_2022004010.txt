                                DECISION

Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a),
from the Agency’s June 21, 2022, final decision concerning the remedies for
his equal employment opportunity (EEO) complaint alleging employment
discrimination in violation of Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973
(Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 791 et seq. For the following
reasons, the Commission MODIFIES the Agency’s final decision and
REMANDS the matter for further action.

                            ISSUES PRESENTED

The issues presented are whether the Agency properly awarded Complainant
$4,000.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory damages; $7,897.50 in attorney’s
fees; and $299.57 in costs.

                                BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a
Supervisory Border Patrol Agent at the Agency’s Brian A. Terry Station,
Tucson Sector in Bisbee, Arizona.

On March 11, 2016, Complainant filed an EEO complaint (“Complaint 1”)
alleging that the Agency discriminated against him based on his national
origin, and in reprisal for prior protected EEO activity, for nine claims such as
an incident when a management official called the supervisors “lazy” during
a meeting. Complainant filed another EEO complaint (“Complaint 2”) on
March 2, 2017, with two claims based on disability (perceived - unspecified),
and in reprisal for prior protected EEO activity, when he learned that he was
reported to the Office of Professional Responsibility for alleged abuse of
prescription medication; and when on April 26, 2017, Complainant’s medical
information was disclosed in an email addressed to 118 recipients.

At the conclusion of the investigations, the Agency provided Complainant
with copies of the reports of investigations and notices of his right to request
a hearing before an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). Complainant timely
requested hearings. An AJ issued a joint decision without a hearing finding
no discrimination as alleged. Complainant appealed.

The Commission affirmed the findings of no discrimination for the claims in
Complaint 1 and the claim in Complaint 2 related to the report of alleged
prescription drug abuse. However, the appellate decision found a violation of
the Rehabilitation Act for the unauthorized disclosure of Complainant’s
medical information. The email in question stated that Complainant had
been transported to a medical center for a rapid heartbeat and tightness in
his chest and left hand. The Agency official who sent the email admitted that
not everyone on the email had a need to know, and that he mistakenly used
the wrong email list. The Agency was ordered to take remedial action,
including conducting a supplemental investigation into Complainant’s claims
for compensatory damages and attorney’s fees. Alan N. v. Dep’t of
Homeland Sec., EEOC Appeal No. 2020001528 (Apr. 8, 2021); request for
recon. denied, EEOC Request No. 2021003175 (Dec. 20, 2021).

On or about June 21, 2022, the Agency issued a final decision for
Complainant’s compensatory damages and attorney’s fees.
                                       3                          2022004010

The Agency found that Complainant did not establish his entitlement for past
or future pecuniary compensatory damages because he did not prove a
nexus between his claimed expenses and the inadvertent release of his
medical information.

Complainant requested $75,000.00 for non-pecuniary compensatory
damages, but the Agency determined that $4,000.00 was more appropriate.
The Agency noted that Complainant provided a list of medical complaints,
such as palpitations and anxiety disorder, but he submitted no supporting
medical note or diagnoses. The Agency provided examples of past
Commission decisions awarding between $2,000.00 and $10,000.00 in
similar cases. For the cases with higher awards, the complainants submitted
detailed medical documentation, including evidence of a diagnosis. Here,
Complainant only provided evidence of doctors’ visits for various reasons
over four years, but there was no medical documentation to connect any
visit to the Agency’s disclosure of medical information. The Agency found
that statements from Complainant and his witness supported an award of
$4,000.00.

The Agency considered Complainant’s request for attorney’s fees of
$29,314.80, billed for 86.9 hours. The Agency did not challenge the
attorney’s hourly rate, but it noted that Complainant’s attorney did not
distinguish between her work for the two claims in Complaint 2. For
example, the itemized bill showed a charge of 1.5 hours on July 10, 2018,
for “review of [report of investigation] for discovery requests.” Since the line
items were too general to permit a meaningful line-by-line review, the
Agency adopted a reduction in hours approach, similar to that in Wilda M. v.
Department of Homeland Security, EEOC Appeal Number 0120142660
(December 2, 2016), in which the Commission reduced the attorney’s fees
by 10%, despite her success in only one of 56 claims. Here, the Agency
found that 24 hours, or approximately 28% of the 86.9 hours claimed, was
reasonable based on the degree of success. Notably, the sole successful
claim was uncontested and straightforward and not on equal footing to the
unsuccessful and litigated claim. The Agency also determined that the costs
submitted for two deposition transcripts totaling $1,069.90 should be
reduced to $299.57, or 28% of the total.

The Agency concluded by awarding $4,000.00 in non-pecuniary
compensatory damages; $7,897.50 in attorney’s fees; and $299.57 in costs.

The instant appeal followed.

                         CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

Through his attorney, Complainant renews his request for $75,000.00 in
non-pecuniary compensatory damages. Complainant states that he has a
heart condition, which was exacerbated by the email, and that the emotional
toll caused his divorce. He offers Commission decisions awarding $8,000.00
through $180,000.00 for his request. Complainant also repeats his claim for
$29,314.80 in attorney’s fees and costs.

The Agency opposed Complainant’s appeal. It asserts that a significant
reduction in attorney’s fees was warranted because there was a failure to
adequately separate and request fees for the sole successful claim. The
Agency also noted that Complainant only provided an unsworn declaration;
additional statements written on his behalf; a list of counseling sessions; the
first page of his divorce decree; and a one-page letter from a friend for his
request for non-pecuniary compensatory damages. While Complainant
claimed that he suffered extreme humiliation and that unidentified
individuals approached him about the incident, he no longer worked at that
duty station as of March 30, 2019. The Agency provided additional
Commission decisions awarding $2,000.00 or $2,500.00 for similar harms to
justify its determination that $4,000.00 was an appropriate award. The
Agency requests that the Commission affirm its final decision.

                           STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29
C.F.R. § 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by
the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110),
at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard
of review “requires that the Commission examine the record without regard
to the factual and legal determinations of the previous decision maker,” and
that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of record,
including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue
its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its
interpretation of the law”).

                                  ANALYSIS

Pursuant to section 102(a) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, a complainant
who establishes unlawful intentional discrimination under either Title VII of
the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., or
                                      5                          2022004010

Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 791
et seq. may receive compensatory damages for past and future pecuniary
losses (i.e., out-of-pocket expenses) and non-pecuniary losses (e.g., pain
and suffering, mental anguish) as part of this “make whole” relief. 42 U.S.C.
§ 1981a(b)(3).

As an initial matter, Complainant did not appeal the Agency’s decision to not
award any pecuniary compensatory damages. As such, we find no reason to
disturb the Agency’s decision for no pecuniary compensatory damages.

Non-Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

Non-pecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise
quantification, i.e., emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental
anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to professional standing, injury to
character and reputation, injury to credit standing, and loss of health. See
EEOC Notice No. 915.302, Enforcement Guidance on Compensatory and
Punitive Damages Available Under Section 102 of the Civil Rights Act of
1991, at 10 (July 14, 1992). There is no precise formula for determining the
amount of damages for non-pecuniary losses except that the award should
reflect the nature and severity of the harm, and the duration or expected
duration of the harm. See Loving v. Dep’t of the Treasury, EEOC Appeal No.
01955789 (Aug. 29, 1997). Non-pecuniary compensatory damages are
designed to remedy the harm caused by the discriminatory event rather
than to punish an agency for the discriminatory action. Furthermore,
compensatory damages should not be motivated by passion or prejudice or
be “monstrously excessive” standing alone but should be consistent with the
amounts awarded in similar cases. See Ward-Jenkins v. Dep’t of the Interior,
EEOC Appeal No. 01961483 (Mar. 4, 1999).

Evidence from a health care provider or other expert is not a mandatory
prerequisite for recovery of compensatory damages for emotional harm. See
Lawrence v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01952288 (Apr. 18, 1996)
(citing Carle v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan. 5,
1993)). Objective evidence of compensatory damages can include
statements from complainants concerning their emotional pain or suffering,
inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to
professional standing, injury to character or reputation, injury to credit
standing, loss of health, and any other non-pecuniary losses that are
incurred as a result of the discriminatory conduct. Id.
                                      6                           2022004010

Statements from others including family members, friends, health care
providers, other counselors (including clergy) could address the outward
manifestations or physical consequences of emotional distress, including
sleeplessness, anxiety, stress, depression, marital strain, humiliation,
emotional distress, loss of self-esteem, excessive fatigue, or a nervous
breakdown. Id. Complainants’ own testimonies, along with the
circumstances of a particular case, can suffice to sustain their burden in this
regard. Id. The more inherently degrading or humiliating the defendant’s
action is, the more reasonable it is to infer that a person would suffer
humiliation or distress from that action. Id. The absence of supporting
evidence, however, may affect the amount of damages appropriate in
specific cases. Id.

An award of non-pecuniary compensatory damages should reflect the extent
to which the agency’s discriminatory action directly or proximately caused
the harm as well as the extent to which other factors also caused the harm.
Johnson v. Dep’t of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No. 01961812 (June 18,
1998). It is the complainants’ burden to provide objective evidence in
support of their claims and proof linking the damages to the alleged
discrimination. Papas v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01930547 (Mar.
17, 1994); Mims v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01933956 (Nov. 23,
1993).

In his declaration, Complainant responded that he suffered “heart issues”
from the stress after his medical information was released. He also stated
that he could no longer meet with other employees due to fear of how he
was perceived based on his medical information. Complainant further
asserted that he suffered from “emotional distress” resulting in his divorce.
Complainant supplied a document showing that his divorce decree was filed
on September 9, 2020. Report of Investigation (ROI) at 25-6, 35.

The record also contains an undated and unsigned document, presumably
authored by Complainant, noting additional harms such as an anxiety
disorder; feelings of uncertainty; and isolation from others, including his
wife. ROI at 31-2. A friend submitted a statement describing changes in
Complainant starting in May 2017, such as stress, frustration, and anger,
and he corroborated that Complainant became distant from his family. ROI
at 33. There is also a document noting dates of medical visits, starting on
April 28, 2017, through October 13, 2021. While Complainant’s initial visits
were for chest pain and heart-related issues, most of the visits were for
unrelated medical issues, such as colds. ROI at 36-40.
                                      7                          2022004010

The evidence supports that Complainant suffered from stress; anxiety; fear;
frustration; anger; an exacerbation of his heart condition; and damage to his
relationships with friends and family members. However, Complainant did
not provide a duration of his harms, and the Agency did not specify the
duration it considered for its $4,000.00 award. Complainant’s document
listing his medical visits shows that he only sought medical care for an
exacerbation of his heart issues and anxiety through September 5, 2017.
ROI at 39-40. Complainant also provided a document showing that he
contacted the Employee Assistance Program on May 1, 2017, and he
attested that he did not seek further counseling after May 1, 2017. ROI at
34, 31. The Agency’s discriminatory action occurred on April 26, 2017, and
we find that it is reasonable to conclude that the duration of Complainant’s
harms was approximately four months.

In support of his claim for $75,000.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory
damages, Complainant cited to Commission decisions awarding between
$8,000.00 and $180,000.00. However, he did not show how the cases were
similar. For example, in a decision awarding $75,000.00, Complainant noted
that the agency’s reassignment exacerbated the complainant’s asthma,
resulting in physical and emotional harm. Carpenter v. Dep’t of Agriculture,
EEOC Appeal No. 01945652 (July 17, 1995). A review of this case shows
that the award was due to a long pattern of the agency’s actions, resulting in
harms that included damaged relationships; long periods of anger and self-
pity; and feelings of helplessness and embarrassment. Evidence showed that
the complainant was subjected to discrimination and harassment for
approximately four years. In Chere S. v. General Services Administration,
EEOC Appeal Number 0720180012 (November 30, 2018), Complainant
noted that the complainant’s harms lasted more than six years to support
the award of $180,000.00. We find that the durations of harms were
significantly greater and not comparable to Complainant’s situation, and they
do not support his request for $75,000.00.

While the Agency relied upon five Commission decisions with non-pecuniary
compensatory damages awards ranging from $2,000.00 and $10,000.00 in
the final decision, and it offered five cases on appeal awarding $2,000.00 or
$2,500.00, most of the cases do not discuss the duration of the harms.

Based on more recent Commission cases, we find that an award of
$10,000.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory damages is more appropriate
when considering the nature, severity, and duration of Complainant’s harms,
and it is not monstrously excessive standing alone.
                                     8                         2022004010

For example, in Velva B. v. Department of Agriculture, EEOC Appeal Number
2023001882 (May 16, 2024), $10,000.00 in non-pecuniary compensatory
damages was awarded for four months of harms such as stress; feelings of
embarrassment and helplessness; anxiety; depression; sleep issues; and
withdrawal from family members and impact on marriage. Another
complainant was awarded $10,000.00 for four months of harms including
stress; anxiety; feelings of hopelessness and dread; depression; migraines;
and high blood pressure in Leora R. v. Department of Health and Human
Services, EEOC Appeal Number 2020004621 (April 11, 2022). Accordingly,
we MODIFY the Agency’s award for non-pecuniary compensatory damages to
$10,000.00.

Attorney’s Fees

By federal regulation, an agency is required to award attorney’s fees and
costs for the successful processing of an EEO complaint in accordance with
existing case law and regulatory standards. EEOC Regulation 29 C.F.R. §
1614.501(e)(1)(ii). To determine the proper amount of the fee, a lodestar
amount is reached by calculating the number of hours reasonably expended
by the attorney on the complaint multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate.
Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886 (1984); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424
(1983).

There is a strong presumption that the number of hours reasonably
expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate, the lodestar, represents a
reasonable fee, but this amount may be reduced or increased in
consideration of the degree of success, quality of representation, and long
delay caused by the agency. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.501(e)(2)(ii)(B). The
circumstances under which the lodestar may be adjusted are extremely
limited, and are set forth in EEO MD-110, Chap. 11 § VI.F. A fee award may
be reduced: in cases of limited success; where the quality of representation
was poor; the attorney’s conduct resulted in undue delay or obstruction of
the process; or where settlement likely could have been reached much
earlier, but for the attorney’s conduct. Id. The party seeking to adjust the
lodestar, either up or down, has the burden of justifying the deviation. Id.

All hours reasonably spent in processing the complaint are compensable, but
the number of hours should not include excessive, redundant, or otherwise
unnecessary hours. EEO MD-110, at Chap. 11 § VI.F. The Commission has
ruled that, when reviewing fee petitions which contain many excessive,
redundant, unnecessary, or inadequately documented expenditures of time,
in lieu of engaging in a line-by-line analysis of each charge claimed, the
                                      9                          2022004010

Commission may calculate the number of hours compensable by applying an
across-the-board reduction to the number of hours requested. See Bernard
v. Dep't of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 01966861 (July 17, 1998).

Complainant’s attorney did not offer any arguments to challenge the
Agency’s decision regarding attorney’s fees. While the Agency provided an
extensive analysis for the reduction in fees, Complainant simply requests
$29,314.80, without explanation for how the Agency erred in its
determination to award $7,897.50. Complainant’s attorney did not refute
that the prevailing claim was straightforward and uncontested and not on
equal footing as the other litigated claim. We find that the Agency’s decision
to take an across-the-board reduction to award 28% of the requested
attorney’s fees and costs was reasonable based on the degree of success
and the inadequately documented expenditure of the attorney’s time.

The submitted billing statement reveals vague descriptions, such as “draft
initial complaint: final draft and exhibits” on January 10, 2020. The
Commission has held that a fee petition must be sufficiently detailed to
permit a factfinder to properly determine if hours were reasonably expended
and to attribute work performed for successful work and discount work
performed for unsuccessful claims. See Jane H. v. Dep’t of the Air Force,
EEOC Appeal No. 2022000355 (Dec. 19, 2022); Rigoberto A. v.
Environmental Protection Agency, EEOC Appeal No. 2021002128 (July 27,
2022). We agree with the Agency that Complainant’s attorney did not
provide sufficient details in her fee petition to support the total hours
claimed. Further, Complainant did not dispute the awarded costs. As such,
we AFFIRM the attorney’s fee award of $7,897.50 and $299.57 in costs.

                                CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions on appeal,
including those not specifically addressed herein, we MODIFY the Agency’s
final decision to award Complainant $10,000.00 in non-pecuniary
compensatory damages; and we AFFIRM the Agency’s decision to award
$7,897.50 in attorney’s fees and $299.57 in costs.
