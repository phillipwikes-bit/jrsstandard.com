                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

Complainant’s Request for Default Judgment

At the outset, we address Complainant’s claim that default judgment be issued against the
Agency for its delay in issuing a final decision. We note that our regulations require agency
action in a timely manner at many points in the EEO process. Tammy S. v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC
Appeal No. 0120084008 (June 6, 2014). Compliance with these timeframes is not optional; as
the Commission stated in Royal v. Dep't of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Request No. 0520080052
(Sept. 25, 2009), “the Commission has the inherent power to protect its administrative process
from abuse by either party and must ensure that agencies, as well as complainants, abide by its
regulations.” Because of the length of time it can take to process a federal sector EEO
complaint, any delays in complying with the time frames in the regulations can impact the
outcome of the complainant's claims. Id.

EEOC Regulations provide that an agency shall issue the final decision within 60 days of
receiving notification that a complainant has requested an immediate decision from the agency,
or within 60 days of the end of the 30-day period for the complainant to request a hearing or an
immediate final decision where the complainant has not requested either a hearing or a decision.
29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b). In the present case, Complainant notified the Agency via email on July
2, 2021, that she was electing a final Agency decision. 2 Thus, a final decision should have been
issued by August 31, 2021. The Agency did not issue a final decision until February 15, 2022.
Thus, we find the Agency delayed a total of 168 days in issuing a final decision.

While Complainant cited Irvin M. v. Dep’t of Homeland Security, EEOC Appeal No.
0120170498 (April 25, 2019), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request No. 2019003340 (Oct. 23,
2019) in support of her request for a default judgment, we find that case distinguishable from the
present case. In Irvin M. the Agency provided no explanation for its delay. In contrast, in the
present case the Agency explained that it had been unable to timely issue a final decision in this
complaint because of an extensive backlog and a shortage of staff needed to process the cases.
The Agency provided a declaration from the Acting Director, Office of Employment
Discrimination Complaint Adjudication (OEDCA), who held that position since May 1, 2021.
OEDCA is responsible for issuing final decisions for the Agency. The Acting Director explained
that since FY19, OEDCA has experienced a sustained increase in case receipts, which resulted in
a significant backlog of cases pending adjudication. The Acting Director also noted that more
recently, disruption and delays occurred in FY20 when transitioning to 100% telework and
electronic case processing during the pandemic. She stated that early in the pandemic it
experienced significant disruption to the Interagency Agreement (IAA) which it entered into with
another Agency to draft final decisions. She also noted that the Director retired on April 30,
2021, which caused disruption, and impacted the ability to hire new staff to cover attrition.
Further, she noted OEDCA experienced five staff departures which impacted the number of final
decisions being issued in the latter half of FY21 and FY22, as a new Director has not yet been
installed.

After a review of the record, although we find that the Agency failed to timely issue its final
Agency decision, we find that the Agency did not act in a manner to warrant a default judgment
sanction. See, e.g., Anthony M. v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Appeal No. 2019003380 (Sept.
22, 2020) (no default judgment when agency decision was two years late); Josefina L. v. Soc.
Sec. Admin., 0120142023 (July 19, 2016), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request No.
0520170108 (Feb. 9, 2017) (finding that the agency's 571-day delay in issuing the decision did
not warrant sanctions, as complainant did not show she was prejudiced by the delay); Abe K. v.
Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141252 (Nov. 4, 2016) (declining to sanction an agency
that issued a decision after approximately 326 days when complainant failed to show that he was
prejudiced by the delay); Jocelyn R. v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 0120152852 (Mar. 11,
2016) (citing Complainant v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A55147 (May 12, 2006)
(declining to sanction an agency that issued a decision after approximately 371 days)).

Upon review, we find no evidence that the Agency’s delay in this case was attributed to
contumacious conduct or bad faith. Further, we find that Complainant failed to show that she
was prejudiced by the Agency's delay. Thus, under the specific circumstances present in this
case, we do not find the Agency’s delay warrants any sanction. We find that the Agency’s
detailed explanation for the delay in issuing its final decision shows that the Agency’s delay does
not reflect negatively on the Agency’s support for the integrity of the EEO process.

Complainant’s Claims of Discrimination

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part
1614, at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review
“requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal
determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents,
statements, and testimony of record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the
parties, and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and
its interpretation of the law”).

A claim of disparate treatment is examined under the three-part analysis first enunciated in
McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). For a complainant to prevail, she
must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination by presenting facts that, if unexplained,
reasonably give rise to an inference of discrimination, i.e., that a prohibited consideration was a
factor in the adverse employment action. See McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802; Furnco
Constr. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567 (1978).

The burden then shifts to the agency to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its
actions. See Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine. 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Once the
agency has met its burden, the complainant bears the ultimate responsibility to persuade the fact
finder by a preponderance of the evidence that the agency acted on the basis of a prohibited
reason. See St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993).

Discrete claims 3 - 16, 22, and 25 - 28

In the present case, the Agency articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its actions
in claims 3 - 16, 22, and 25 - 28. Regarding claim 3, the Agency stated Complainant was denied
advanced FMLA leave due to her irregular attendance record. Regarding claims 7 and 13, the
Agency stated Complainant was required to use FMLA leave when she admittedly was tardy to

that therefore for every 80 hours she continued to accrue LWOP, VATAS (leave tracking
system) took away the annual leave and sick leave accrued in that pay period, which for
Complainant was eight hours of annual leave and four hours of sick leave.

Per Se Retaliation

The Commission has stated that adverse actions need not qualify as “ultimate employment
actions” or materially affect the terms and conditions of employment to constitute unlawful
retaliation, EEOC Compliance Manual, Section 8: Retaliation (May 20, 1998); Burlington
Northern and Santa Fe Railway Co. v White, 548 U.S. 53 (2006) (finding that the antiretaliation
provision protects individuals from a retaliatory action that a reasonable person would have
found “materially adverse,” which in the retaliation context means that the action might have
deterred a reasonable person from opposing discrimination or participating in the EEOC charge
process).

Comments that, on their face, discourage an employee from participating in the EEO process
violate the letter and spirit of the EEOC regulations and evidence a per se violation of the law.
Complainant v. Dep’t of the Army, EEOC Request No. 05970584 (October 8, 1998)
(complainant told that filing an EEO suit was the wrong way to go about getting a promotion);
Complainant v. Dep’t of Energy, EEOC Appeal No. 0120083727 (June 4, 2009), req. for recon.
denied, EEOC Request No. 0520090560 (August 21, 2009) (per se violation found when a labor
management specialist told complainant “as a friend” that his EEO claim would polarize the
office); Vincent v. United States Postal Service, EEOC Appeal No. 0120072908 (August 3,
2009), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request No. 0520090654 (December 16, 2010) (per se
violation found when supervisor, during an employee meeting, referenced that EEO complaints
                                                14                                     2022002223

had been filed and said “what goes around, comes around”). When a supervisor's behavior has a
potentially chilling effect on use of the EEO complaint process - the ultimate tool that employees
have to enforce equal employment opportunity - the behavior is a per se violation. Charlie K. v.
Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, EEOC Appeal No. 0120142315 (January 24,
2017), req. for recon. denied, EEOC Request No. 0520170235 (December 19, 2017).

Upon review of the record, we find that claims 20 and 21 could constitute per se reprisal.
Specifically, on September 4, 2020, Complainant met with S1 and S2 to express various
grievances about her job and S1. Complainant stated that during that meeting and in front of S1,
S2 asked her why she did not go find a new job. Complainant also stated that in front of S1
where S2 was asking Complainant about finding a new job, S2 asked if Complainant filed an
EEO complaint, and what was the status of it. Complainant stated that she responded, “I don’t
feel comfortable answering that question.” Complainant noted at that point S2 said, “It doesn’t
matter, they go for years, I’ve never seen any of them get resolved.”

We find there is a dispute as to whether S2 engaged in per se reprisal, and we remand the matter
for a supplemental investigation. In its final decision, regarding claim 20, the Agency found that
Complainant failed to demonstrate unlawful harassment or reprisal as to S2’s alleged comment
on September 4, 2020, that she should look for another job if she were unwilling to accept S1’s
supervisory oversight. Regarding claim 21, the Agency found the record failed to demonstrate
that S2 made any inappropriate comments about Complainant’s EEO activity during the
September 4, 2020 meeting. The Agency stated while Complainant asserted that S2 asked about
the status of her pending EEO complaint and stated the EEO process could take years to
complete, S2 denied making any such comments or discouraging Complainant in any EEO
activity. The record, however, reveals that when asked about the comments Complainant alleged
that S2 made regarding Complainant looking for another job S2 responded, “I recall those
allegations, but . . . that is not quite the same interpretation of what was said.” When asked about
the comments regarding warning Complainant that EEO cases can go on for years S2 responded,
“It wasn’t a warning. It was a conversation whereby I said … I have been told that EEO cases
can go on for a very long time, and I believe what I had said … have you heard anything about
your EEO complaint.” We find the Agency’s statement in its final decision that S2 denied
making such comments to be a mischaracterization of S2’s statement.

Also, the Agency claimed that Complainant presented no other evidence demonstrating S2
discouraged her or made any other inappropriate comments regarding her EEO activity and there
was no such evidence in the record. However, we note that the record contains an email
Complainant sent to S2 on September 4, 2020, describing that at the meeting that day with S1
present, S2 said, “If I am unhappy working there that I should consider looking for another job”
and that if “[S2] were me, that is what [S2] would do.” Complainant also stated in the email that
she felt uncomfortable with S2 asking if she filed an EEO case or if that was her intention. In the
email, Complainant noted that she responded by telling S2 that she was not going to answer that
question. In the email, Complainant stated that S2 then said that EEO cases can go on for years
and years.

Further, we note that in its final decision, the Agency stated that S1 denied that she witnessed
any inappropriate comments as alleged. Thus, the Agency stated that given the conflicting
statements of the parties, and with no basis to credit Complainant’s version over both S1 and
S2’s testimony, it could not be found that she demonstrated it was more likely than not that S2
made any inappropriate comments concerning her EEO activity. However, a review of the
record provided by the Agency on appeal does not contain a statement from S1 regarding claims
20 or 21. Complainant stated, and the Agency conceded in its final decision, the two statements
in claims 20 and 21 were made in front of S1. While the record contains an affidavit from S1
and a supplemental affidavit from S1, there is no indication S1 was specifically asked about
these two claims. Thus, we shall remand claims 20 and 21 for a supplemental investigation.

Hostile Work Environment (claims 1, 2, 17 - 21, 23, 24, and 29)

In considering whether the alleged actions, whether individually or collectively, constitute
harassment, the Commission notes that, in Harris v. Forklift Systems, the Supreme Court
reaffirmed the holding of Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57 (1986), that harassment
is actionable if it is sufficiently severe or pervasive that it results in an alteration of the
conditions of the complainant's employment. See EEOC Notice No. 915.002 (March 8, 1994),
Enforcement Guidance on Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc. at 3. To establish a claim of
harassment a complainant must show that: (1) she belongs to a statutorily protected class; (2) she
was subjected to unwelcome verbal or physical conduct involving the protected class; (3) the
harassment complained of was based on the statutorily protected class; (4) the harassment had
the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with her work performance and/or creating an
intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See McCleod v. Social Security Administration, EEOC Appeal No.
01963810 (August 5, 1999) (citing Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982)).

Furthermore, in assessing whether the complainant has set forth an actionable claim of
harassment, the conduct at issue must be viewed in the context of the totality of the
circumstances, considering, inter alia, the nature and frequency of offensive encounters and the
span of time over which the encounters occurred. See 29 C.F.R. § 1604.11(b); EEOC Policy
Guidance on Current Issues of Sexual Harassment, N 915 050, No. 137 (March 19, 1990); Cobb
v. Department of the Treasury, Request No. 05970077 (March 13, 1997). However, as noted by
the Supreme Court in Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 788 (1998): “simple
teasing, offhand comments, and isolated incidents (unless extremely serious) will not amount to
discriminatory changes in the “terms and conditions of employment.” The Court noted that such
conduct “must be both objectively and subjectively offensive, [such] that a reasonable person
would find [the work environment to be] hostile or abusive, and . . . that the victim in fact did
perceive to be so.” Id. See also Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 752 (1998);
Clark County School Dist. v. Breeden, 532 U.S. 268 (2001).

We find Complainant failed to show the Agency subjected her to a hostile work environment.
We note many of Complainant's harassment allegations include instances that can generally be
described as relating to personality conflicts and disagreements with managerial decisions.
                                               16                                    2022002223

 Regarding claim 1, Complainant alleged that from March 24, 2020 to April 27, 2020, S1
erroneously indicated that Complainant’s request for FMLA leave for Complainant’s own health
condition instead of family care. Even if S1 made an error in listing the reason for FMLA leave,
Complainant does not allege she was denied FMLA leave. Further, there is no evidence
