                                           DECISION

Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC or
Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s November 9, 2018 final
decision concerning her equal employment opportunity (EEO) complaint alleging employment
discrimination in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42

amended, 29 U.S.C. § 621 et seq. For the following reasons, the Commission MODIFIES the
Agency’s final decision.

                                        BACKGROUND

Complainant worked as a Human Resources (HR) Specialist, GS-0201-14, in the Agency’s HR
Office at its Headquarters in Washington, D.C. On November 2, 2017, Complainant filed an EEO
complaint in which she alleged that Agency discriminated against her on the bases of race
(African-American), sex (female), and age (69) when:

       1. Beginning on July 9, 2017 and continuing until her retirement in November 2017, the
          Acting HR Director, her second-line supervisor (S2), denied Complainant’s repeated
          requests to reclassify her position to reflect that she had been performing GS-15 level
          supervisory duties and was entitled to commensurate pay; and

Position Reclassification

Complainant averred that from August 2015 until December 2016, she performed the duties of
Chief of the Corporate Recruitment and Career Programs Division, and that when the position was
abolished in January 2017 pursuant to a reorganization, she continued to perform those duties until
she retired in November 2018. She claimed that while she served in this capacity, she planned,
directed, administered and coordinated the Agency’s nationwide recruitment and outreach
programs; managed a $4.7 million budget; and conducted mid-year and annual performance

                                   ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

Standard of Review

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b),
the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).
See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9,
§ VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of
record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).
                                                 11                                     2019001523

Disparate Treatment – Position Reclassification

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim such as this, Complainant must satisfy the three-part
evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411
U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Her first step would generally be to establish a prima facie case by
demonstrating that she was subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances that
would support an inference of discrimination. Furnco Const. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576
(1978). The prima facie inquiry may be dispensed in this case, however, since S2 and other
Agency officials articulated a legitimate and nondiscriminatory reason for not reclassifying
Complainant’s position description from GS-14 to GS-15. See U.S. Postal Service Bd. of
Governors v. Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 713-17 (1983). According to these officials, Complainant had
been a GS-14 team leader between August 2015 and her retirement in November 2017, and that
despite Complainant’s claims to the contrary, the position description change in question was for
a GS-14 supervisory position, not a GS-15 management position.

To ultimately prevail, Complainant must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the
Agency's explanation is a pretext for discrimination. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.,
530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000); St. Mary's Hon. Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993); Tex. Dep’t of
Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981). Pretext can be demonstrated by showing
such weaknesses, implausibilities, inconsistencies, incoherencies, or contradictions in the
Agency's proffered legitimate reasons for its action that a reasonable fact finder could rationally
find them unworthy of credence. Opare-Addo v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120060802
(Nov. 20, 2007), req. for recon. den. EEOC Request No. 0520080211 (May 30, 2008).

Without question, there is credible affidavit testimony from the CCSB that S2 may have followed
a practice that prevented African American employees of the HR Office from receiving promotions
unless white employees also received comparable promotions at approximately the same time. If
true, this would certainly constitute enough evidence to establish the existence of a discriminatory
motive on the part of S2. What is unclear, however, is whether Complainant was even entitled to
a promotion to GS-15 through a desk audit of her position as she claims and whether S2 was
instrumental in preventing her from receiving that desk audit. Put another way, Complainant must
not only establish the existence of a discriminatory motive on the part of S2 but also that S2 caused
the audit for a GS-15 position not to happen as a result of that motive. See e.g. Renee L. v. Dep’t
of Commerce, EEOC Appeal No. 0120141032 (Mar. 29, 2017), req. for recon. den. EEOC Request
No. 0520170339 (Aug. 8, 2017) (Complainant failed to present enough evidence to establish the
existence of a discriminatory *** motive on the part of the [responsible management official] in
connection with [emphasis added] the actions described in [Complainant’s EEO complaint]).

On this question, the record is ambiguous, at best. Complainant claimed that she had submitted
her request for a position reclassification through CW1, but CW1 denied that he received such a
request from Complainant. Furthermore, CW1, who was sympathetic towards Complainant in her
harassment claim against S2, averred that it was he, not Complainant, who had initiated the desk
audit, and that the reclassification was for a GS-14 supervisory position, not a GS-15 management
position.
                                                12                                     2019001523

S1 affirmed that he was unable to recall that Complainant had actually been performing GS-15
level duties, that Complainant had declined an opportunity to act in a GS-15 capacity as part of a
rotation, and that he knew of no employees in the HR office who had received a grade increase
based upon a position description reclassification. The DD also averred that Complainant did not
perform the duties of a GS-15 division chief. Moreover, none of the coworkers cited by
Complainant as comparatives had served as team leads under S2. One of them, CW5, got his GS-
15 position by successfully applying for that job as an outside candidate. Finally, the DD averred
that all HR actions involving positions at grades GS-14 and GS-15 required the approval of
officials who were above S2 in the organizational hierarchy, typically S3, as well as the
concurrence of the Executive Director.

As Complainant chose not to request a hearing, the Commission does not have the benefit of an
Administrative Judge's credibility determinations after a hearing. Therefore, the Commission can
only evaluate the facts based on the weight of the evidence presented. That evidence is not
sufficient to establish the necessary causal connection between S2’s allegedly unlawful motivation
and Complainant’s failure to have her position reclassified to grade GS-15.

Hostile Environment

To establish a claim of harassment Complainant must show that: (1) she belongs to a statutorily
protected class; (2) she was subjected to harassment in the form of unwelcome verbal or physical
conduct involving the protected class; (3) the harassment complained of was based on her
statutorily protected classes; (4) the harassment affected a term or condition of employment and/or
had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with the work environment and/or creating
an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982). Further,
the incidents must have been “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of
[complainant's] employment and create an abusive working environment.” Harris v. Forklift
Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993).

The antidiscrimination statutes are not civility codes. Rather, they forbid “only behavior so
objectively offensive as to alter the conditions of the victim's employment.” Oncale v. Sundowner
Offshore Serv., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 81 (1998). Therefore, to prove her harassment claim,
Complainant must establish that she was subjected to conduct that was either so severe or so
pervasive that a “reasonable person” in Complainant's position would have found the conduct to
be hostile or abusive. Complainant must also prove that the conduct was taken because of her
protected classes. Only if Complainant establishes both of those elements, hostility and motive,
will the question of Agency liability present itself.

Here, Complainant did not merely allege in general terms that S2 had treated her more negatively
than employees outside of her protected race, sex, and age groups. She identified specific incidents
that occurred between November 2016 and March 2017, including verbal attacks at staff meetings,
constant criticisms of her work, accusations of failing to properly implement programs, and
exclusions from meetings and communications critical to her job performance.
                                                  13                                     2019001523

All of these incidents had a cumulatively adverse effect upon Complainant’s morale and well-
being, to the point at which any reasonable person in her position would have found S2’s behavior
to be hostile and abusive.

As to S2’s motive, indicators of discriminatory animus include discriminatory statements or past
personal treatment attributable to those responsible for the personnel action that led to the filing of
the complaint, comparative or statistical data revealing differences in treatment across various
protected-group lines, unequal application of Agency policy, deviations from standard procedures
without explanation or justification, or inadequately explained inconsistencies in the evidentiary
record. Mellissa F. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120141697 (Nov. 12, 2015). In
addition to her own affidavit testimony, Complainant presented sworn statements from three
current and former coworkers, all of whom attested to S2’s discriminatory animus in how he
treated Complainant and other African American women. According to the CCSB, S2 opined
directly to her that African Americans were incompetent and warned that he would not hire them
even if they were interviewed. She corroborated Complainant’s affidavit testimony that S2 had
excluded her from important HR meetings and left her off email lists. She also averred that S2
treated white employees more favorably than black employees by giving them career development
details, promotions, and tuition payments that he did not give to black employees. The CCSB even
went so far as to call S2 a “black racist.” Complainant’s former subordinate averred that she was
hired despite S2’s desire not to do so. Additionally, CW1 affirmed that he had warned S2 on
several occasions that his actions would invite legal scrutiny and that S2 had disregarded his
warnings. All of these witnesses corroborated Complainant’s sworn statements regarding S2’s
behavior towards her. The Agency has presented neither testimonial nor documentary evidence
which contradicts or undercuts the statements given by the CCSB, Complainant's former
subordinate, or CW1 or which causes us to question the veracity of these individuals as witnesses.

When harassment by a supervisor creates an unlawful hostile environment, the Agency can raise
an affirmative defense by showing that it exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct promptly
any harassment and that Complainant unreasonably failed to take advantage of any preventive or
corrective opportunities provided by the Agency or to avoid harm otherwise. Enforcement
Guidance of Vicarious Employer Liability for Unlawful Harassment by Supervisors, EEOC Notice
No. 915.002 § V(A) (June 18, 1999); Marielle L. v. Soc. Sec. Admin., EEOC Appeal No.
0120162299 (Mar. 29, 2018); Joel P. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120162383
(Mar. 5, 2018). Complainant reported S2’s conduct to S1 and DD, but apparently they did nothing
about it. The DD professed that there was never a basis for her to conclude that S2’s actions were
discriminatory notwithstanding that Complainant had come to her with detailed accounts of how
S2 had treated her. According to CW1, there were other warning signs as well, as evidenced by
his sworn statements that S2 took issue with “strong women,” that he observed many women
leaving the Agency under S2’s leadership, and that S2 was “only one of many EEO issues in the
HR Office.” We therefore find that the Agency failed to meet its burden of proof to show that it
had exercised reasonable care to prevent or promptly correct harassment by S2 against
Complainant and other employees.
                                                14                                     2019001523

The Agency likewise failed to show that Complainant unreasonably failed to take advantage of
available preventive or corrective opportunities. Ultimately, we find that the Agency is vicariously
liable for the sex and race-based discriminatory harassment by S2 against Complainant.

                                         CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions on appeal, we MODIFY the
Agency’s final decision. We AFFIRM the Agency’s final decision as it pertains to Complainant’s
position-reclassification claim and REVERSE the Agency’s final decision as it pertains to
Complainant's harassment claim.
