                     DECISION ON A PETITION FOR ENFORCEMENT

On June 23, 2020, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC or Commission)
docketed a petition for enforcement to examine the enforcement of an Order set forth in EEOC
Appeal No. 0120162417 (January 9, 2018). The Commission accepts this petition for
enforcement pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503.

                                        BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Petitioner worked for the Agency as a Facility
Manager at the U.S. Embassy in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan. Petitioner filed a complaint in which she
alleged that the Agency discriminated against her on the basis of national origin (Russian) in
violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e
et seq. On June 17, 2016, the Agency issued a final decision finding that Petitioner was
subjected to discrimination as alleged in one of her claims and ordered the payment of $479.40
in past pecuniary compensatory damage and referred the issue of personal travel on foreign
passports to the Agency’s Diplomatic Security administrative office to issue guidance clarifying
whether the use of such passports constitutes exercise of foreign citizenship.

                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

EEOC Regulation 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(a) provides that an aggrieved person may petition for
enforcement of an order issued by the Commission under its appellate jurisdiction. In this case,
Petitioner alleges that the Agency is not in compliance with the Commission's order in EEOC
Appeal No. 0120162417.

The January 9, 2018, Order restated the portion of the Agency’s final decision that referred the
issue of personal travel on foreign passports to the Appropriate Diplomatic Security office for the
issuance of clearer guidance. The Agency’s September 5, 2019, compliance report included the
Agency’s Security Executive Agent Directive (SEAD) 3, which was effective June 12, 2017.
SEAD 3 states that reportable activities for personnel with access to classified information or
who hold a sensitive position includes “[a]pplication for, possession, or use of a foreign passport
of identity card for travel.” While the Agency had issued the guidance in 2016, well before the
Commission issued its appellate decision on January 9, 2018, the Agency did not provide
documentation to the Commission for 604 days. Also, although we note that SEAD 3 does not
explicitly state whether use of a foreign passport constitutes exercise of foreign citizenship, we
find that the Agency has substantially complied with this provision.

Within 60 days of January 9, 2018, the Agency was ordered to expunge the undated letter
presented to Petitioner on July 2, 2015, from all official records. On September 5, 2019, the
Agency provided a March 29, 2018, email from the Adverse Actions Division Chief, Office of
Personnel Security & Suitability, which stated that the letter had been removed from Petitioner’s
security file. Assuming that the letter was expunged from Agency records on March 29, 2018,
this was 79 days after the issuance of the Order, and the Agency did not provide documentation
to the Commission for another 525 days.
                                                 5                                     2020003866

On February 20, 2020, the Compliance Officer followed up with the Agency, requesting that the
Agency provide an affidavit or signed letter from the appropriate Agency official stating that the
document was removed from all official records. See Rick G. v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec., EEOC
Petition No. 2019005318 (Oct. 26, 2020) (unable to ascertain whether Agency in compliance
with order to expunge record based on provided memorandum memorializing deletion of records
that was not signed by author of memorandum). Although the Agency did not provide further
documentation as requested, we find that the Agency has established it substantially complied
with this provision.

Within 30 days of January 9, 2018, the Agency was ordered to post a notice. The Agency’s
September 5, 2019, compliance report included a copy of the notice. Although the Agency
provided a copy of the posting notice, but it did not include documentation such as photos of the
posting in the physical location or an affidavit or letter from an appropriate official stating when
and where the posting notice was posted. On February 20, 2020, the Compliance Officer asked
the Agency to provide photos or screenshots of the posting notice as well as an affidavit from the
appropriate Agency official stating when and where the posting notice was posted. The Agency’s
October 23, 2020, compliance report included a June 13, 2018, memorandum to the record from
the U.S. Embassy Bishkek Management Officer regarding the notice, which included a photo of
the physical location of the notice. We note that the Agency did not post the notice until April
10, 2018, which was 61 days after the deadline, and that the Agency did not provide the
documentation for more than two years. Although the Agency did not provide an affidavit and
the provided memorandum was not signed, we find that the Agency has substantially complied
with this provision of the Order.

Within 90 days of January 9, 2018, the Agency was ordered to provide a minimum of eight hours
of in-person or interactive EEO training to the management officials responsible for
discriminating against Petitioner, including S2 and S6. Therefore, the Agency was required to
provide EEO training to S2 and S6 by April 9, 2018. Based on documents included in the
October 23, 2020 compliance report, S6 completed eight and a half hours of online EEO training
on January 23, 2019, October 31, 2019, and November 4, 2019, and S2 completed 15 hours of
in-person EEO training February 2-3, 2020. We note that, by the time S6 completed the
referenced training, 574 days had passed since the deadline and that, by the time S2 completed
the referenced training, 665 days had passed since the deadline. Further, the record reflects that
the training completed by S6 on January 23, 2019, was an online No FEAR Act Training that all
U.S. citizen direct-hire employees are required to complete every two years. Moreover, the
training completed by S6 on October 31, 2019, was an online EEO/Diversity Awareness for
Managers and Supervisors that the Agency requires all direct-hire employees who are managers
or supervisors to complete, and the training completed by S2 was the 15-hour classroom version
of the required EEO/Diversity Awareness for Managers and Supervisors. The Commission has
held that it is unable to ascertain agency compliance with a settlement agreement term stating
that the agency would provide EEO training to named responsible management officials where it
was unclear if the EEO training was anything other than what the Agency provided to all of its
managers for general training. Wes S. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Petition No. 2019002871 (Sept.
18, 2019).
                                                6                                     2020003866

Therefore, it is unclear whether the EEO training provided to S2 and S6 was appropriate for
responsible management officials pursuant to a finding of discrimination. We note that, if the
Agency had timely provided training to S2 and S6 and had timely provided evidence of
compliance with the Commission’s Order, the Commission would have been able to ascertain
whether S2 and S6 received appropriate training before they retired. However, considering the
retirement of S2 and S6, we find that the Agency has substantially complied with the training
order.

Within 60 days of January 9, 2018, the Agency was ordered to consider disciplining the
responsible management officials responsible for discriminating against Petitioner, including S2
and S6. Therefore, the deadline for the Agency to consider disciplinary action against S2 and S6
was March 12, 2018. 4 The Agency’s September 5, 2019, compliance report included a May 1,
2018, memorandum referring consideration of discipline for S6 to the executive office of
Diplomatic Security. May 1, 2018, was 50 days after the deadline for the Agency to consider
discipline, but the Agency did not refer consideration of discipline until this date. S6 retired on
December 31, 2019, apparently before discipline was considered. Regarding consideration of
discipline for S2, the Agency’s September 5, 2019, compliance report stated that it also referred
consideration of discipline for S2 to the executive office of Human Resources on May 1, 2018.
The Agency’s October 23, 2020, compliance report stated that S2 no longer worked in Human
Resources and that, on August 3, 2020, the Agency sent a memorandum for consideration of
discipline for S2 to the Bureau of Global Talent Management’s Conduct, Suitability and
Discipline office. In 2021, the Agency informed the Compliance Officer that, although the
Agency issued a proposal of discipline for S2 on August 18, 2021, the Agency was unable to
proceed with the disciplinary action because S2 had voluntarily retired. The Agency did not
provide the Commission with copies of the May 1, 2018, memorandum, the August 3, 2020,
memorandum, or the disciplinary proposal for S2.

We are concerned about the Agency’s significant delays in complying the provision to consider
discipline against the responsible management officials and the Agency’s failure to provide
supporting evidence in a timely manner, or, in the case of the referenced documents concerning
consideration of discipline for S2, the Agency’s failure to provide any supporting documentation.
The effective date of S6’s retirement, December 31, 2019, was 609 days after the May 1, 2018,
memorandum referred consideration of discipline for S6 and 659 days after the deadline for the
Agency’s deadline to consider discipline. More egregiously, the Agency did not refer
consideration of discipline for S2 to the proper chain of command until August 3, 2020, 875 days
after the deadline for the Agency to consider discipline. More than a year after the referral, on
August 18, 2021, the Agency proposed discipline. This was 1,255 days after the Agency’s
deadline to consider discipline. Further, because the Agency subjected Petitioner to
discrimination in 2015, we note that the Agency did not issue the proposed discipline for S2 until
2,239 days had passed since the most recent instance of discrimination.

The Agency’s extensive delays reflect serious disregard for the Commission’s authority to ensure
compliance with its orders pursuant to a finding of discrimination. Although we cannot
determine whether the Agency deliberately disregarded the Commission’s orders, the result of
the Agency’s years-long delay was that S6 was able to retire before discipline could be
considered and that S2 was able to retire before the Agency could consider the proposed
discipline. Despite these concerns, because the Agency has provided documentation establishing
that S2 and S6 have retired, we find that the Agency is now in substantial compliance with this
order.

Despite the Agency’s deficiencies in its reports and delays, at this point, we have no choice but
to find that the Agency has substantially complied with our January 9, 2018, Order. However, we
must take notice that the Agency’s compliance was not timely, and the Agency also failed to
submit its compliance report to the Commission within 30 days of the completion of all ordered
corrective action. Moreover, some of the documentation provided by the Agency to demonstrate
compliance was inadequate. 5 When an agency fails to provide the required reports and/or
evidence of its compliance, the EEOC’s Office of Federal Operations (OFO) is unable to
effectively fulfill its regulatory responsibility to monitor compliance with its orders. The
Commission may order further action if the Agency has further compliance issues. See, e.g.,
Nannette T. v. Dep’t of the Army, EEOC Petition No. 2019004335 (Jan. 31, 2020) (ordering
agency to submit written report to OFO’s Director, Federal Sector Programs based on pattern of
failing to report compliance with EEOC orders).

                                        CONCLUSION

The Commission finds that the Agency has substantially complied with the Order in Appeal No.
0120162417 and DENIES the petition for enforcement.

                            RIGHT TO REQUEST COUNSEL (Z0815)

If you want to file a civil action but cannot pay the fees, costs, or security to do so, you may
request permission from the court to proceed with the civil action without paying these fees or
costs. Similarly, if you cannot afford an attorney to represent you in the civil action, you may
request the court to appoint an attorney for you. You must submit the requests for waiver of
court costs or appointment of an attorney directly to the court, not the Commission. The
court has the sole discretion to grant or deny these types of requests. Such requests do not alter
the time limits for filing a civil action (please read the paragraph titled Petitioner’s Right to File a
Civil Action for the specific time limits).
