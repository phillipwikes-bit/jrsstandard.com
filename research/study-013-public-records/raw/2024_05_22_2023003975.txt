criteria detailed in 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c).

Following the Agency’s investigation of the claims, Complainant requested a Final Agency
Decision (FAD). On February 15, 2022, the Agency issued its FAD finding no discrimination.
With regard to Complainant’s claims alleging disparate treatment, the FAD found that the
Agency articulated legitimate nondiscriminatory reasons for its actions and that Complainant
failed to establish that such reasons were pretextual. With regard to the claims alleging
harassment, the FAD found that the actions complained of were insufficiently severe and/or
pervasive to constitute harassment.

Complainant subsequently appealed.

In EEOC Appeal No. 2022002223, we affirmed the FAD in part and vacated and remanded in
part. We initially denied Complainant’s request for a default judgement, finding that while the
Agency was late in issuing its FAD. There was no evidence of contumacious conduct or bad
faith by the Agency and that Complainant failed to show that she was prejudiced by the Agency's
delay. With regard to claims 3 through 16, 22, and 25 through 28, we found that Agency
officials articulated legitimate nondiscriminatory reasons for their actions, such as the fact that
Complainant did not have sufficient leave in her leave balance to cover her leave requests, she
was made to use FMLA leave when she was tardy, leave was deducted from her leave balance
because she had used excessive amounts of FMLA leave, her telework requests were denied
because the clinic was returning to in-person care, she was provided protective equipment, there
was no record Complainant ever actually applied for the position for which she was not selected,

and the timekeeper inadvertently entered “FMLA employee” rather than “FMLA family.” We
further found that Complainant failed to establish that such articulated reasons were pretextual.

With regard to claims alleging harassment, specifically 1, 2, 17 through 21, 23, 24, and 29, we
found that the incidents related to personality conflicts and disagreements with management

The instant request for reconsideration from Complainant followed.

                                CONTENTIONS ON REQUEST

Complainant maintains that our prior decision contained clearly erroneous issues of law and
material fact. Specifically, Complainant contends that with regard to the denial of telework, the
record shows that Supervisor 1 “advocated” for otherwise similarly situated coworkers to
telework while denying telework to Complainant, thus showing that the Agency’s articulated
reason for denying her telework, namely that the clinic was returning to in-person care, is a
pretext. Complainant next contends that we failed to consider the testimony of the union
president whose testimony supports Complainant’s allegation that Supervisor 1 subjected her to
treatment that was sufficiently severe to create a hostile work environment. Finally, Complainant
argues that the evidence shows that Supervisor 2 made comments that amount to per se reprisal
and that we erred in not making such a finding and remanding the matter for a supplemental
investigation.

                                   STANDARD OF REVIEW

EEOC Regulations provide that the Commission may, in its discretion, grant a request to
reconsider any previous Commission decision issued pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a), where
the requesting party demonstrates that: (1) the appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous
interpretation of material fact or law; or (2) the appellate decision will have a substantial impact
on the policies, practices, or operations of the agency. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c).

                                           ANALYSIS

In her Request, Complainant cites various pages in the Report of Investigation (ROI) to support
her contention that our prior decision finding that the Agency articulated a legitimate
nondiscriminatory reason for denying Complainant telework involved a clearly erroneous
interpretation of material fact or law. A review of the record and specifically the pages cited by
Complainant reveal that Complainant is simply repeating arguments made during the Agency
investigation and again on appeal, namely that in her opinion, telework was available to others
and that she was denied telework for discriminatory reasons, a contention denied by Supervisor
1.
                                                5                                    2023003975

We note that to ultimately prevail on appeal, Complainant needed to prove, by a preponderance
of the evidence, that the Agency's explanation was pretextual. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing
Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000); St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519
(1993). With the evidence from Supervisor 1 that telework was no longer available for other
employees as well, not just Complainant, the evidence below concerning this matter was in
equipoise, which was insufficient to meet Complainant’s burden of proof. Therefore,
Complainant has failed to establish that it was “clearly erroneous” for us to have found on appeal
that she failed to establish that the Agency’s articulated reason was a pretext.

With regard to harassment, Complainant cites the union president who in an unsworn statement
sent via email on May 14, 2021, stated that he was present when Supervisor 1 conducted a “fact-
finding” interview of Complainant and that Supervisor 1:

       [S]eemed to have fixated on [Complainant] and has been very aggressive towards
       [Complainant]. … [The fact-finding interview] was the most abusive, demeaning,
       and disrespectful meeting I had participated in. [Supervisor 1] was very nasty
       towards [Complainant] aggressively asking the questions. … [Supervisor 1] is the
       most unpleasant, mean-spirited individual I have come across in a very long time.

Even assuming the veracity of the union president’s statement, we note that to establish a claim
of harassment a complainant must show that: (1) she belongs to a statutorily protected class; (2)
she was subjected to unwelcome verbal or physical conduct involving the protected class; (3) the
harassment complained of was based on the statutorily protected class; (4) the harassment had
the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with her work performance and/or creating an
intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See McCleod v. Social Security Administration, EEOC Appeal No.
01963810 (August 5, 1999) (citing Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982). A
review of the union president’s unsworn statement, however, does not show that Supervisor 1’s
treatment of Complainant either involved or was based on her protected bases. We therefore
again find that Complainant has not shown that our prior decision finding no harassment was
clearly erroneous.

Finally, Complainant argues that Supervisor 2’s statements to Complainant asking her about the
status of her EEO Complaint and telling her that EEO cases can go on for years and years
constitute per se reprisal and that we erred in not making such a finding but remanding the
matter for a supplemental investigation. We note, however, that it is undisputed that Supervisor 1
was also present at the time the alleged comments were made, but that, as we found below, the
record shows she was not asked about the exchange during the investigation. As such, we find
the investigation regarding these claims to have been incomplete since Supervisor 1 could either
corroborate or deny that the comments were made and could also provide relevant context.
Given that we did not make an ultimate finding one way or the other regarding this matter we
find that our decision to remand for a supplemental investigation has not been shown to be
clearly erroneous.

After reviewing the previous decision and the entire record, the Commission finds that the
request fails to meet the criteria of 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c), and it is the decision of the
Commission to deny the request. The decision in EEOC Appeal No. 2022002223 remains the
Commission's decision. There is no further right of administrative appeal on the decision of the
Commission on this request.

                                            ORDER

Unless the Agency has already done so, within 60 days of the date this decision is issued, the
Agency shall conduct a supplemental investigation to develop an accurate factual record
regarding Complainant’s claim that the Agency engaged in per se reprisal. The Agency shall
obtain all relevant evidence to address Complainant’s claim, including, but not limited to
obtaining an affidavit from Supervisor 1, who was identified as being present at the September 4,
2020, meeting in which Supervisor 2 is alleged to have engaged in per se reprisal. Supervisor 1
should be asked about what she witnessed during the September 4, 2020, meeting. Upon
completion of the investigation, the Agency must provide the Complainant with a copy of the
supplemental record and findings. The Complainant may, within 15 days of receipt of the
supplemental record, submit a statement concerning the supplemental record. Thereafter, within
120 days of the date this decision is issued, the Agency shall issue a new final decision deciding
whether Complainant was subjected to per se reprisal.

A copy of the additional evidence obtained pursuant to this Order and a copy of the new final
decision shall be sent to the Compliance Officer as referenced herein.
