                                           DECISION

On July 3, 2018, Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
May 29, 2018, final order concerning his equal employment opportunity (EEO) complaint

Complainant stated that he had asthma and that he was allergic to iodine, dust, and other toxins.

On appeal, Complainant contends that the AJ erred in granting partial summary judgment for the

                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

On appeal, Complainant challenges the AJ’s partial grant of summary judgment in favor of the
Agency.2 In rendering this appellate decision we must scrutinize the AJ’s legal and factual
conclusions, and the Agency’s final order adopting them, de novo. See 29 C.F.R. §1614.405(a)
(stating that a “decision on an appeal from an Agency’s final action shall be based on a de novo
review . . .”); see also Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part
1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9, § VI.B. (Aug. 5, 2015) (providing that an administrative
judge’s determination to issue a decision without a hearing, and the decision itself, will both be
reviewed de novo). This essentially means that we should look at this case with fresh eyes. In
other words, we are free to accept (if accurate) or reject (if erroneous) the AJ’s, and Agency’s,
factual conclusions and legal analysis – including on the ultimate fact of whether intentional
discrimination occurred, and on the legal issue of whether any federal employment
discrimination statute was violated. See id. at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (explaining that the de novo
standard of review “requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the
factual and legal determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the
documents, statements, and testimony of record, including any timely and relevant submissions
of the parties, and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record
and its interpretation of the law”).

Summary Judgment

We determine whether the AJ appropriately issued a partial decision without a hearing. The
Commission’s regulations allow an AJ to issue a decision without a hearing upon finding that
there is no genuine issue of material fact. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.109(g). EEOC’s decision without a
hearing regulation follows the summary judgment procedure from federal court. Fed. R. Civ. P.
56. The U.S. Supreme Court held summary judgment is appropriate where a judge determines no
genuine issue of material fact exists under the legal and evidentiary standards. Anderson v.
Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). In ruling on a summary judgment motion, the
judge is to determine whether there are genuine issues for trial, as opposed to weighing the
evidence. Id. at 249. At the summary judgment stage, the judge must believe the non-moving
party’s evidence and must draw justifiable inferences in the non-moving party’s favor. Id. at 255.
A “genuine issue of fact” is one that a reasonable judge could find in favor for the non-moving
party. Celotex v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986); Oliver v. Digital Equip. Corp., 846 F.2d
103, 105 (1st Cir. 1988). A “material” fact has the potential to affect the outcome of a case.

An AJ may issue a decision without a hearing only after determining that the record has been
adequately developed. See Petty v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 01A24206 (July 11, 2003).
We carefully reviewed the record and find that it is adequately developed. To successfully
oppose a decision without a hearing, Complainant must identify material facts of record that are
in dispute or present further material evidence establishing facts in dispute. Here, Complainant
generally asserted that the AJ should have made credibility determinations and has identified
some disputed facts, but we find that these are not material. For example, Complainant argues
that management knew or should have known that reassigning him to the annex building was an
ineffective accommodation because it was dirty. However, Complainant identifies no evidence
that could establish that the Agency was aware of the conditions in the annex before it received
the FOH report, and he notes in his brief that he did not vocalize his concerns to management.
Although Complainant states that he did not report his concerns about the annex to management
because he feared reprisal, we find that no reasonable factfinder could find that management was
aware of the conditions in the annex while Complainant was working there. We find that the AJ
properly issued a decision without a hearing for the claims at issue in this appeal.

Disparate Treatment

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim, Complainant must satisfy the three-part evidentiary
scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792
(1973). He must generally establish a prima facie case by demonstrating that he was subjected to
an adverse employment action under circumstances that would support an inference of
discrimination. Furnco Constr. Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576 (1978). The prima facie
inquiry may be dispensed with in this case, however, since the Agency has articulated legitimate
and nondiscriminatory reasons for its conduct. See U.S. Postal Serv. Bd. of Governors v.
Aikens, 460 U.S. 711, 713-17 (1983); Holley v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Request No.
05950842 (Nov. 13, 1997).
                                               10                                   0120182433

To ultimately prevail, Complainant must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the
Agency’s explanation is a pretext for discrimination. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products,
Inc., 530 U.S. 133 (2000); St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993); Tex. Dep’t
of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981); Holley, supra; Pavelka v. Dep’t of the
Navy, EEOC Request No. 05950351 (Dec. 14, 1995).

Complainant alleged that he was subjected to discrimination when S3 denied his request to
telework on August 3, 2012. The Agency’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory explanation for not
allowing Complainant to telework or work remotely from an alternative site such as the airport
was that Complainant needed to access sensitive, confidential, and classified information and
that the HSI IG team needed to be located near HSI management and Criminal Investigators to
facilitate the effectiveness of analysis. Although Complainant asserts that he could have worked
effectively from home or from the airport, his assertion does not establish that the Agency’s
legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason was a pretext for discrimination. We will analyze this claim
as a denial of a reasonable accommodation herein.

Complainant also alleged that he was discriminated against when his application for the VLTP
program was not approved. The Agency’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for not
approving Complainant’s application were that it was not clear he established a qualifying health
emergency because his application stated that he could work and that he had not exhausted his
accrued paid leave. As evidence of pretext, Complainant states that he was initially told that
there was no application form for the VLTP and that S1 did not collect his certified mailing that
contained a copy of his application. However, a mistake made by an agency is not evidence of
pretext unless there is evidence that the mistake was based on a complainant's protected classes.
See Vickey S. v. Dep't of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 0120112893 (Nov. 17, 2015); Hsieh v.
Dep't of Veterans Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120120980 (June 4, 2012); Carroll v. Dep't of
Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 01A20985 (Jan. 21, 2003). We find that Complainant has not
established that he was told that there was no VLTP application form or that S1 did not collect
the certified mailing because of Complainant’s membership in any protected class, and he has
not otherwise established pretext for discrimination by a preponderance of the evidence in the
record.

Complainant alleged discrimination with respect to being moved to the annex building. The
Agency’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory explanation for its actions was that the annex was an
alternative on site space where Complainant could work while the main building was cleaned
because his request to telework or move remotely could not be granted. As evidence of pretext,
Complainant states that his workspace in the annex lacked privacy and amenities such as a
landline telephone and argues that the FOH investigations revealed that the annex was actually
dirtier and more dangerous than the main building. There is no evidence in the record that
management was aware of the environmental problems with the annex before Complainant
moved there in August 2012. Moreover, we find that Complainant has not established by a
preponderance of the evidence in the record that the Agency’s proffered reason was a pretext
designed to mask discrimination based on disability and/or reprisal. We will separately analyze
this in the context of a reasonable accommodation.
                                                 11                                     0120182433

Complainant alleged that he was subjected to discrimination when he was moved back into the
main building in March 2013. The Agency’s legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for moving
Complainant back into the main building were that IH1 found management had complied with
her recommendations, that DR2 told Complainant that it was reasonable to move back to the

Reasonable Accommodation

Complainant alleged that the Agency denied him a reasonable accommodation for his disability
when he was not permitted to telework or wok remotely. In order to establish that Complainant
was denied a reasonable accommodation, Complainant must show that: (1) he is an individual
with a disability; (2) he is a qualified individual with a disability; and (3) the Agency failed to
provide a reasonable accommodation. See EEOC Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable
Accommodation and Undue Hardship Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, No. 915.002
(Oct. 17, 2002).

An agency is required to make reasonable accommodation to the known physical and mental
limitations of a qualified individual with a disability unless the agency can show that
accommodation would cause an undue hardship. 29 C.F.R. §§ 1630.2(o) and (p). “The term
“qualified,” with respect to an individual with a disability, means that the individual satisfies the
requisite skill, experience, education and other job-related requirements of the employment
position such individual holds or desires and, with or without reasonable accommodation, can
perform the essential functions of such position.” 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(m).

Complainant initially requested to telework or to work remotely. However, we find that this
would not constitute a reasonable accommodation, because Complainant’s job duties required a
secure environment to access sensitive information and because the HSI IG needed to work in
close proximity to each other, HSI management, and HSI Criminal Investigators. The Agency
moved Complainant to the annex from the main building as an alternate reasonable
accommodation.

Complainant also alleged that reassigning him to the annex building was not an effective
reasonable accommodation. While Complainant is entitled to an effective reasonable
accommodation, he is not entitled to the accommodation of his choice. Lynette B. v. Dep’t. of
Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 0720140010 (Dec. 3, 2015). The Agency moved Complainant to the
annex, and he did not complain about the conditions of his workspace to management while he
was working there. Moreover, the record reflects that Complainant used minimal sick leave
while working in the annex from August 20, 2012, through March 1, 2013. We therefore agree
that the Agency had fulfilled its obligation under the Rehabilitation Act.

Hostile Work Environment

To establish a claim of harassment a complainant must show that: (1) he belongs to a statutorily
protected class; (2) he was subjected to harassment in the form of unwelcome verbal or physical
conduct involving the protected class; (3) the harassment complained of was based on his
statutorily protected class; (4) the harassment affected a term or condition of employment and/or
had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with the work environment and/or creating
an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982).
Further, the incidents must have been "sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of
[complainant's] employment and create an abusive working environment." Harris v. Forklift
Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The harasser's conduct should be evaluated from the
objective viewpoint of a reasonable person in the victim's circumstances. Enforcement Guidance
on Harris v. Forklift Systems Inc., EEOC Notice No. 915.002 at 6 (Mar. 8, 1994).

Upon review, we find that Complainant has not established that he was subjected to harassment
based on his disability and/or participation in protected EEO activity that was sufficiently severe
or pervasive to constitute a hostile work environment. For example, there is no evident
connection between management’s alleged delay in providing copies of FOH reports and
Complainant’s membership in any protected class. Complainant alleged that management made
remarks that he could consider applying to jobs elsewhere in the Agency or about the amount of
sick leave that he was taking. We find that these comments do not rise to the level of a hostile
work environment. Accordingly, Complainant has not established that he was subjected to a
hostile work environment.

                                         CONCLUSION

We AFFIRM the Agency’s final order fully implementing the AJ’s decision.
