                               BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a
Carrier Technician at the Agency’s Garland-South Station in Garland, Texas.

On April 17, 2021, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging that the Agency
subjected her to discriminatory harassment based on disability (physical) when:

      1.    since on or about November 7, 2020, Complainant was continually
            harassed/questioned about her work restrictions and not provided
            assistance;
      2.    since on or about November 9, 2020, Complainant was required to do
            the walking portion of her route;
      3.    on December 23, 2020, Complainant was yelled at and embarrassed in
            front of her coworkers;
      4.    on December 23, 2020, Complainant was required to submit a new
            CA-17, and not paid for the time she missed from work to get the CA-
            17 completed;
      5.    on December 23, 2020, Complainant was moved off Route 74 and
            requested to case and carry Route 23;
      6.    on February 1, 2021, Complainant was called to the Supervisor’s desk
            to discuss her medical documents;
      7.    on March 23, 2021, Complainant was harassed and referred to the
            District Reasonable Accommodation Committee (DRAC);
      8.    on April 2, 2021, Complainant’s request for leave was not granted and
            she was charged Absent Without Official Leave (AWOL); and
      9.    on or about April 15, 2021, management gave Complainant a
            modified assignment which changed her start time to 10:00 a.m., and
            they did not provide her with a mail cart.

At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided Complainant with a
copy of the report of investigation (ROI) and notice of her right to request a
hearing before an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). Complainant requested a
hearing, but the AJ dismissed the hearing request based on her failure to comply
with the Commission’s orders. The AJ remanded the complaint for the Agency to
issue a final decision pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b). The Agency’s final
decision found no discrimination as alleged. Complainant appealed.
                                        3                             2025002816

On September 23, 2024, the Commission vacated the Agency’s final decision and
remanded the complaint for a supplemental investigation. As an initial matter, the
appellate decision found that the AJ’s dismissal of Complainant’s hearing request
for her repeated failures to respond to the AJ’s orders without cause was not an
abuse of the AJ’s discretion. The appellate decision then found that the prior
investigation was inadequate. For example, the record did not contain medical
documentation regarding Complainant’s physical restrictions or supporting
document for the grievance decisions. Micheline L. v. United States Postal Service,
EEOC Appeal Number 2022003909 (Sept. 23, 2024).

Upon completion of the supplemental investigation, the Agency issued another
final decision. The Agency assumed that Complainant was an individual with a
disability and qualified for her position, and it found that she was accommodated
with an additional mail bag and a cart after she provided medical documentation.
The Agency then determined that Complainant had not established a prima facie
case for claims 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, or 9, 2 but that management officials provided
legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for the actions. For example, Complainant
was charged AWOL because she only requested eight (8) hours of leave for two
(2) days that covered sixteen (16) hours. The Agency found that Complainant did
not show that the reasons were pretextual. The Agency also determined that
Complainant had not established harassment because the complained of events
occupied the normal scope and course of industrial relations. The Agency
concluded that Complainant failed to prove discrimination as alleged.

The instant appeal followed. Complainant asserts that the Agency failed to
consider critical evidence, such as her union grievance rulings that she was
subjected to harassment and denied an accommodation of a mail cart. She also
argues that the Agency falsely upheld the AWOL charge despite clear evidence
that her leave was approved. Complainant also challenges the dismissal of her
hearing request as a violation of due process. 3
The Agency opposes Complainant’s appeal.

                            STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency’s decision is subject to de novo review by the
Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5,
2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal
determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the
documents, statements, and testimony of record, including any timely and relevant
submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s
own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).

                                   ANALYSIS

Reasonable Accommodation (Claims 1 and 9)

An agency is required to make reasonable accommodation to the known physical
and mental limitations of an individual with a disability unless the agency can
show that accommodation would cause an undue hardship. 29 C.F.R. §§ 1630.2(o),
(p). In order to establish that she was denied a reasonable accommodation,
Complainant must show that: (1) she is an individual with a disability as defined
by 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(g); (2) she is a “qualified” as defined by 29 C.F.R. §
1630.2(m); and (3) the Agency failed to provide a reasonable accommodation. See
EEOC Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable Accommodation and Undue
Hardship Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, EEOC Notice No. 915.002
(Oct. 17, 2002) (Enforcement Guidance).

Assuming, for the purpose of analysis, and without so finding, that Complainant is
an individual with a disability and is qualified for her position, we find that the
Agency did not fail to accommodate her.

For claims 1 and 9, Complainant requested a mail cart as an accommodation on or
about March 23, 2021, and she submitted a supporting medical document, dated
March 16, 2021. ROI at 109, 187. The Station Manager attested that the cart was
ordered after they received medical documentation. ROI at 151.
                                         5                              2025002816

In the Step B grievance decision issued on June 10, 2021, the Dispute Resolution
Team (DRT) noted that Complainant was informed during the last week of March
2021 that a cart would be provided to her, but none was supplied. The Union
highlighted that there were two (2) mail carts at another Garland station (Main Post
Office) that were not being used, and the DRT ordered that one (1) of these carts
be provided to Complainant within seven (7) calendar days. Supplemental ROI at
87-8. It is reasonable to conclude that Complainant did not receive a cart until June
17, 2021, and there was a delay in providing a cart for over two (2) months since
late March 2021.

The Commission has held that failure to respond to a request for accommodation in
a timely manner may result in a finding of discrimination. See Denese G. v. Dep’t
of the Treasury, EEOC Appeal No. 0120141118 (Dec. 29, 2016); Shealey v. Equal
Employment Opport. Comm., EEOC Appeal No. 0120070356 (April 18, 2011);
Villanueva v. Dep’t of Homeland Security, EEOC Appeal No. 01A34968 (Aug.
10, 2006). In determining whether there was an unnecessary delay, we are to
consider (1) the reasons for the delay; (2) the length of the delay; (3) how much the
individual with a disability and the employer each contributed to the delay; (4)
what the employer was doing during the delay; and (5) whether the required
accommodation was simple or complex to provide. Enforcement Guidance at
Question 10, n.38.

The Commission has previously found an unreasonable delay for two (2) or three
(3) months when there is no reasonable explanation in the record to justify the
delay. See Barrett V. v. Dep’t of Defense, EEOC Appeal NO. 2021001566 (Jun. 3,
2021) (a management official’s response that it was difficult to ascertain the start
date of the reasonable accommodation was not substantiated by the record, and the
agency’s Disability Program Manager could not explain the delay after informing
the management officials of their need to respond); req. for recons. den., EEOC
Request No. 2021004104 (Apr. 25, 2022); Ria T. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC
Appeal No. 0120182257 (Jan. 14, 2020) (management officials had no recollection
and did not know why there was a two-month delay in responding to the
complainant’s reasonable accommodation request).

In this case, the record contains the Station Manager’s explanation in response to
Complainant’s grievance that they needed to order a cart and obtain approval by
Safety to make sure it was not a hazard, and that no cart was available at Garland-
South. Supplemental ROI at 98.
                                         6                             2025002816

While the accommodation would have been simple to provide if the Garland-South
location obtained an available cart from the Main Post Office, there is no
indication that they shared resources or that the Station Manager had the authority
to take it prior to the DRT’s decision. We find that there is an explanation for the
over 2-month delay, and that Complainant did not show an unreasonable delay for
claim 1.

Complainant also complained that her modified job assignment changed her start
time to 10:00 a.m. in claim 9. The Station Manager responded that it was standard
operating procedure to change the start time when an employee has an on-the-job
injury with restrictions. ROI at 151. While the Rehabilitation Act provides that
qualified individuals with a disability be granted an effective reasonable
accommodation, it does not entitle them to the accommodation of their choice. See
Castaneda v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01931005 (Feb. 17, 1994); see
also Enforcement Guidance at Question 9. Complainant preferred to work a
schedule with an earlier start time, but she did not challenge the 10:00 a.m. start
time as ineffective. As such, we find that Complainant did not show a failure to
accommodate by the Agency.

Disparate Treatment (Claims 2, 4, 5, and 8)

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim, a complainant must satisfy the three-part
evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp.
v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). A complainant must initially establish a prima facie
case by demonstrating that he/she was subjected to an adverse employment action
under circumstances that would support an inference of discrimination. Furnco
Constr. Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576 (1978). Proof of a prima facie case will
vary depending on the facts of the particular case. McDonnell Douglas, 441 U.S. at
802 n.13. The burden then shifts to the agency to articulate a legitimate,
nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine,
450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). To ultimately prevail, a complainant must prove, by a
preponderance of the evidence, that the agency’s explanation is a pretext for
discrimination. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 143
(2000); St. Mary’s Honor Ctr v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993).
                                        7                             2025002816

Assuming, arguendo, a prima facie case of discrimination, the Agency articulated
legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for claims 2, 4, 5, and 8. For claim 2,
Complainant alleged that the Station Manager took away the driving and apartment
components of her route and assigned her only the walking portion. The Station
Manager denied that Complainant was assigned only walking and noted that four
(4) of the five (5) routes on Complainant’s bid assignment were all walking. In
addition, Complainant’s medical restrictions allowed walking. ROI at 140. A
Supervisor added that no one was available to carry the walking portion of
Complainant’s route, and that they adhered to her medical restrictions. ROI at 165.

Complainant claimed that the Station Manager required her to leave to obtain
another CA-17 form, and she was not paid for the time when she missed work
(claim 4). A review of the medical document dated December 16, 2020, shows that
it was only partially filled out, and Complainant submitted a completed one dated
December 23, 2020. ROI at 178-9. The Station Manager responded that
Complainant was paid by the Office of Workers’ Compensation Program (OWCP)
when she goes to appointments. The Station Manager provided Complainant’s
Everything Report and highlighted the 1.33 hours of OWCP time for December 23,
2020. ROI at 143, 154.

In claim 5, Complainant alleged that the Station Manager had Complainant switch
routes to carry Route 23. The Station Manager attested that Route 23 was within
Complainant’s bid assignment. ROI at 144.

For claim 8, the Station Manager explained that Complainant submitted a leave
request for eight (8) hours for April 1-2, 2021, but this only covered one (1) day,
and Complainant should have requested sixteen (16) hours. Supplemental ROI at
46. Complainant’s leave request form confirms that she only requested eight (8)
hours of leave for April 1st and 2nd, which was approved. ROI at 188. On appeal,
Complainant contends that the Agency falsely upheld the AWOL charge despite
evidence that her leave was approved. However, Complainant offers no dispute
that her approved leave request was for only eight (8) hours or evidence that she
submitted another leave request to cover the remaining eight (8) hours.
Complainant did not show that the proffered reasons are not worthy of belief, and
her bare assertions that management officials discriminated against her are
insufficient to prove pretext or that the actions were discriminatory.
                                          8                              2025002816

Further, the Commission has long held that an Agency has broad discretion to set
policies and carry out personnel decisions, and it should not be second-guessed by
the reviewing authority, absent evidence of unlawful motivation. See Texas Dep’t
of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 259 (1981); Vanek v. Dep’t of the
Treasury, EEOC Request No. 05940906 (Jan. 16, 1997). In this case, there is no
evidence of unlawful motivation for the Agency’s actions. Accordingly, we find
that Complainant did not establish disability discrimination for claims 2, 4, 5, or 8.

Harassment (Claims 1, 3, 6, and 7)

In order to establish a prima facie case of harassment, Complainant must prove, by
a preponderance of the evidence, the existence of five elements: (1) that she is a
member of a statutorily protected class; (2) that she was subjected to unwelcome
conduct related to her protected class; (3) that the harassment complained of was
based on her protected class; (4) that the harassment had the purpose or effect of
unreasonably interfering with her work performance and/or creating an
intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) that there is a basis
for imputing liability to the employer. See Celine B. v. Dep’t of Navy, EEOC
Appeal No. 2019001961 (Sept. 21, 2020); Humphrey v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC
Appeal No. 01965238 (Oct. 16, 1998). See also Henson v. City of Dundee, 682
F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982), approved in Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson, 477 U.S.
57, 66-67 (1986); Flowers v. Southern Reg’l Physician Serv. Inc., 247 F.3d 229
(5th Cir. 2001). The harasser’s conduct should be evaluated from the objective
viewpoint of a reasonable person in the victim’s circumstances. Oncale v.
Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 81 (1998).

In other words, to prove her hostile work environment claim, Complainant must
establish that she was subjected to conduct that was either so severe or so
pervasive that a “reasonable person” in Complainant’s position would have found
the conduct to be hostile or abusive. Complainant must also prove that the conduct
was taken because of a protected basis; in this case, her disability. Only if
Complainant establishes both of those elements – hostility and motive – will the
question of Agency liability present itself.

Complainant alleged harassment when the Station Manager yelled at and
embarrassed Complainant in front of her coworkers on December 3, 2020 (claim
3), when she stating that Complainant needed to move from route 74 to 23 because
she was on limited duty.
                                         9                             2025002816

The Station Manager also purportedly said that she would not accept
Complainant’s CA-17. Supplemental ROI at 31. Crediting that this was based on
Complainant’s disability, we find that this isolated event was insufficiently severe
to rise to the level of unlawful harassment.

Complainant also complained that she was harassed when she was asked about her
medical restrictions and documents (claims 1 and 6), and when she was referred to
the DRAC (claim 7). However, the Commission has held that events surrounding
an interactive process for reasonable accommodation do not constitute harassment.
See Wilson v. Environmental Protection Agency, EEOC Appeal No. 0120082755
(Aug. 8, 2011); Dahlman v. Consumer Product Safety Commission, EEOC Appeal
No. 0120073190 (Apr. 15, 2010). In addition, Complainant described for claim 6
that the Station Manager called an OWCP representative, and they discussed that
Complainant would need to submit a doctor’s note to get paid. ROI at 131.
Complainant’s version of events does not show that they had any discussion about
her medical restrictions or condition.

On appeal, Complainant asserts that a union grievance ruling found that she was
subjected to harassment. However, the finding that management officials violated
articles of the collective bargaining agreement does not prove that Complainant
was subjected to harassment under EEO laws. While the DRT found merit in the
allegations that the Station Manager yelled at or made derogatory remarks to
carriers about their performance and/or medical restrictions while on the workroom
floor,4 Complainant’s witness did not substantiate that the Station Manager’s
remarks were due to Complainant’s protected class. Specifically, he stated that the
Station Manager was “real smart and hostile” towards Complainant “for no
apparent reason,” and that the Station Manager talks down to a lot of employees
that she does not like. Supplemental ROI at 63, 74. Accordingly, we find that
Complainant did not establish harassment based on her disability.

                                 CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions on appeal, including
those not specifically addressed herein, we AFFIRM the Agency’s final decision.

                       RECONSIDERATION (M1125)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if
Complainant or the Agency submits a written request that contains arguments or
evidence that tend to establish that:
      1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of
         material fact or law; or
      2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies,
         practices, or operations of the agency.

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Sector
(OFS) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party
requesting reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the
request, that statement or brief must be filed together with the request for
reconsideration. A party shall have twenty (20) calendar days from receipt of
another party’s request for reconsideration within which to submit a brief or
statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at
Chap. 9 § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit their request for reconsideration, and any statement or
brief in support of their request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx. Alternatively, Complainant can
submit their request and arguments to the Director, Office of Federal Sector, Equal
Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed to P.O. Box
77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street,
NE, Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s
request to reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFS receives it by mail within
five days of the expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. §
1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the
EEOC’s Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g).
Either party’s request and/or statement or brief in opposition must also include
proof of service on the other party, unless Complainant files their request via the
EEOC Public Portal, in which case no proof of service is required.

Failure to file within the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s
request for reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances
prevented the timely filing of the request. Any supporting documentation must be
submitted together with the request for reconsideration. The Commission will
consider requests for reconsideration filed after the deadline only in very limited
circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(f).
