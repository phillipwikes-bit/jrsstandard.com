                                    DECISION

Complainant filed an appeal to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission
(EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
June 2, 2025, final decision concerning his equal employment opportunity (EEO)
complaint alleging employment discrimination in violation of Section 501 of the
Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 791 et seq.
For the following reasons, the Commission AFFIRMS the Agency’s final decision.

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant was employed by
the Agency as a Social Worker at the Agency’s Carl R. Darnall Army Medical
Center in Fort Cavazos, Texas.

On June 22, 2021, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging discrimination by
the Agency on the basis of disability (PTSD) when:

   1. He failed the Defense Health Agency Behavioral Health Consultant training
      conducted April 12-16, 2021;
   2. Sometime between April 12-16, 2021, he was made to break his reasonable
      accommodation by having to evaluate a person with Post-Traumatic Stress
      Disorder (PTSD) during the Behavioral Health Consultant training; and
   3. He was not allowed to practice as a Behavioral Health Consultant at Monroe
      Clinic.

Complainant was diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder in December 2020
following the mass shooting that occurred at Fort Hood. See Report of Investigation
(ROI) at 157. Because of his diagnosis, his mental health provider recommended
that Complainant should not work directly with people who have PTSD. See ROI
at 104. Complainant requested a reasonable accommodation to be transferred to the
Child and Family Behavioral Health System but the Agency offered him an
alternative accommodation of working as a Social Worker at one of the Soldier
Centered Medical Homes as an Internal Behavioral Health Consultant (BHC),
because this was “the least invasive environment” with regard to Complainant’s
condition because it would not involve long-term therapeutic care and the only role
played in any possible PTSD case would be transferring the case to the Department
of Behavioral Health. See ROI at 118-20. Complainant accepted the alternative
accommodation.

The Behavioral Health Program Manager (Program Manager) stated that all
Behavioral Health Consultants are required to demonstrate a set of core
competencies which requires going through the BHC training. See ROI at 236-37.
He asserted that this is important because the PCBH model of care is different from
typical specialty behavioral health work. See ROI at 237. Complainant stated that
he believed he should have passed the BHC training because he followed the training
format explicitly but no matter what he said or did, he was told it was wrong. See
ROI at 162. He insisted that there were many inconsistencies in the training and no
discussion about things he found problematic. See ROI at 162.
                                          3                              2025003358

He asserted that during the training, he was required to assess and treat a virtual
PTSD patient, which he failed, even though treating patients with PTSD was a
violation of his reasonable accommodation. See ROI at 167. He stated that he did
not know ahead of time what the test case would be as it was only assigned minutes
before the assignment. See ROI at 167. He asserted that because he did not pass the
training, he was not allowed to practice from February 2021 to May 2021 even
though he is licensed as a clinical social worker by the state of Texas and had passed
the written portion of the training. See ROI at 168-69.

The Program Manager stated that the Trainer told him that Complainant had a lack
of knowledge about how to assess/treat pain, was not able to perform a thorough
assessment, was not able to present an adequate biopsychosocial formulation, and
did not demonstrate sufficient knowledge about evidence-based interactions. See
ROI at 238. He further explained that Complainant did not fail the training due to
the single role play involving PTSD but rather that the Trainer and two other
program managers observed Complainant’s performance across six separate role
plays and across all those role plays, Complainant did not demonstrate the required
competencies. See ROI at 240-41. He further noted that he was not aware of
Complainant’s condition or his reasonable accommodation prior to the training. See
ROI at 242.

The Chief of the Department of Behavioral Health (Chief) stated that he supported
reassigning Complainant to the position of Behavioral Health Consultant within the
department of Soldier Medicine because it was the least invasive environment with
regard to the limitations and recommendations made by Complainant’s
psychologist. See ROI at 331. He asserted that he had no organizational relationship
with Complainant once Complainant moved to the new position but that he was
aware that all BHCs are required to complete the training in order to be qualified to
see patients in a primary care setting. See ROI at 339. He denied ever making a
comment that anything associated with Complainant’s condition was
“controversial,” or ever disclosing Complainant’s medical condition to the Program
Manager. See ROI at 343.

At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided Complainant with a
copy of the report of investigation and notice of his right to request a hearing before
an Equal Employment Opportunity Commission Administrative Judge, or a final
decision from the agency. When Complainant requested a final Agency decision,
the Agency issued a final decision pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b).
                                          4                              2025003358

The decision concluded that Complainant did not prove that the Agency subjected
him to discrimination as alleged. Complainant then filed the instant appeal.

On appeal, Complainant, among other things, argues that the Agency should be
sanctioned for failing to issue the final decision until more than three years after he
requested a decision. On the merits, Complainant contends that the Agency’s final
decision erred because it did not properly analyze Complainant’s claim as a denial
of reasonable accommodation claim. Complainant further contends that the Agency
should not have required Complainant to take the training as a form of reasonable
accommodation.

The Agency, in pertinent part, argued that sanctions are not warranted and also that
its final decision should be affirmed.

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency’s decision is subject to de novo review by the
Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at 9-16 (Aug. 5,
2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the Commission
examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and
testimony of record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties,
and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record
and its interpretation of the law”).

We will first briefly address Complainant’s request for sanctions against the Agency
for its failure to issue a final decision in a timely manner. While we recognize that
the Agency’s issuance of its final decision was very untimely, in the circumstances
of this case, we do not find that sanctions are warranted. Complainant has not
alleged, nor has the record shown, that Complainant was prejudiced by the Agency’s
delay in issuing its decision. We emphasize that the record indicates that even during
the time before Complainant was permitted to practice as a BHC due to his failure
to pass the training, the evidence indicates that Complainant was paid his full salary
and did not suffer any adverse action for his failure to pass the BHC training. See
e.g., Josefina L. v. Soc. Sec. Admin., EEOC Appeal No. 0120142023 (July 19,
2016), req. for recon. den’d, EEOC Request No. 0520170108 (Feb. 9, 2017) (finding
that the Agency’s 571-day delay in issuing a final decision did not warrant sanctions,
as complainant did not show she was prejudiced by the delay); Jocelyn R. v. Dep’t
                                          5                              2025003358

of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 0120152852 (Mar. 11, 2016) (citing Vunder v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01A55147 (May 12, 2006) (declining to sanction an
agency that issued a final decision after approximately 371 days)). We therefore
decline to issue a sanction against the Agency. We do, however, remind the Agency
of its obligation to adhere to the Commission’s regulatory timeframes and issue final
decisions in accordance with 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b).

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim, a complainant must satisfy the three-part
evidentiary scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp.
v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). A complainant must initially establish a prima facie
case by demonstrating that he was subjected to an adverse employment action under
circumstances that would support an inference of discrimination. Furnco Constr.
Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576 (1978). Proof of a prima facie case will vary
depending on the facts of the particular case. McDonnell Douglas, 441 U.S. at 802
n.13.    The burden then shifts to the agency to articulate a legitimate,
nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine,
450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). To ultimately prevail, a complainant must prove, by a
preponderance of the evidence, that the agency’s explanation is a pretext for
discrimination. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 143
(2000); St. Mary’s Honor Ctr v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993).

We find that the record does not support Complainant’s assertion that he was
subjected to disparate treatment. He did not identify any similarly-situated
individuals who were treated more favorably nor did he offer anything beyond his
unsupported assertions to indicate that either the Program Manager or the Chief were
motivated by any discriminatory animus. In addition, there is nothing in the record
beyond Complainant’s unsupported assertions to indicate that the training was unfair
or that Complainant’s performance during the training should have resulted in
Complainant’s passing the training. See Trent M. v. Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC
Appeal No. 2022000776 (Nov 2, 2022) (stating that a complainant’s subjective
assessment of his own performance or repeated assertions of his belief that his
supervisor’s evaluation of his performance was unfair is not sufficient without more
to establish pretext). While it is clear that Complainant feels that he was not treated
fairly, there is no evidence aside from his assertions to indicate that the Agency was
motivated by discriminatory animus. The Commission has repeatedly stated that
mere assertions or conjecture that an agency’s explanation is a pretext for intentional
discrimination is insufficient because subjective belief, however genuine, does not
constitute evidence of pretext.
                                          6                              2025003358

See Jed T. v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec’y, EEOC Appeal No. 2022000805 (May 18,
2023); Juliet B. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120182519 (Oct. 8, 2019);
Richardson v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Petition No. 03A40016 (Dec. 11, 2003).

We further find that Complainant did not establish that the Agency denied him a
reasonable accommodation as alleged.3 While Complainant insists that the Agency
violated the Rehabilitation Act by denying him his requested accommodation of
being assigned to the Child and Family Behavioral Health System, it is well settled
that an employee does not have to be given the accommodation of his choice as long
as the accommodation offered is effective. See McCall v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.,
EEOC Appeal No. 0120114251 (Feb. 21, 2013).

To the extent that Complainant appears to argue that he should have been given an
accommodation to assist him with the BHC training, the record does not show that
Complainant ever indicated to the Agency that he might need a reasonable
accommodation to assist him with the training. While we acknowledge that the fact
that the single role play during the training that involved PTSD was in violation of
Complainant’s limitations, the Agency cannot be held responsible for it when there
is no evidence that the Program Manager or the trainer knew about Complainant’s
restrictions at the time. See Franklin P. v. Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC Appeal No.
2024000622 (May 1, 2025) (“Because the obligation to provide a reasonable
accommodation is always prospective, agencies are not required to excuse past
performance deficiencies even if they were the result of an individual’s disability.”).
The Program Manager stated that Complainant never mentioned his condition until
after he had already failed the training and Complainant did not dispute this,
asserting only his unsupported belief that the Chief had told the Program Manager
about his condition. See Michal G. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No.
2022004272 (Sept. 26, 2022) (stating that the interactive process required by the
Rehabilitation Act is not a one-way street and Complainant cannot simply place the
entire burden on the Agency to take action). Moreover, the record does not indicate
that Complainant failed the training due to the single role play involving PTSD. The
Program Manager stated that Complainant failed the training because he failed to
demonstrate the core competencies over all of the role plays and Complainant

himself acknowledged that he was told that he was doing everything wrong,

obligated to excuse the violation of job performance standards of a position through
the reasonable accommodation process. See Leticia W. v. Dep’t of War, EEOC
Appeal No. 2025000799 (July 6, 2026).

Therefore, based on our review of the record and the contentions on appeal,
including those not specifically addressed herein, we AFFIRM the Agency’s final
decision finding no discrimination.


                     STATEMENT OF RIGHTS - ON APPEAL
                         RECONSIDERATION (M1125)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if
Complainant or the Agency submits a written request that contains arguments or
evidence that tend to establish that:
      1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of
         material fact or law; or
      2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies,
         practices, or operations of the agency.
Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Sector
(OFS) within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party
requesting reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request,
that statement or brief must be filed together with the request for
reconsideration.
                                           8                              2025003358

A party shall have twenty (20) calendar days from receipt of another party’s request
for reconsideration within which to submit a brief or statement in opposition. See
29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for
29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).
Complainant should submit their request for reconsideration, and any statement or
brief in support of their request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx. Alternatively, Complainant can
submit their request and arguments to the Director, Office of Federal Sector, Equal
Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed to P.O. Box
77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street, NE,
Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s
request to reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFS receives it by mail within
five days of the expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604.

An agency’s request for reconsideration must be submitted in digital format via the
EEOC’s Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP). See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). Either
party’s request and/or statement or brief in opposition must also include proof of
service on the other party, unless Complainant files their request via the EEOC
Public Portal, in which case no proof of service is required.
Failure to file within the 30-day time period will result in dismissal of the party’s
request for reconsideration as untimely, unless extenuating circumstances prevented
the timely filing of the request. Any supporting documentation must be
submitted together with the request for reconsideration. The Commission will
consider requests for reconsideration filed after the deadline only in very limited
circumstances. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604(f).

                     RIGHT TO REQUEST COUNSEL (Z0815)

If you want to file a civil action but cannot pay the fees, costs, or security to do so,
you may request permission from the court to proceed with the civil action without
paying these fees or costs. Similarly, if you cannot afford an attorney to represent
you in the civil action, you may request the court to appoint an attorney for you. You
must submit the requests for waiver of court costs or appointment of an
attorney directly to the court, not the Commission. The court has the sole
discretion to grant or deny these types of requests. Such requests do not alter the
time limits for filing a civil action (please read the paragraph titled Complainant’s
Right to File a Civil Action for the specific time limits).
