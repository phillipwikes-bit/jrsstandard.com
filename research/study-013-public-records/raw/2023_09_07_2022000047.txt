Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s

Article 35 of the National Collective Bargaining Agreement (CBA) between the Agency and the
National Treasury Employees Union described the procedures by which bargaining unit
employees would be scheduled and assigned overtime work. ROI at 198. For employees covered
by the Customs Officer Pay Reform Act (COPRA), overtime assignments would be made in
accordance with the following call-out order:

   1. volunteers in the overtime pool where payment of additional hours is not required;
   2. volunteers from part-time employees in the overtime pool where payment of additional
      hours is not required;
   3. at the Agency’s discretion, supervisors who are not fully integrated into the daily
      operational work schedule where payment of additional hours is not required;
   4. involuntary drafts in the overtime pool where payment of additional hours is not required;
   5. volunteers in the overtime pool where payment of additional hours is required;
   6. involuntary drafts in the overtime pool where payment of additional hours is required;
      and
   7. involuntary drafts from part-time employees in the overtime pool where payment of
      additional hours is required.

ROI at 201. In the event that the call-out order is exhausted, employees who have been granted
an excusal may be drafted for overtime assignments as a “last resort.” ROI at 201. Exceptions to
the procedures for assigning overtime include employees unavailable to report at the prescribed
time and employees who are unable to perform the full range of duties. ROI at 202. Article 35 of
the CBA provides that, subject to operational requirements, employees will be excused from
being assigned overtime involuntary drafts when management determines:

   1. a significant but temporary hardship exists;
   2. as a result of a temporary medical condition, an employee cannot perform the full range
       of duties required under the employee’s position;
   3. an employee who has worked on a regularly scheduled shift and overtime basis for 16 or
       more hours within a 24-hour period without a continuous eight-hour off-duty period;
   4. an employee on their last scheduled shift prior to a regular day off or scheduled annual
       leave; and
   5. any other valid reason, at management’s discretion.

ROI at 203.

On October 31, 2021, CRCL issued its second final decision on behalf of the Agency. The final
decision assumed, for the purposes of analysis, that Complainant was a qualified individual with
a disability based on his medical documentation, which required limited sitting and standing
work restrictions between August 2015 and March 2016. However, the Agency found that
Complainant did not establish a prima facie case of denial of a reasonable accommodation.
According to the Agency, Complainant conceded that he was not medically qualified for the full
range of CBPO duties, including primary traveler inspections, due to his medical restrictions.
The Agency stated that the CBPO production standards required prolonged sitting and standing
(for up to eight hours) to safely execute all essential tasks, including conducting primary and
secondary border inspections of travelers and vehicles. The Agency determined that, to the
extent that full-duty status and being exclusively assigned to NII work was Complainant’s
preferred accommodation, this would not be an effective accommodation. The Agency found
that, because this accommodation would not enable Complainant to perform the essential
functions of his position, it would not be effective. The Agency noted that it was not required to
eliminate an essential function or otherwise lower production standards as a reasonable
accommodation. Accordingly, the Agency concluded that the evidence in the record did not
establish that the Agency failed to provide Complainant a reasonable accommodation or that the
Agency inappropriately reassigned him to light duty as an accommodation in December 2015.

Considering Complainant’s placement on light duty as a disparate treatment claim, the Agency
found that management provided legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for assigning
Complainant to light duty. Specifically, management reassigned Complainant to light duty
because he provided documentation of medical restrictions that medically disqualified him from
performing essential duties of the full-duty CBPO position. The Agency determined that, to the
extent Complainant identified comparators, the comparators were either supervisors or managers
who were not similarly situated to Complainant or the comparators had different medical
conditions, different work restrictions, and/or worked under different chains of command. The
Agency found that Complainant failed to present any evidence of pretext.

Regarding Complainant’s allegation regarding official time, the final decision concluded that the
Agency provided Complainant and Union-1 with a reasonable amount of official time. Although
Chief-1 originally denied Union-1’s request for official time due to operational need, he
subsequently approved official time for Union-1. The Agency stated that Complainant and
Union-1 were provided six hours of official time in December 2015 and January 2016 during the
EEO counseling stage of Complainant’s complaint, and the Agency determined that this was a
reasonable amount of official time.

                                 CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

On appeal, Complainant contends that the Agency did not consider all of the evidence he
provided during the third supplemental investigation and failed to interview important witnesses,
including PD-1, PD-2, SCBPO-3, SCBPO-4, and CBPO-4. Complainant claims that the Agency
should have allowed him to amend his complaint, as he made his request prior to the conclusion
of the investigation. Complainant asserts that, as a member of the NII Team, his duties were
primarily sedentary and administrative, and he was able to perform all of the NII Operator duties
within his medical restrictions. Complainant argues that he could perform all of the essential
functions of his position and that overtime was not an essential function but rather a method of
accomplishing work. According to Complainant, the Agency applied CBPO requirements to
Complainant that were not applied to other CBPOs in his workgroup. Regarding overtime,
Complainant notes that the CBA provides for exceptions and excusals from overtime
assignments, including an automatic exception for employees who are unable to perform the full

accommodate him would have constituted undue hardship. Complainant requests the issuance of
a decision in his favor and appropriate remedies.

In response to Complainant’s appeal, the Agency contends that it supplemented the record as
ordered by the Commission with the second and third supplemental investigations. According to

basis by first-level supervisors. The Agency contends that supervisors often made these

Complainant had a medical condition substantially limited one or more major life activities.

The Agency also argues that there is no evidence in the record that the first-line supervisors who
received Complainant’s overtime excusal requests considered Complainant’s injury to be a

aligned with his medical restrictions. According to the Agency, Complainant was not a qualified

The Agency argues that, despite Complainant’s claim that the NII Team duties were

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part
1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard
of review “requires that the Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and
legal determinations of the previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents,

parties, and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and

Amendment Request

The Agency denied Complainant’s July 8, 2021, request to amend his EEO complaint to include
a hostile work environment claim and a claim that he was compelled to retire. At any time prior
to the Agency’s mailing of the notice required by 29 C.F.R. § 1614.108(f) at the conclusion of an
investigation, 29 C.F.R. § 1614.106(d) permits a complainant to amend a pending EEO
complaint to include issues or claims like or related to those raised in the complaint.” See EEO
MD-110, at Ch. 5, § III.B. A later claim or compliant is “like or related” to the original
complaint if the later claim or complaint adds to or clarifies the original complaint and could
have reasonably been expected to grow out of the original compliant during the investigation.
See Scher v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05940702 (May 30, 1995); Calhoun v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05891068 (Mar. 8, 1990).

On appeal, Complainant contends that the Agency should have allowed him to amend his
complaint because the investigation was not complete. As the Agency noted in its letter denying
Complainant’s amendment request, the Agency had transmitted notice of the right to request a
hearing to Complainant on July 18, 2016, and Complainant had requested the issuance of a final
decision. After the conclusion of the first supplemental investigation on December 5, 2016, the
Agency again provided Complainant with notice of his right to request a hearing pursuant to 29
C.F.R. § 1614.108(f), and, on December 13, 2016, Complainant again requested a final decision.
                                               13                                    2022000047

Although the Agency had not yet completed its third supplemental investigation when
Complainant requested to amend his EEO complaint, we do not find that the Agency erred in
denying Complainant’s request to amend the instant complaint. The Agency had already twice
provided Complainant notice of the right to request a hearing, and Complainant had already
twice elected a final decision. Our decision in EEOC Appeal No. 0120180153 ordered the
Agency to conduct a supplemental investigation because the investigative record was inadequate
concerning the claims accepted for investigation. Because Complainant had already requested
the issuance of a final decision, we ordered the Agency to issue a new final decision at the

EEO counselor.

Placement on Light Duty

Complainant alleged that he was subjected to discrimination based on disability, age, and reprisal
when, on December 7, 2015, he was placed on light duty after providing management with
updated medical documentation.

To prevail in a disparate treatment claim, Complainant must satisfy the three-part evidentiary
scheme fashioned by the Supreme Court in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792
(1973). Complainant must initially establish a prima facie case by demonstrating that he was
subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances that would support an inference
of discrimination. Furnco Constr. Co. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 576 (1978). Proof of a prima
facie case will vary depending on the facts of the particular case. McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S.
at 802 n. 13. In order to establish a prima facie case of disparate treatment under the
Rehabilitation Act, Complainant must demonstrate that: (1) he is an “individual with a
disability”; (2) he is “qualified” for the position held or desired; (3) he was subjected to an
adverse employment action; and (4) the circumstances surrounding the adverse action give rise
to an inference of discrimination. Lawson v. CSX Transp., Inc., 245 F.3d 916 (7th Cir. 2001).
The burden then shifts to the Agency to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its
actions. Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). To ultimately prevail,
Complainant must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the Agency’s explanation is
pretextual. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 120 S.Ct. 2097 (2000);
St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 519 (1993).

In order to establish that Complainant was denied a reasonable accommodation, Complainant
must show that: (1) he is an individual with a disability; (2) he is a qualified individual with a
disability; and (3) the Agency failed to provide a reasonable accommodation. See EEOC
Enforcement Guidance on Reasonable Accommodation and Undue Hardship Under the
Americans with Disabilities Act (RA Enforcement Guidance), No. 915.002 (Oct. 17, 2002). An
agency is required to make reasonable accommodation to the known physical and mental
limitations of a qualified individual with a disability unless the agency can show that
accommodation would cause an undue hardship. 29 C.F.R. §§ 1630.2(o) and (p).
                                                 14                                     2022000047

“The term “qualified,” with respect to an individual with a disability, means that the individual
satisfies the requisite skill, experience, education and other job-related requirements of the
employment position such individual holds or desires and, with or without reasonable
accommodation, can perform the essential functions of such position.” 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(m).

When making an accommodation request, an employee is not required to use
the magic words “reasonable accommodation.” See RA Enforcement Guidance at Q.1. Instead,
the employee need only inform the Agency that she needs an adjustment or change at work for a
reason related to a medical condition. See Triplett-Graham v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal
No. 0120044720 (Feb. 24, 2006). However, an Agency is permitted to seek documentation
where it is necessary to determine that the individual has a covered disability for which the
requested accommodation is necessary. See 29 C.F.R. § 1630.14; Karen P. v. Dep’t of Veterans
Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120181875 (Sept. 20, 2019). The Commission has recognized that
an agency is not required to remove any of the essential functions of a position as a reasonable
accommodation. See RA Enforcement Guidance. See also Larraine S. v. Dep’t of Agriculture,
EEOC Appeal No. 0120180647 (Aug. 15, 2019); Carlton T. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal
No. 0120151566 (Feb. 7, 2018); Timika O. v. Dep’t of the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 0220140008
(Mar. 9, 2017).

First, we consider whether Complainant is an individual with a disability. Complainant stated
that he has a herniated disc and pinched nerve in his back, which limits his ability to stand and sit
for an extended period of time. ROI at 19. Although Complainant averred in his June 10, 2016,
investigative affidavit that the long-term prognosis was unknown, by that time he had been living
with the impairment for more than four years. ROI at 19. Complainant also stated that the same
medical restrictions remained in effect through his return to full-duty status. ROI at 523.
Complainant indicated that he took pain medication to deal with the pain and discomfort of his
condition. ROI at 20. Accordingly, we find that Complainant established that he was
substantially limited in the major life activities of sitting and standing and, therefore, that he was
an individual with a disability.

Complainant must also show that he was a “qualified” individual with a disability within the
meaning of 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(m). The regulation defines such an individual as a disabled
person who, with or without a reasonable accommodation, can perform the essential functions of
the position in question. Essential functions “are the duties of a job,” that is, “the outcomes that
must be achieved by someone in that position.” Gilberto S. v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec., EEOC
Petition No. 0320110053 (July 10, 2014); Ta v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
0120080613 (Dec. 23, 2013); Finnegan v. Dep’t of the Air Force, EEOC Request No. 05980065
(Sept. 26, 2001). In determining whether an individual is qualified for a job, the Commission
asks whether that person can perform the essential functions of the job when at work. Gilberto
S., supra; Cottrell v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 07A00004 (Feb. 2, 2001);
McCullough v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 01942773 (April 25, 1996). Employers do
not have to eliminate essential functions of a position to accommodate an individual with a
disability. See RA Enforcement Guidance at “General Principles.”
                                               15                                    2022000047

We consider whether Complainant was a qualified individual with a disability with respect to the
CBP Officer position. The Agency contends that Complainant was not qualified for the CBPO
position because working overtime was an essential function of the position and Complainant
could not work all his involuntary overtime assignments. Although Complainant worked fewer
hours of overtime from 2012 to 2015 than the identified comparators holding the same position,
CBPO-1, CBPO-2, CBPO-3, and CBPO-4, Complainant worked 67.5 hours of overtime in 2012,
88.5 hours of overtime in 2013, 68.15 hours of overtime in 2014, and 20.5 hours of overtime in
2015. ROI at 508. Attendance and timing, including overtime, are methods by which a person
accomplishes the essential functions of the job and are subject to the Agency’s obligation to
provide a reasonable accommodation absent undue hardship. Gilberto S., supra (holding that the
ability to work significant amounts of overtime was not an essential function of the Customs and
Border Protection Officer). The Commission has recognized in precedential federal sector cases
that “the proper way to determine whether an individual is qualified for a position is to ask
whether that person can perform the essential functions of the position when at work.” Gilberto
S., supra, (emphasis in original) (citing Cottrell, supra; McCullough, supra; Ruiz v. U.S. Postal
Serv., EEOC Request No. 05880859 (May 21, 1990)). For a CBPO, the essential functions may
include inspection, intelligence analysis, examination, and law enforcement activities. ROI at
160. For a CBPO assigned to the NII team, essential functions appear to include operating the
NII imaging equipment, interpreting images, troubleshooting the technology, and daily
utilization reporting. ROI at 571-82. The record reflects that, since his 2012 injury, Complainant
received “successful” work appraisals. ROI at 21. There is no evidence in the record suggesting
that there were any deficiencies in the work performed by Complainant when he was in the full-
duty CBPO position. Moreover, Complainant’s unrebutted testimony that he was returned to full-
duty CBPO work in 2018 based on 2017 medical documentation with the same medical
restrictions as when he was placed on light duty in 2015 shows that he was able to perform the
fundamental duties of the CBPO position despite his medical restrictions. Accordingly, we find
that Complainant was a qualified individual with a disability who could perform the essential
functions of a Customs and Border Protection Officer.

Because Complainant was qualified and could perform the essential job duties of the CBPO
position, the appropriate question is whether the Agency was obligated to accommodate his
disability by excusing him from non-NII overtime assignments. The Agency must therefore
establish that excusing Complainant from such overtime assignments would cause undue
hardship. The Agency does not have to provide a reasonable accommodation that would cause an
undue hardship. Generalized conclusions will not suffice to support a claim of undue hardship.
Instead, undue hardship must be based on an individualized assessment that show that a specific
reasonable accommodation would cause significant difficulty or expense. See RA Enforcement
Guidance. A determination of undue hardship should be based on several factors, including:

   a.   the nature and cost of the accommodation needed;
   b.   the overall financial resources of the facility making the reasonable accommodation;
   c.   the number of persons employed at this facility;
   d.   the effect on expenses and resources at this facility;
                                               16                                    2022000047

   e. the overall financial resources, size, number of employees, and type of location of
      facilities of the employer (if the facility involved in the reasonable accommodation is part
      of a larger entity);
   f. the type of operation of the employer, including the structure and functions of the
      workforce, the geographic separateness, and the administrative or fiscal relationship of
      the facility involved in making the accommodation to the employer;
   g. the impact of the accommodation on the operation of the facility.

See 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(p); Id.

Upon review, we find that the Agency failed to establish by the preponderance of the evidence
that excusing Complainant from non-NII overtime assignments created an undue hardship on the
Agency’s operations. The Agency’s final decision stated that there were between 400 and 500
employees at the Hidalgo/Pharr POE who could execute CBPO duties. The record shows that
there were numerous reasons that a CBPO could be exempted from or request to be excused
from involuntary overtime assignments. In May 2012, management approved Complainant’s
request to be excused from overtime so he could keep his plans to drop off items he was donating
to his church. ROI at 396.

For more than three years, Complainant submitted requests for overtime excusals for non-NII
overtime assignments, and his requests were granted by various first-level supervisors. Chief-1
stated that, because overtime excusals were granted by individual supervisors and there were no
central records of such excusals, he was unaware of how often Complainant had requested to be
excused from overtime until he received Complainant’s updated medical documentation in
December 2015. Indeed, the Agency did not track overtime excusals based on medical
restrictions or overtime excusals on any basis. The fact that management did not notice that
Complainant was excused from overtime assignments upwards of 40 times since 2012 shows
that continuing to excuse Complainant from non-NII overtime assignments would not
significantly disrupt the facility’s operations.

We are also not convinced by the generalized assertions of various supervisory officials that
frequently excusing Complainant from overtime would lower morale among other CBPOs and
among the Supervisory CBPOs. Our Enforcement Guidance makes it clear that an employer
cannot claim undue hardship “based on the fact that provision of a reasonable accommodation
might have a negative impact on the morale of other employees.” See RA Enforcement
Guidance.

We find that the Agency failed to show that continuing to excuse Complainant from non-NII
overtime assignments would cause undue hardship. Accordingly, the preponderance of the
evidence in the record establishes that Complainant was denied a reasonable accommodation as
alleged when he was placed on light duty on December 7, 2015, until he was returned to full duty
on January 3, 2018. Based on our finding of disability discrimination, we will not analyze
Complainant’s claim that he was subjected to discrimination based on age and/or reprisal when
he was placed on light duty, as he would not be entitled to any additional relief.
                                               17                                    2022000047


An Agency is not liable for compensatory damages under the Rehabilitation Act where it has
consulted with Complainant and engaged in good faith efforts to provide a reasonable
accommodation, but has fallen short of what is legally required. See Teshima v. U.S. Postal
Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01961997 (May 5, 1998). Where an Agency has failed to provide a
reasonable accommodation, compensatory damages may be awarded if the Agency fails to
demonstrate that it made a good faith effort to provide the individual with a reasonable
accommodation for his disability. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a)(3); Gunn v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC
Appeal No. 0120053293 (June 15, 2007).

Here, we find sufficient evidence to conclude that Complainant is entitled to compensatory
damages. The Agency’s decision to place Complainant on light duty appears to have been made
in the absence of any consideration of the Agency’s obligation to provide reasonable
accommodation. See Beth G. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC Appeal No. 2019005972 (Nov.
19, 2020) (complainant entitled to compensatory damages where manager denied complainant’s
request for telework when experiencing a flare-up of symptoms without considering the agency’s
obligation to provide reasonable accommodation). Moreover, Complainant was forcibly
reassigned to the light duty position despite successfully performing the duties of his full-duty
position for more than three years after his injury. We therefore conclude that compensatory
damages are available to the extent Complainant establishes his entitlement to such damages on
remand. See West v. Gibson, 527 U.S. 212 (1999).

Denial of Official Time

Complainant alleged that the Agency denied requests made by his representative, Union-1, on
December 14, 16, and 22, 2015, for official time to participate in Complainant’s initial interview
with the EEO counselor. EEOC Regulation 29 C.F.R. § 1614.605(b) states, in relevant part, “If
the complainant is an employee of the agency, he or she shall have a reasonable amount of
official time, if otherwise on duty, to prepare the complaint and to respond to agency and EEOC
requests for information. If the complainant is an employee of the agency and he designates
another employee of the agency as his or her representative, the representative shall have a
reasonable amount of official time, if otherwise on duty, to prepare the complaint and respond to
agency and EEOC requests for information.”

The Commission has stated that a claim regarding the denial of official time concerns a violation
of the Commission’s regulation and does not require a determination of whether the denial was
motivated by discrimination. See Edwards v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Request No. 05960179
(Dec. 23, 1996). The Commission held that such a claim should not be processed in accordance
with 29 C.F.R. 1614.108 et seq., since the focus is not on the motivation, but rather on the
justification of why the complainant was denied a reasonable amount of official time. Edwards,
supra.
                                                18                                    2022000047

The actual number of hours to which a complainant is entitled will vary, depending on the nature
and complexity of the complaint and considering the mission of the agency and the agency’s
need to have its employees available to perform their normal duties on a regular basis. Whatever
time is spent in meetings and hearings with Agency officials or AJs processing a complaint is
automatically deemed reasonable. The Commission considers it reasonable for agencies to
expect their employees to spend most of their time doing the work for which they are employed.
EEO MD-110, Chap. 6 § VII.C. Therefore, an agency may restrict the overall hours of official
time afforded to a representative, for both preparation purposes and for attendance at meetings
and hearings, to a certain percentage of that representative’s duty hours in any given month,
quarter or year. Id.

Here, the record reflects that Chief-1 approved Complainant’s December 2015 requests for
official time to initiate contact with an EEO counselor and to participate in an interview with the
EEO counselor. Chief-1 initially denied Union-1’s December 2015 requests for official time to
represent Complainant for operational reasons, explaining that Union-1 was assigned to work
overtime at the time in question and that the Agency does not permit union representatives to
conduct official time while working overtime. However, after Union-1 filed a grievance on the
issue, the record reflects that the Agency compensated Union-1 with overtime pay for the time he
had spent representing Complainant. Under these circumstances, we do not find that the Agency
denied Complainant or his representative a reasonable amount of official time. Accordingly, we
affirm the Agency’s determination that Complainant was not denied a reasonable amount of
official time.

                                RECONSIDERATION (M0920)

The Commission may, in its discretion, reconsider this appellate decision if Complainant or the
Agency submits a written request that contains arguments or evidence that tend to establish that:
       1. The appellate decision involved a clearly erroneous interpretation of material fact or
          law; or
       2. The appellate decision will have a substantial impact on the policies, practices, or
          operations of the agency.

Requests for reconsideration must be filed with EEOC’s Office of Federal Operations (OFO)
within thirty (30) calendar days of receipt of this decision. If the party requesting
reconsideration elects to file a statement or brief in support of the request, that statement or
brief must be filed together with the request for reconsideration. A party shall have twenty
(20) calendar days from receipt of another party’s request for reconsideration within which to
submit a brief or statement in opposition. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405; Equal Employment
Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO MD-110), at Chap. 9 § VII.B
(Aug. 5, 2015).

Complainant should submit his or her request for reconsideration, and any statement or brief in
support of his or her request, via the EEOC Public Portal, which can be found at
https://publicportal.eeoc.gov/Portal/Login.aspx

Alternatively, Complainant can submit his or her request and arguments to the Director, Office
of Federal Operations, Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, via regular mail addressed
to P.O. Box 77960, Washington, DC 20013, or by certified mail addressed to 131 M Street, NE,
Washington, DC 20507. In the absence of a legible postmark, a complainant’s request to
reconsider shall be deemed timely filed if OFO receives it by mail within five days of the
expiration of the applicable filing period. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.604.

               COMPLAINANT’S RIGHT TO FILE A CIVIL ACTION (T0610)

This decision affirms the Agency’s final decision/action in part, but it also requires the Agency
to continue its administrative processing of a portion of your complaint. You have the right to
file a civil action in an appropriate United States District Court within ninety (90) calendar
days from the date that you receive this decision on both that portion of your complaint which
the Commission has affirmed and that portion of the complaint which has been remanded for
continued administrative processing. In the alternative, you may file a civil action after one
hundred and eighty (180) calendar days of the date you filed your complaint with the Agency,
or your appeal with the Commission, until such time as the Agency issues its final decision on
your complaint. If you file a civil action, you must name as the defendant in the complaint the
person who is the official Agency head or department head, identifying that person by his or her
full name and official title. Failure to do so may result in the dismissal of your case in court.
“Agency” or “department” means the national organization, and not the local office, facility or
department in which you work. If you file a request to reconsider and also file a civil action,
filing a civil action will terminate the administrative processing of your complaint.

                          RIGHT TO REQUEST COUNSEL (Z0815)

If you want to file a civil action but cannot pay the fees, costs, or security to do so, you may
request permission from the court to proceed with the civil action without paying these fees or
costs. Similarly, if you cannot afford an attorney to represent you in the civil action, you may
request the court to appoint an attorney for you. You must submit the requests for waiver of
court costs or appointment of an attorney directly to the court, not the Commission. The
court has the sole discretion to grant or deny these types of requests.
