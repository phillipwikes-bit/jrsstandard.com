                                   STANDARD OF REVIEW

In rendering this appellate decision we must scrutinize the AJ’s legal and factual conclusions,
and the Agency’s final order adopting them, de novo. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a) (stating that
a “decision on an appeal from an Agency’s final action shall be based on a de novo review . . .”);
see also Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614 (EEO
MD-110), at Chap. 9, § VI.B. (Aug. 5, 2015) (providing that an administrative judge’s
determination to issue a decision without a hearing, and the decision itself, will both be reviewed
de novo).

This essentially means that we should look at this case with fresh eyes. In other words, we are
free to accept (if accurate) or reject (if erroneous) the AJ’s, and Agency’s, factual conclusions
and legal analysis – including on the ultimate fact of whether intentional discrimination occurred,
and on the legal issue of whether any federal employment discrimination statute was violated.
See id. at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the

                                           ANALYSIS

The Commission’s regulations allow an AJ to issue a decision without a hearing upon finding
that there is no genuine issue of material fact. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.109(g). EEOC’s decision
without a hearing regulation follows the summary judgment procedure from federal court. Fed.
R. Civ. P. 56. The U.S. Supreme Court held summary judgment is appropriate where a judge
determines no genuine issue of material fact exists under the legal and evidentiary standards.
Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). In ruling on a summary judgment
motion, the judge is to determine whether there are genuine issues for trial, as opposed to
weighing the evidence. Id. at 249. At the summary judgment stage, the judge must believe the
non-moving party’s evidence and must draw justifiable inferences in the non-moving party’s
favor. Id. at 255. A “genuine issue of fact” is one that a reasonable judge could find in favor for
the non-moving party. Celotex v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986); Oliver v. Digital Equip.
Corp., 846 F.2d 103, 105 (1st Cir. 1988). A “material” fact has the potential to affect the
outcome of a case.

An AJ may issue a decision without a hearing only after determining that the record has been
adequately developed. See Petty v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 01A24206 (July 11, 2003).
We carefully reviewed the record and find that it is adequately developed. To successfully
oppose a decision without a hearing, Complainant must identify material facts of record that are
in dispute or present further material evidence establishing facts in dispute. Here, Complainants
appear to argue that the lack of a hearing denied them due process by having a full chance to
present their case. Complainants have not, however, identified any facts which are in dispute
and we emphasize that Complainants were provided the opportunity to respond after the
supplemental investigation and rebut the Agency’s additional statements. Because we find that
Complainants have not shown that there are any material facts at issue or any denial of due
process in the Agency’s investigation of their claims, we find that the AJ’s issuance of summary
judgment was proper. See Complainant v. Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC Appeal No. 0120121928
(May 15, 2014).

Religious Accommodation

The traditional framework for establishing a failure to provide a religious accommodation
requires a complainant to demonstrate that: “(1) [he] has a bona fide religious belief, the practice
of which conflicted with [his] employment, (2) [he] informed the agency of this belief and
conflict, and (3) the agency nevertheless enforced its requirement against [him].” See Baum v.
Social Security Administration, EEOC Appeal No. 01A05985 (Mar. 21, 2002). If the
complainant makes this showing, the burden shifts to the Agency to show either that it offered a
reasonable accommodation or that it could not reasonably accommodate the employee’s
religious practice or observance without undue hardship.
                                                6         2020000512, 2020000513, 2020000611

If the Agency makes this showing, it is entitled to prevail. Moreover, Title VII does not require
the Agency to offer an employee’s preferred accommodation or to demonstrate that an
employee’s preferred accommodation would cause an undue hardship. See generally EEOC v.
Abercrombie & Fitch Stores, Inc., 575 U.S. 768 (2015); EEOC Compliance Manual on Religious
Discrimination, Section 12 (Jan. 15, 2021).

The Supreme Court in Groff v. DeJoy, 600 U.S. 447 (2023), recently clarified that a showing of
undue hardship requires an employer to prove that “the burden of granting an accommodation
would result in substantial increased costs in relation to the conduct of its particular business.”
Groff, 500 U.S. at 470. The Court went on to state that Title VII requires an assessment of a
possible accommodation’s effect on “the conduct of the employer’s business,” which includes
“coworker impacts that go on to affect the conduct of the business.” Groff, 600 U.S. at 472.

In this case, the Agency is asserting it would impose an undue burden to excuse Complainants
from the training. The Senior Training Coordinator stated that granting an exemption would
mean the Agency could not hold an employee responsible if the employee violated federal
regulations prohibiting discrimination against the LGBTQI+ community, and thereby put the
Agency at risk of violating federal law. 6 See ROI 1, Ex. 8. The Training Chief stated that the
training did not either promote a lifestyle or encourage adopting a certain lifestyle or ask
employees to subscribe to a certain belief system, but rather only informed employees of the
requirement to treat all other employees with respect. See Supplemental ROI 1, Ex. 38.

Complainant 1 insists that the training “institutionalized” the LGBT lifestyle and that he is being
“forced to participate in emails and training that go against [his] Christian religious beliefs,”
including being “inundated” with emails pertaining to Pride Month and LGBT issues.
Complainants 2 and 3 both specifically point out the statement in the training that sexual
orientation is not a choice as going against their religious beliefs.

We find that Complainants have not established a prima facie case that the Agency violated Title
VII by denying them a religious accommodation because they did not establish an actual conflict
with their religious beliefs. To clarify, we find that they have not shown that merely attending
the training required them to modify their religious observance or practice or to change their
religious beliefs. While Complainant 1 specifically objected to receiving emails about Pride
Month, he has not identified how merely receiving such emails constituted being “forced to
participate” in any way. See, e.g., Latoya D. v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 0120170628
(March 29, 2017) (dissemination of information regarding LGBT events does not state a claim of
harassment); Bryce B. v. Dep’t of Defense, EEOC Appeal No. 0120160405 (Oct 29, 2016)

(exposure to display of posters, banners, and TV advertisements in support of LGBT Pride

to the specific statement made in the training about sexual orientation not being a choice, they do
not identify how or why merely listening to a statement with which they disagree would cause
them to change their religious beliefs. The training did not attack their beliefs or require
Complainants to attest to any change in their beliefs or encourage Complainants to endorse or
actively support LGBT rights. See Barrett V. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 2019005478
(March 7, 2024) (finding that attending a mandatory training on how to treat customers and
employees with respect, with particular emphasis on how this professional treatment applies to
LGBTQ+ individuals did not establish a conflict with the complainant’s religious beliefs). Here,
the training only informed Complainants of the requirement to treat all employees with respect
regardless of their protected bases, including sexual orientation and gender identity. Moreover,
Complainant 3 admitted that the training did not conflict with his religious beliefs as he asserted
that in his opinion, the training was unnecessary because his religious beliefs include “lov[ing]
your neighbor as you do yourself.” See ROI 3, Ex. 7.

To the extent that Complainants allege that there is a conflict between their religious beliefs and
the Agency’s mandatory training, we find that the Agency established that it would be an undue
burden for the Agency to exempt Complainants from attending the training. The Commission
and the courts have generally recognized that it would be an undue hardship to provide
exemptions from training on EEO laws and anti-discrimination policies because employers need
to make sure that their employees know and comply with such laws and workplace rules. See
EEOC Compliance Manual, Section 12, Religious Discrimination (Jan. 15, 2021) at Example 55
(stating that it may be an undue hardship to excuse an employee from any part of a training
where the training provides information on how to perform the job or on how to comply with
equal employment opportunity obligations); see also Peterson v. Hewlett-Packard Co., 358 F.3d
599 (9th Cir. 2004) (holding that it would have constituted an undue hardship to eliminate
portions of its diversity program to which employee raised religious objections because to do so
would have infringed upon the company’s right to encourage tolerance and good will among its
workforce.); Barrett V. v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No. 2019005478 (finding that granting
the complainant an exemption from the training on EEO laws would pose an undue hardship on
the conduct of the agency’s business). Similarly, in this case, as the Senior Training Coordinator
stated, allowing a religious exemption to these trainings would have potentially required the
Agency to be in violation of federal EEO laws by not allowing them to hold their employees
accountable for violations of the EEO laws. The Assistant Director for Field Operations further
explained that an employee who was given an exemption from the training would not be bound
by the Agency policy and workplace rules to treat all other employees, Forest Service customers,
or members of the public fairly and respectfully. See ROI 1, Ex. 13.

Complainants also contend that the Agency erred in not considering any possible
accommodations such as permitting them to sign a document stating that they had read the new
ruling that discrimination on the basis of sex included LGBT individuals. We reject
Complainants’ argument as it is not clear that such a bare acknowledgement would have been
adequate to prevent the Agency from being in violation of federal law.
                                                 8         2020000512, 2020000513, 2020000611

For example, Complainants’ assertion that they should be allowed to use the term “homosexual,”
as scientifically correct does not consider the history or the connotations associated with the use
of the word as a derogatory term. 7 Put another way, Complainants are inadvertently
demonstrating the very purpose of the training, to explain such historical contexts to enable them
to communicate with everyone in a respectful manner as required by Agency policy and federal
law. We note that the Supervisory Biological Scientist explained that in their positions as
Foresters, Complainants’ job duties entail working with civilians and federal and state employees
and they must interact with many different personnel. See ROI 1, Ex. 10. He stated that the
training was “a tool in assisting with understanding how to communicate with everyone.” See
id. In requesting an exemption from the training, Complainants were essentially asking to be
exempted from being adequately trained in what federal law requires, which cannot be
considered a reasonable request.

In this case, we find that Complainants have not established that being required to attend the
mandatory training placed any actual burden on their religious observance or practices nor have
they established that the training tried to modify their religious beliefs or required them to attest
to any such change in their beliefs. Even assuming arguendo that attending such a training could
be said to burden their religious beliefs, we find that the Agency established that it would have
been an undue hardship to provide a religious exemption for the training as doing so would have
laid the Agency open to potentially violating federal law and regulations, which require the
Agency to ensure all employees are properly trained on the requirements of the EEO laws. See,
e.g., Ansonia Bd. of Educ. v. Philbrook, 479 U.S. 60 (1986) (noting that Congress did not
impose a duty on the employer to accommodate at all costs); see also 29 C.F.R. §1614.102
(requiring agencies to communicate and inform their employees of the EEO laws in order to
prevent discrimination); EEO Management Directive 715, Section II, part B (requiring agencies
to provide training to employees to ensure proper enforcement of EEO laws).

                                          CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions on appeal, including those not
specifically addressed herein, we AFFIRM the Agency’s final orders finding that Complainants
did not establish that they were discriminated against as alleged.
