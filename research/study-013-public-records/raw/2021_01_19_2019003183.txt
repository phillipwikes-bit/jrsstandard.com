                                    STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b),
the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a).
See Equal Employment Opportunity Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9,
§ VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015) (explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the
Commission examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and testimony of
record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties, and . . . issue its decision
based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record and its interpretation of the law”).
                                   ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

Disparate Treatment

Complainant alleges that she was subjected to disparate treatment. A claim of disparate treatment
is examined under the three-part analysis first enunciated in McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green,
411 U.S. 792 (1973). For a complainant to prevail, she must first establish a prima facie case of
discrimination by presenting facts that, if unexplained, reasonably give rise to an inference of
discrimination, i.e., that a prohibited consideration was a factor in the adverse employment action.
McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802, n. 13; Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567 (1978).

Here, Complainant asserted that she was subjected to disparate treatment due to association with
her daughter and her daughter’s disability. A complainant attempting to establish a prima facie
case of discrimination by association under the Rehabilitation Act must establish: (1) that she was
subjected to an adverse employment action; (2) that she was qualified for the job at that time; (3)
that her employer knew at that time that she had a relationship with an individual with a disability;
and (4) that the adverse employment action occurred under circumstances which raised a
reasonable inference that the disability of the individual with whom she had a relationship was a
determining factor in [the employer's] decision. Den Hartog v. Wasatch Academy, 129 F.3d at
1085 (10th Cir. 1997) (applying the Americans with Disabilities Act, or ADA).

In Polifko v. Office of Personnel Mgt., EEOC Request No. 05940611 (Jan. 4, 1995), the
Commission observed that it is unlawful under the association provision to “exclude or deny equal
jobs or benefits to, or otherwise discriminate against” an individual based on the individual's
association with an individual with a known disability. 29 C.F.R. § 1630.8.

Once a prima facie case of discrimination is established, the burden then shifts to the agency to
articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its actions. Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v.
Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). Once the agency has met its burden, the complainant bears
the ultimate responsibility to persuade the fact finder by a preponderance of the evidence that the
agency acted on the basis of a prohibited reason. St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502
(1993).

This established order of analysis in discrimination cases, in which the first step normally consists
of determining the existence of a prima facie case, need not be followed in all cases. Where the
Agency has articulated a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the personnel action at issue, the
factual inquiry can proceed directly to the third step of the McDonnell Douglas analysis, the
ultimate issue of whether Complainant has shown by a preponderance of the evidence that the
Agency’s actions were motivated by discrimination. U.S. Postal Serv. Bd. of Governors v. Aikens,
460 U.S. 711, 713-714 (1983); Hernandez v. Dep’t. of Transp., EEOC Request No. 05900159
(June 28, 1990); Peterson v. Dep’t. of Health and Human Serv., EEOC Request No. 05900467
(June 8, 1990); Washington v. Dep’t. of the Navy, EEOC Petition No. 03900056 (May 31, 1990).

Upon our review of the voluminous evidentiary record which consists of numerous affidavits and
exhibits and having fully considered and analyzed the Agency’s decision which recites and
addresses each claim and identifies the evidentiary support on which it relied for its alleged actions,
we conclude that the preponderant evidence does not establish discriminatory animus. 5

Regarding Complainant’s disparate treatment claims, if we assume, arguendo, that Complainant
established a prima facie case of discrimination based on her association with her daughter’s
disability, and her protected EEO activity, we ultimately find that the Agency has articulated
legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its actions. For example, the record demonstrated that
Complainant had frequent issues with time management, whether arriving at or leaving work, or
utilizing the proper Idle Code when she stepped away from her desk to either use the restroom,
speak with a colleague, or get water. As a result, Complainant was often issued disciplinary
actions. The record here demonstrated that Complainant’s job required her to be on the phones,
and that punctuality was a key component to her work. Therefore, management’s concerns
regarding Complainant’s time management and proper usage of Idle Codes was simply part of
maintaining efficient business operations.

Actions taken by the alleged discriminating officials were routine managerial actions, such as
issuing discipline, maintaining workplace efficiency, and handling leave requests, which, absent
discriminatory animus, will not be second-guessed by the Commission. We note that agencies
generally have broad discretion to carry out personnel decisions and exercise business judgment.
Shapiro v. Soc. Sec. Admn., EEOC Request No. 05960403 (Dec. 6, 1996).

Additionally, Complainant repeatedly acknowledged using her phone at work, either listening to
music, or simply having her phone. Management was displeased with the amount Complainant
used her phone during work hours, and as a result, issued memorandums and disciplinary actions
to Complainant. In this matter, Complainant argued that the actions were retaliatory and
discriminatory. However, we again find that Complainant has failed to demonstrate a nexus
between the adverse actions and her associational disability and/or prior EEO activity.

Complainant also acknowledged that she often left work 10 minutes early, an issue that
management routinely spoke to Complainant about. Complainant was also frequently issued
AWOLs to due to leaving early. Complainant argued that the AWOLs were discriminatorily issued
as AWOLS were only meant to be issued by fifteen-minute increments. In this matter, we find
Complainant’s argument unpersuasive. Complainant acknowledged her own habit of leaving 10
minutes early. This 5-minute difference does not mean the Agency or management has to allowed
Complainant to frequently leave early with no recourse. Complainant again has failed to provide
evidence to demonstrate that there was a nexus between the AWOLs she received and her
association with her daughter’s disability or retaliation for participating in EEO activity.

Lastly, to the extent that Complainant claims the Agency violated her FMLA rights associated
with her daughter’s care, we note that the FMLA falls under the regulatory ambit of the Department
of Labor, not the Commission. Therefore, the Commission has no jurisdiction over this type of
claim. See Complainant v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0120122478 (Sept. 26, 2012).

Harassment

In her harassment claim, in addition to the incidents discussed above, Complainant alleged a
variety of incidents ranging from being issued AWOLs; issued a variety of disciplinary actions;
deduction of overtime minutes; having management frequently walk by her desk to monitor her;
intentionally not providing her with work; and, constantly issuing her memorandums about her
work place behavior and performance.

To establish a claim of harassment a complainant must show that: (1) she belongs to a statutorily
protected class; (2) she was subjected to harassment in the form of unwelcome verbal or physical
conduct involving the protected class; (3) the harassment complained of was based on her
statutorily protected class; (4) the harassment affected a term or condition of employment and/or
had the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with the work environment and/or creating
an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) there is a basis for imputing
liability to the employer. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897 (11th Cir. 1982).
                                                 9                                      2019003183

Further, the incidents must have been “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of
[complainant’s] employment and create an abusive working environment.” Harris v. Forklift
Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993).

To prove her harassment claim, Complainant must establish that she was subjected to conduct that
was either so severe or so pervasive that a “reasonable person” in Complainant’s position would
have found the conduct to be hostile or abusive. Complainant must also prove that the conduct
was taken because of a protected basis – in this case, her association with her daughter’s disability
as the primary caregiver and/or retaliatory animus. We note that only if Complainant establishes
both of those elements – hostility and motive – will the question of Agency liability present itself.

Here, as already concluded above, there is no evidence to support a finding that Complainant’s
associational disability or prior protected activity played any role whatsoever in the Agency’s
actions. Moreover, the responsible management officials provided a legitimate, non-
discriminatory explanation for each claim alleged. A finding of harassment on these claims is
precluded by our determination that Complainant failed to establish that the actions taken by the
Agency were motivated by discriminatory or retaliatory animus. See Oakley v. U.S. Postal Serv.,
EEOC Appeal No. 01932923 (Sep. 21, 2000). In sum, Complainant failed to prove that her
associational disability or retaliatory animus played any role in the incidents she proffered as
evidence of her harassment claim.

                                          CONCLUSION

Based on a thorough review of the record and the contentions on appeal, including those not
specifically addressed or referenced herein, we AFFIRM the Agency’s finding that it did not
discriminate or retaliate against Complainant as alleged.
