                                           DECISION

On December 3, 2020, Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment Opportunity
Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(a), from the Agency’s
October 28, 2020, final decision concerning an equal employment opportunity (EEO) complaint
alleging employment discrimination in violation of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964
(Title VII), as amended, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., and Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act of
1973 (Rehabilitation Act), as amended, 29 U.S.C. § 791 et seq.

                                        BACKGROUND

During the period at issue, Complainant worked as Management and Program Analyst and
Contracting Officer Representative (COR), GS-14, at the Information Technology Services
Office for the Agency’s headquarters facility in Washington, District of Columbia.

In November 2011, Complainant was in a vehicle accident that left her with injuries that have
been characterized as disabling and permanent.

On December 19, 2012, Complainant filed a formal EEO complaint alleging that the Agency
discriminated against her on the bases of sex (female), disability (lumbar, thoracic, and cervical
sprain/strain, muscle spasms with associated shoulder sprain/strain, post traumatic headaches,

                                 ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS

On appeal, Complainant challenges the compensatory damages award provided by the Agency.
Counsel for Complainant contends that, for non-pecuniary compensatory damages, $100,000
was inadequate. Complainant argues that the AJ’s award did not properly account for the pain
she has suffered from fibromyalgia. Counsel for Complainant submits statements from
Complainant, her chiropractor and her neurologist. Complainant’s counsel asserted that non-
pecuniary compensatory damages should be increased because Complainant’s neurologist and
endocrinologist testified that Graves’ Disease had been exacerbated by anxiety attributable to the
Agency’s discriminatory actions. Complainant also cited sworn affidavits from Complainant,
family members regarding increased non-pecuniary compensatory damages. In sum,
Complainant’s attorney maintains that an award of $250,000 was justified based on similar cases
where agency denials of accommodations had resulted in similar harm.

In addition, Complainant’s attorney challenges the Agency’s decision to deny pecuniary
compensatory damages. According to her Counsel, Complainant stated that she should have
been awarded pecuniary compensation in the amount of $26,141.15. The appellate brief justified

Pecuniary Damages

Pecuniary losses include for example, moving expenses, job search expenses, medical expenses,
psychiatric expenses, physical therapy expenses, and other quantifiable out-of-pocket expenses
that are incurred as a result of the discriminatory conduct. To recover damages, Complainant had
to prove that the discrimination by the Agency caused her losses.         The critical question is
whether Complainant incurred the pecuniary losses as a result of the Agency’s discriminatory
conduct. Enforcement Guidance: Compensatory and Punitive Damages Available under § 102
of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 (EEOC Damages Guidance), EEOC Notice No. 915.002 (July
14, 1992). The Agency’s pecuniary liability is limited to those damages that Complainant has
clearly shown to be caused by the Agency’s discriminatory conduct. Timothy M. v. Dep’t of the
Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 2019001562 (Feb. 4, 2020) citing EEO Management Directive for 29
C.F.R. Part 1614 at Ch. 11, § VII.B (Aug. 5, 2015).

On appeal, Complainant has asserted that she is entitled to pecuniary compensation from the
Agency for spinal care provided by her chiropractor. Complainant asserted that the Agency’s
discrimination delayed her recovery in a manner that cost her $15,285 in visits to her
chiropractor between January 2, 2013, to July 9, 2015. From the record, it is unclear whether
Complainant’s visits to the chiropractor were caused by failure to accommodate. The Agency
evidenced that Complainant’s need for chiropractic care was characterized as “permanent”
during her successful lawsuit against the other driver who was deemed responsible for those
particular injuries that were inflicted by the 2011 car accident. We therefore find that the
Agency’s decision not to award pecuniary damages for visiting her chiropractor was proper.

Complainant’s appellate brief also argued that the Agency should have paid an additional $2,745
in pecuniary damages so that she could recoup the costs from the treatment received from her
neurologist because of pain and fibromyalgia. In medical documents dated from June 17, 2013,
to August 25, 2015, Complainant’s neurologist expressed concerns about her physical ability to
return to working in the office and also stated that Complainant had reported stress at work.
However, the neurologist’s letters did not clearly explain how the discriminatory acts found to
have occurred exacerbated Complainant’s pain and fibromyalgia as opposed to the injuries that
she had already sustained as a result of the vehicular accident. Consequently, the Agency’s
denial of pecuniary damages for the neurologist’s treatment was appropriate.

Complainant claimed an additional $700 in damages owing to medical treatment from her
endocrinologist that she received between June 17, 2013 until February 5, 2016.
                                                 5                                    2021001179

According to Complainant’s Counsel, such treatment was provided by her neurologist for the
Agency’s discrimination exacerbating the conditions of Complainant’s Graves Disease. The
record revealed that Complainant had been diagnosed with Graves Disease as early as 2008 and
received regular treatment thereafter. We find that the treatment records do not support
Complainant’s argument that four visits to the neurologist were due to exacerbation of her
Graves Disease on account of the relevant instances of discrimination. Therefore, the Agency
properly decided to deny Complainant’s damages that were claimed for the visits to her
endocrinologist.

Furthermore, from this record, it was difficult to discern how the Agency’s discriminatory
actions or omissions aggravated Complainant’s pre-existing neck and lower back afflictions. For
these medical issues, Complainant sought to recover out-of-pocket expenses totalling
$24,371.92, based on various medical procedures provided between January 2, 2013 to February
8, 2016. Two of the medical professionals who addressed Complainant’s neck and lower back
pain had attributed these problems to the November 2011 car accident rather than Agency
discrimination. Moreover, instead of showing exacerbation, the Agency highlighted evidence
that Complainant’s neck and lower back health improved during the timeframe when the
discriminatory acts occurred. As a result, we find that Complainant did not establish causation
necessary to prove entitlement for damages related to her neck and back conditions.

Non-Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

Non-pecuniary compensatory damages are losses that are not subject to precise quantification,
i.e., emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, injury to
professional standing, injury to character and reputation, injury to credit standing, and loss of
health. EEOC Enforcement Guidance: Compensatory and Punitive Damages Available under §
102 of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 (EEOC Enforcement Guidance), EEOC Notice No. 915.002
(July 14, 1992). Although there is no precise formula by which to calculate nonpecuniary
damages, the method for computing non-pecuniary damages should be based on the severity and
the duration of harm Complainant suffered. Carpenter v. Dep’t of Agric., EEOC Appeal No.
01945652 (July 17, 1995). Objective evidence in support of a claim for non-pecuniary damages
claims includes statements from Complainant and others, including family members, co-workers,
and medical professionals. EEOC Enforcement Guidance at 10; Carle v. Dep’t of the Navy,
EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan. 5, 1993). Non-pecuniary damages must be limited to
compensation for the actual harm suffered as a result of discriminatory actions by the Agency.
EEOC Enforcement Guidance at 13; Cortez J. v. Dep’t of Def., EEOC Appeal No. 0120182712
(Nov. 29, 2019) citing Carter v. Duncan-Higgans. Ltd., 727 F.2d 1225 (D.C. Cir. 1994). A
proper award of non-pecuniary compensatory damages should not be monstrously excessive; a
proper non-pecuniary compensatory damages award cannot be a product of passion or prejudice;
it should be consistent with non-pecuniary compensatory damages awarded in similar cases.
Ward-Jenkins v. Dep’t of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No. 01961483 (Mar. 4, 1999).

Here, Complainant has described emotional and physical distress as causally connected to the
Agency delaying reasonable accommodations or providing inadequate ones.
                                                6                                    2021001179

Complainant and her mother stated that the inadequate IT equipment provided by the Agency
caused Complainant to “lash-out” at her parents and become estranged from her siblings.
According to Complainant’s co-workers, while denied telework, they witnessed that
Complainant having to exert herself in the office caused pain and frustration to a degree that they
witnessed swelling of the joints in her hands. They also testified that the Agency’s actions
humiliated her and damaged her reputation. Complainant’s psychiatrist stated that the
discrimination had caused Complainant’s depression and PTSD. The psychiatrist assessed
Complainant as twelve percent mentally disabled directly from the discrimination at issue in the
present case.

Even after having considered all of the aforementioned testimonial evidence within the record,
we find that the Agency’s non-pecuniary compensatory damages award was not arbitrary or
capricious. Our determination supported by recent decisions finding $100,000 appropriate
following findings of similar Rehabilitation Act violations in denying or delaying reasonable
accommodations to federal employees with disabilities. In Elsa S. v. Nat’l Aeronautics and
Space Admin., EEOC Appeal No. 0720180021 (Feb. 14, 2020), we awarded $100,000 in non-
pecuniary compensatory damages for an agency’s delay in reasonably accommodating the
complainant despite her multiple requests, where complainant’s underlying disabling conditions
were exacerbated and also caused stress, embarrassment, depression, and loss of confidence. In
Clifford L. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 2021001926 (Feb. 2, 2022), the Commission
increased non-pecuniary compensatory damages to $100,000, based on finding that the agency
had failed to reasonably accommodate complainant over a period of five years. In Liz M. v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 0720180020 (Oct. 18, 2018), EEOC awarded $100,000 in non-
pecuniary compensatory damages for the agency's failure to provide the complainant a
reasonable accommodation for approximately one year. In Samuel R. v. Dep’t of Veterans
Affairs, EEOC Appeal No. 0120140216 (Feb. 25, 2015), we awarded non-pecuniary
compensatory damages of $100,000, where a complainant had suffered additional physical harm
and psychological harm from an agency’s failing to accommodate an obvious pre-existing
medical condition. For non-pecuniary compensatory damages, the Agency decision awarding
$100,000 was consistent with the aforementioned cases with similar fact patterns. Moreover, we
find that such an award was not “monstrously excessive” standing alone, nor was it the product
of passion or prejudice.

                                         CONCLUSION

Accordingly, we AFFIRM the Agency’s final order implementing the AGENCY’s decision and
we REMAND this matter to the Agency to implement the relief awarded in accordance with the
ORDER below.

            IMPLEMENTATION OF THE COMMISSION’S DECISION (K0719)

Under 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(c) and §1614.502, compliance with the Commission’s corrective
action is mandatory. Within seven (7) calendar days of the completion of each ordered
corrective action, the Agency shall submit via the Federal Sector EEO Portal (FedSEP)
supporting documents in the digital format required by the Commission, referencing the
compliance docket number under which compliance was being monitored. Once all compliance
is complete, the Agency shall submit via FedSEP a final compliance report in the digital format
required by the Commission. See 29 C.F.R. § 1614.403(g). The Agency’s final report must
contain supporting documentation when previously not uploaded, and the Agency must send a
copy of all submissions to Complainant and his/her representative.

If the Agency does not comply with the Commission’s order, Complainant may petition the
Commission for enforcement of the order. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(a). Complainant also has the
right to file a civil action to enforce compliance with the Commission’s order prior to or
following an administrative petition for enforcement. See 29 C.F.R. §§ 1614.407, 1614.408, and
29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(g). Alternatively, Complainant has the right to file a civil action on the
underlying complaint in accordance with the paragraph below entitled “Right to File a Civil
Action.” 29 C.F.R. §§ 1614.407 and 1614.408. A civil action for enforcement or a civil action
on the underlying complaint is subject to the deadline stated in 42 U.S.C. 2000e-16(c) (1994 &
Supp. IV 1999). If Complainant files a civil action, the administrative processing of the
complaint, including any petition for enforcement, will be terminated. See 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.409.

Failure by an agency to either file a compliance report or implement any of the orders set forth in
this decision, without good cause shown, may result in the referral of this matter to the Office of
Special Counsel pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.503(f) for enforcement by that agency.
