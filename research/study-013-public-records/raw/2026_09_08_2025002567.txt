On April 25, 2025, Complainant filed an appeal with the Equal Employment
Opportunity Commission (EEOC or Commission), pursuant to 29 C.F.R. §
1614.403(a), from the Agency’s March 28, 2025, final decision concerning his equal

                                  BACKGROUND

At the time of events giving rise to this complaint, Complainant worked as a Nuclear
Security Officer at the Agency’s Browns Ferry Nuclear Plant in Decatur, Alabama.

On December 16, 2022, Complainant filed an EEO complaint alleging that the
Agency discriminated against him on the basis of disability (pseudofolliculitis
barbae) when on December 1, 2022, the Agency denied his reasonable
accommodation request.

At the conclusion of the investigation, the Agency provided Complainant with a
copy of the report of investigation and notice of his right to request a hearing before
an EEOC Administrative Judge (AJ). In accordance with Complainant’s request,
the Agency issued a final decision pursuant to 29 C.F.R. § 1614.110(b). The
decision concluded that Complainant proved that the Agency denied him a
reasonable accommodation when it revoked his security clearance due to his
inability to shave because the Agency changed the gas masks it used to one that
required a person not to have any facial hair. Complainant was placed in non-pay
status on December 1, 2022, and ultimately resigned on October 18, 2023, which
Complainant characterized as a constructive discharge. The decision therefore
ordered that the Agency reinstate Complainant to his previous position as a Nuclear
Security Officer and provide him with reasonable accommodation; conduct a
supplemental investigation into compensatory damages; expunge any discipline
related to Complainant’s failure to be clean-shaven from his personnel record;
provide training to the responsible management officials; and post a notice regarding
the discrimination finding.

Complainant requested backpay, pecuniary compensatory damages in the total
amount of $33,778.31 (comprising $13,055.94 for his lost Agency contribution to
his TSP and the tax penalty for early withdrawal of his TSP benefits; $5,722.37 in
mileage to his new employment; and $15,000.00 in death benefits for his late
daughter). Complainant also sought nonpecuniary compensatory damages up to
$300,000.00 for the weight loss and insomnia he suffered due to the Agency’s denial
of his reasonable accommodation, severe irritable bowel syndrome, depression and
anxiety for which he is on medication; and withdrawal from his friendships, which
were all exacerbated by his grief over the loss of his daughter on October 28, 2023.
                                         3                             2025002567

In support of his request, Complainant submitted medical documentation of his
diagnoses, an affidavit from himself and his wife; documentation of his early
withdrawal from his retirement fund; and documentation of his mileage costs to his
new employment. Complainant also sought attorney’s fees in the total amount of
$26,395.40 (comprising 48.7 hours of work at an hourly rate of $542).

The Agency issued a decision on damages, denying Complainant’s request for
pecuniary damages for his tax penalty for early withdrawal from his retirement
funds, mileage reimbursement, and a death benefit because it found that
Complainant had not provided sufficient documentation to support the amount or to
establish that the losses were due to the Agency’s discrimination. The Agency
awarded Complainant backpay in the amount of $85,766.97 and $6,264.72 for lost
retirement contributions and $10,000.00 in nonpecuniary compensatory damages.
The Agency further awarded Complainant an attorney’s fee award in the amount of
$19,480.00, comprising 48.7 hours of work at an hourly rate of $400.

                          CONTENTIONS ON APPEAL

On appeal, Complainant argues that the Agency erred in not awarding pecuniary
compensatory damages as requested because Complainant established that he was
entitled to them. Complainant also contends that the Agency’s award of $10,000.00
was inadequate to compensate Complainant for the extent and severity of the
physical and emotional harm he suffered. Lastly, Complainant argues that the
Agency erred in determining the appropriate hourly rate for Complainant’s counsel
was $400 rather than $542.

In response, the Agency argued that its award of damages was supported by the
record and case law and should be affirmed. Specifically, the Agency noted that
Complainant was not entitled to a death benefit for the loss of his daughter because
it would not have been paid by the Agency even if Complainant had still been
employed at the time of his daughter’s death but rather would have been payable by
Complainant’s insurance, MetLife. The Agency also contends that Complainant has
not established that he is entitled to nonpecuniary compensatory damages greater
than $30,000.00 at most.

                            STANDARD OF REVIEW

As this is an appeal from a decision issued without a hearing, pursuant to 29 C.F.R.
§ 1614.110(b), the Agency's decision is subject to de novo review by the
Commission. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.405(a). See Equal Employment Opportunity
Management Directive for 29 C.F.R. Part 1614, at Chapter 9, § VI.A. (Aug. 5, 2015)
(explaining that the de novo standard of review “requires that the Commission
examine the record without regard to the factual and legal determinations of the
previous decision maker,” and that EEOC “review the documents, statements, and
testimony of record, including any timely and relevant submissions of the parties,
and . . . issue its decision based on the Commission’s own assessment of the record
and its interpretation of the law”).

                                    ANALYSIS

Pecuniary Compensatory Damages

When discrimination is found, an agency must provide the complainant with a
remedy that constitutes full, make-whole relief to restore him as nearly as possible
to the position she would have occupied absent the discrimination. See, e.g., Franks
v. Bowman Transp. Co., 424 U.S. 747, 764 (1976); Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody,
422 U.S. 405, 418-19 (1975); Adesanya v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
01933395 (July 21, 1994). Pursuant to section 102(a) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991,
a complainant who establishes unlawful intentional discrimination under Title VII
and the Rehabilitation Act may receive compensatory damages for past and future
pecuniary losses (i.e., out-of-pocket expenses) and nonpecuniary losses (e.g., pain
and suffering, mental anguish) as part of make-whole relief. 42 U.S.C. §
1981a(b)(3). Compensatory damages do not include back pay, interest on back pay,
or any other type of equitable relief. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(2). In West v. Gibson,
527 U.S. 212 (1999), the Supreme Court held that Congress afforded the
Commission the authority to award compensatory damages in the administrative
process.

Pecuniary losses are out-of-pocket expenses incurred because of the agency's
unlawful action, including job-hunting expenses, moving expenses, medical
expenses, psychiatric expenses, physical therapy expenses, and other quantifiable
out-of-pocket expenses.
                                          5                              2025002567

Past pecuniary losses are losses incurred prior to the resolution of a complaint
through a finding of discrimination, or a voluntary settlement. EEO MD-110, at
Chap. 11, VII.B.2 (internal citations omitted).

In a claim for pecuniary, compensatory damages, a complainant must demonstrate,
through appropriate evidence and documentation, the harm suffered because of the
agency’s discriminatory action. See Sheila O. v. Dep’t of Health and Human Srvcs.,
EEOC Appeal No. 2021001250 (June 7, 2022). Objective evidence in support of a
claim for pecuniary damages includes documentation showing actual out-of-pocket
expenses with an explanation of the expenditure. The agency is only responsible for
those damages that are clearly shown to be caused by the Agency’s discriminatory
conduct. To recover damages, a complainant must prove that the employer's
discriminatory actions were the cause of the pecuniary loss. Id. (citations omitted).

The Commission has held that an award of pecuniary damages is proper to
compensate a complainant for tax penalties which result from early withdrawals
from a retirement plan. Gonzales v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
0720080027 (May 30, 2008). Complainant requested that the Agency award him
$5,027.60 for the tax liability as a result of his early withdrawal from the TVA
retirement system. We agree with Complainant that he is entitled to compensation
for the tax penalty he incurred as a result of his early withdrawal from his retirement
account. Contrary to the Agency’s argument on appeal, we do not find that the mere
fact that Complainant made the withdrawal in January 2024, almost a year after he
stopped receiving payments from the Agency, is dispositive as to whether
Complainant established that the early withdrawal was due to the Agency’s actions.
Complainant’s affidavit in support of his request explained that he withdrew from
his retirement account in order to pay off the debts he had incurred in the previous
months due to his loss of income.

We find that Complainant submitted sufficient documentation to support his request.
On appeal, Complainant submitted a copy of the TVA retirement system Benefit
Cashout Application which noted that a 20% tax penalty would be incurred for an
early withdrawal. In his request for pecuniary damages, Complainant submitted a
copy of an email from the TVA retirement system confirming that he had made a
pension cashout on January 16, 2024, of $25,138.00, “less taxes,” in the amount of
$5,027.60. We therefore find that Complainant is entitled to the amount of
$5,027.60 to compensate him for the tax penalty from his early withdrawal from his
retirement account and modify the Agency’s final order accordingly.
                                         6                              2025002567

Complainant also argues that the Agency erred in denying his request for
compensation for the mileage he incurred for his commute to his new employment,
stating that due to the loss of his job with the Agency, Complainant was forced to
accept another job 16.5 miles away for a total commuting distance of 33 miles.
Complainant contends that he is entitled to $6,696.20 for the mileage he incurred
through the last day of his employment at the other job. We agree with Complainant
that he is entitled to be reimbursed for the mileage he incurred for his commute to
the new employment he took when he lost his job at the Agency due to its failure to
accommodate him. Contrary to the Agency’s argument on appeal, we find that
Complainant has provided sufficient documentation to support his request.
Complainant submitted a copy of one of his pay stubs from his new employer,
indicating that in one week, he worked 40 hours, which is consistent with 5 working
days. Complainant also submitted a chart documenting the number of days he
worked in any given period. Under the circumstances, we find that this is sufficient
documentation of his entitlement to mileage. See Marybeth C. v. Dep’t of Health
and Human Srvcs., EEOC Appeal No. 0120170811 (June 11, 2019) (affirming an
Agency’s award of mileage reimbursement where it was supported only by the
complainant’s own statement). We therefore find that Complainant is entitled to
mileage damages in the amount of $6,696.20.

Lastly, Complainant argues that he is entitled to death benefits in the amount of
$15,000.00 for the amount the Agency would have paid him on the event of his
insured daughter’s death if Complainant had still been employed by the Agency.
The Agency argues that the death benefit is a type of optional dependent insurance
administered by MetLife, not the Agency, and therefore, Complainant must make
any claim to MetLife. We agree with the Agency that Complainant is not entitled to
damages in the form of death benefits for his late daughter because the evidence
indicates that Complainant’s entitlement to such a benefit needs to be adjudicated
by MetLife as the insurer. Moreover, because it is a form of optional insurance,
contrary to Complainant’s argument on appeal, it is not among the employment
benefits that Complainant would automatically have been entitled to had he still been
employed by the Agency. We therefore deny Complainant’s request for death
benefits as a form of pecuniary damages.

Nonpecuniary Compensatory Damages

Nonpecuniary losses are losses that are not subject to precise quantification, i.e.,
emotional pain, suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life,
injury to professional standing, injury to character and reputation, injury to credit
standing, and loss of health. See EEOC Notice No. 915.302, Enforcement Guidance
on Compensatory and Punitive Damages Available Under Section 102 of the Civil
Rights Act of 1991, at 10 (July 14, 1992). There is no precise formula for
determining the amount of damages for nonpecuniary losses except that the award
should reflect the nature and severity of the harm and the duration or expected
duration of the harm. See Loving v. Dep’t of the Treasury, EEOC Appeal No.
01955789 (Aug. 29, 1997). The Commission notes that nonpecuniary compensatory
damages are designed to remedy the harm caused by the discriminatory event rather
than punish the agency for the discriminatory action. Furthermore, compensatory
damages should not be motivated by passion or prejudice or “monstrously
excessive” standing alone but should be consistent with the amounts awarded in
similar cases. See Ward-Jenkins v. Dep’t of the Interior, EEOC Appeal No.
01961483 (Mar. 4, 1999).

Evidence from a health care provider or other expert is not a mandatory prerequisite
for recovery of compensatory damages for emotional harm. See Lawrence v. U.S.
Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No. 01952288 (Apr. 18, 1996) (citing Carle v. Dep’t of
the Navy, EEOC Appeal No. 01922369 (Jan. 5, 1993)). Objective evidence of
compensatory damages can include statements from a complainant concerning his
emotional pain or suffering, inconvenience, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of
life, injury to professional standing, injury to character or reputation, injury to credit
standing, loss of health, and any other nonpecuniary losses that are incurred as a
result of the discriminatory conduct. Id.

Statements from others including family members, friends, health care providers,
and other counselors (including clergy) could address the outward manifestations or
physical consequences of emotional distress, including sleeplessness, anxiety, stress,
depression, marital strain, humiliation, emotional distress, loss of self-esteem,
excessive fatigue, or a nervous breakdown. Id. Complainant’s own testimony, along
with the circumstances of a particular case, can suffice to sustain her burden in this
regard. Id.
                                          8                              2025002567

The more inherently degrading or humiliating the defendant's action is, the more
reasonable it is to infer that a person would suffer humiliation or distress from that
action. Id. The absence of supporting evidence, however, may affect the amount of
damages appropriate in specific cases. Id.

Complainant has the burden of proving the existence, nature and severity of the
alleged emotional harm. Man H. v. Dep’t. of Homeland Sec’y, EEOC Appeal No.
0120161218 (May 2, 2017). Complainant must also establish a causal relationship
between the alleged harm and the discrimination. Id. Absent such proof of harm and
causation, a complainant is not entitled to compensatory damages, even if there were
a finding of unlawful discrimination. Id.; Wilda M. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC
Appeal No. 0120141087 (Jan. 12, 2017) (awards for emotional harm are warranted
only if complainant establishes a sufficient causal connection between the agency’s
illegal actions and her injury).

On appeal, Complainant disagrees with the Agency’s decision to award him only
$10,000.00 in nonpecuniary compensatory damages. Complainant maintains that he
suffered from severe irritable bowel syndrome, lost his appetite resulting in a loss of
20 pounds, suffered from insomnia and depression, and withdrew from his friends,
and that the new job he took when he was forced to resign from his position with the
Agency is more physically demanding and has caused ongoing joint and muscle
pain.

Here, while we find that the evidence in the record is not sufficient to support an
award of more than $100,000.00 as Complainant argues, we agree with Complainant
that the Agency’s award of $10,000.00 was insufficient to compensate Complainant
for the harm he suffered as a result of the Agency’s failure to accommodate his
disability. While the medical documentation Complainant submitted indicates that
Complainant had suffered from anxiety and insomnia even prior to the Agency’s
discrimination, we find that the medical documentation, as well as Complainant’s
own testimony and that of his wife, establish that Complainant’s insomnia,
depression, and anxiety were significantly exacerbated due to the Agency’s
discrimination because the medical notes mentioned the primary stressors in
Complainant’s life as being the loss of his previous job at the Agency and the effect
of his daughter’s suicide.
                                           9                              2025002567

We believe that an award of nonpecuniary damages in the amount of $50,000.00 is
appropriate. We find that this amount is not motivated by passion or prejudice, is
not “monstrously excessive” standing alone, and is consistent with the amounts
awarded in other cases. See Chere S. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
2023000107 (May 19, 2025) (affirming an AJ’s award of $60,000.00 where the
complainant suffered severe exacerbation of pre-existing migraines, depression, and
withdrawal from relationships but where the harms were caused partly due to the
deaths of some family members); Elsa O. v. U.S. Postal Serv., EEOC Appeal No.
2022003509 (Aug. 4, 2023) (awarding the complainant $50,000.00 where the
Agency’s denial of reasonable accommodation caused exacerbation of her pre-
existing diabetes, weight loss, hair loss, depression, and anxiety); Dolores J. v. Dep’t
of Veterans Affs., EEOC Appeal No. 2022000405 (Mar. 29, 2023) (complainant
awarded $50,000.00 based on own testimony of sleeplessness, suicidal ideations,
and loss of relationships as a result of discrimination); Carol P. v. Small Business
Admin., EEOC Appeal No. 2021004687 (March 9, 2022) (affirming an AJ’s award
of $50,000.00 where the Agency’s failure to accommodate the complainant’s
disability caused the complainant to suffer mood swings, loss of joy, migraines,
emotional distress, exacerbation of high blood pressure, sleeplessness, marital
discord, and loss of self-esteem); Greg M. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC Appeal
No. 0120160345 (Jan. 31, 2018) (increasing an AJ’s award to $50,000.00 where the
complainant suffered sleep disruptions, weight gain, stomach pain, panic attacks and
withdrawal from his family but some of the harm was due to an unrelated accident).

Attorney’s Fees

The Commission’s regulations require federal agencies to award attorney’s fees and
costs for the successful processing of an EEO complaint in accordance with existing
case law and regulatory standards. 29 C.F.R. § 1614.501(e)(1)(ii). Attorney’s fees
are calculated by determining the lodestar, which consists of the number of hours
reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate. All hours reasonably
spent to process the complaint are compensable; however, the number of hours
should not include excessive, redundant, or otherwise expenditures of time.

The reasonable hourly rate for an attorney is determined by the prevailing market
rate in the relevant legal community for similar services by lawyers of reasonably
comparable skill, experience and reputation. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 895
(1984).
                                          10                             2025002567

Recent fees awarded by the courts or through settlement to attorneys of comparable
reputation and experience performing similar work are also useful guides in setting
an appropriate rate. Nat’l Ass’n of Concerned Veterans v. Sec’y of Def., 675 F.2d
319 (D.C. Cir. 1982).

In this case, the Agency determined that an hourly rate of $400 was appropriate
based on recent Commission decisions which recognized that an hourly rate of $400
was the prevailing market rate for attorneys in Birmingham, Alabama. On appeal,
Complainant argues that the Agency erred because it ignored that the Agency had
previously found that an hourly rate of $542 was appropriate and also because the
rate of $400 did not take into account the increase in cost of living. In this case, we
agree with Complainant that the Agency erred because it did not take into account
the increase in cost of living since the decisions on which the Agency relied. The
Commission has held that the attorneys are entitled to the reasonable hourly rate at
the time of the award. See Opfer v. Dep’t of Commerce, EEOC Appeal No.
01A44558 (Dec. 29, 2005), citing Mareno v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC Appeal
No. 01943104 (Feb. 14, 1996) (the Commission reaffirmed its position that the
proper customary hourly rate is the reasonable hourly rate in effect at the time of the
award and not at the time the services are provided). The cases on which the Agency
relied in establishing the hourly rate for attorneys in Birmingham, Alabama all date
from at least five years ago. See Colene M. v. Dep’t of Veterans Affs., EEOC
Appeal No. 2019005810 (July 19, 2021) (affirming an Agency’s award in 2019 of
attorney’s fees at an hourly rate of $400 for an attorney in Birmingham, AL); Janel
B. v. Dep’t of Justice, EEOC Appeal No. 0120132683 (Nov. 4, 2015) (finding that
an hourly rate of $400 was the prevailing market rate in 2013 for an attorney with
more than 20 years of experience in civil rights). On appeal, Complainant points out
that an hourly rate of $400 in 2018 would be $516.96 in the present day when
adjusted for inflation using the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Consumer Price
Index (CPI). We therefore find that an hourly rate of $515 reflects a reasonable
estimation of the current prevailing market rate for experienced attorneys in
Birmingham, Alabama.
