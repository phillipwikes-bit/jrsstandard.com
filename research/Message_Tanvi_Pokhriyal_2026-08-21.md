**Subject: The paper is finished, and two things I need from you**

```
Hi Tanvi,

The employment paper is drafted in full and your case set is its primary result. Two
things, and the second one is genuinely your decision rather than mine.

First, please stop collecting. You offered to sit for a few more cases and I am grateful,
but you do not need to. The pilot closed at 22 on 29 July and 22 is enough. Adding cases
now, after the analysis is written, would weaken the paper rather than strengthen it,
because a reviewer cannot distinguish a corpus that grew from a corpus that grew once
somebody knew what the numbers looked like. Nothing is outstanding from you on the case set.

What your 22 matters show. Records you read as incomplete, before you knew how anything
resolved, drew an adverse finding in 7 of 9 cases against 2 of 13 for the records you
passed. That is a five-fold difference and it holds at p = 0.0073. The paper also reports
two other ways of defining an adverse outcome, and under one of them the result is not
significant, so all three are reported side by side rather than only the strongest. I would
rather the paper say that plainly than have a reviewer find it.

Your set is also doing work beyond its own result. The companion public-records study found
no such association, and your corpus is what explains why: published freedom-of-information
cases reach publication because a legal question was live, so their outcomes are not a fair
test. Yours are not filtered that way. A formal test shows the two studies do not actually
disagree with each other. Neither could have shown that alone.

Second, the limitation, which I want you to see before a reviewer does.

The paper states plainly that you recorded both the reads and the outcomes for your cases,
and that this means the reads were not independent of the person assigning the outcome.
That is a real weakness in the design and it is mine, not yours: I set the protocol up that
way and did not build in a second pair of hands. It appears in the methods section, again
in the limitations, and the fix is written into the design for the next study, which puts
reads and outcomes with different people.

I am telling you directly because it sits next to your name on a paper you are a co-author
of, and you should not meet it first in a reviewer's comments.

What I need from you when you have a moment:

1. Whether Sections 5.1 and 5.2 describe the pilot the way you actually ran it, including
   how you coded outcomes. The draft is attached.
2. Whether you hold any financial or commercial interest in the Justification Review
   Standard, and whether you received any funding for this work. The answer is almost
   certainly no to both, but the journal requires each author to state it in their own
   words and I am not willing to write it on your behalf.

Your written observations on what you noticed across these cases, the documentation
features that tended to come before a challenge or an adverse finding, are still very
welcome and would sit in the discussion in your own voice. They are not required for
submission and there is no deadline on them.

Thank you for the care you put into this. Twenty-two adjudicated matters read one at a time
from the record alone is slow, unglamorous work, and it is the part of this programme that
touches real cases.

Best,
Phillip
```
